Audit the shop before the purchase, because the purchase is the point.
Charts and their treaty; arrows and the read-outs that measure them;
machines of every slot-count; a rule for carrying, chosen in the open; and
a machine that reads the failure of loops to close. Every station of that
inventory was built without measuring a single length --- the reader was
asked, chapter by chapter, to notice the absence --- and the construction
has now genuinely run out of things it can build without one. So this
chapter, at last, goes shopping, and the book keeps its standing promise:
the price of everything is paid in the open.

What is a ruler, in the language this book now owns? A machine. At each
point, install a machine with two arrow-slots --- symmetric, so the slots
do not care about their order, and positive, so that no arrow but the zero
arrow reads zero against itself --- and let the installation vary smoothly
from point to point in every chart. That field of two-slot machines is the
metric tensor. Feed it the same arrow twice and the answer is declared to
be the arrow's squared length; feed it two different arrows and the answer
carries what the trade distills into the angle between them. The proposal
is Riemann's, from the 1854 lecture this book keeps returning to: geometry
enters not as a global grid but as a length-rule given infinitesimally ---
his line element --- point by point, chart by chart, with which geometry a
given space actually wears left, in his own insistence, to be determined
by experience. Everything in this chapter descends from that one
installation, and the standing references for the descent are the
subject's standard texts --- do Carmo's \emph{Riemannian Geometry}, Lee's
\emph{Riemannian Manifolds} --- which this book cites once and then
declines to re-derive.

Now watch what the purchase settles, because three debts fall due at once,
and each was flagged, chapters ago, as a debt.

First, lengths themselves. A curve gains a length: integrate, along it,
the length the metric assigns its velocity --- the speed --- and distances
follow as the infimum of curve-lengths between two points. The paths that
locally spend that length most frugally are the geodesics, the subject's
straight lines, and with them the reader's oldest geometric vocabulary ---
how long, how far, how straight --- is finally licensed on curved ground.

Second, the marriage promised in Chapter 3. An arrow and a read-out were
distinct species, their conversion flagged as a purchase not yet payable.
Here is the payment. Take the metric's two-slot machine and feed an arrow
into one slot only, leaving the other open: what remains is a machine with
one open arrow-slot --- which is to say, a read-out. The metric pairs each
arrow with its own covector, one-to-one, and the pairing is geometry, not
notation: it depends on the installed ruler and changes if the ruler
changes. The physicist's daily act of lowering an index is exactly this
feeding-of-one-slot, the raising the inverse machine's, and the license
the reader has used since their second year is hereby dated and priced:
legitimate wherever a metric stands installed, bought at the cost of
installing it, and not one chapter earlier.

Third, the selection promised in Chapter 5. Among all the carrying rules a
manifold admits --- and Chapter 5 was honest that nothing so far preferred
one --- the ruler singles out exactly one: the unique rule that is
torsion-free and that preserves the ruler itself under carrying, so that
lengths and angles ride unchanged along any transport. That is the
fundamental theorem of the subject, its proof in the standard texts just
cited, and the selected rule bears Levi-Civita's name --- the same
Levi-Civita whose 1917 picture made carrying walkable --- closing a loop
of names this book finds pleasing but spends nothing on. From here the
machinery composes: the metric selects its connection, the connection
feeds Chapter 6's loop-machine, and the curvature that emerges is the
curvature \emph{of the ruler's own geometry} --- Riemannian geometry
proper, the discipline the 1854 lecture called for.

And a fourth settlement, smaller but owed to the very first chapter: the
ruler refines the neighborhoods. Around each point the metric draws the
balls of every radius --- all points within distance epsilon --- and these
balls are neighborhoods in exactly Chapter 1's sense, a basis for the
space's regions of validity. The reader's epsilon-and-ball calculus is
thereby recovered whole. But the order of construction must be kept
honest, because it is the moral of the entire book: the topology was
declared first, and the metric's balls \emph{describe} the same
neighborhoods more finely --- they do not create the space, they
instrument it. The limits of Chapter 1 needed no norm; the norm, arriving
six chapters later, agrees with them.

One paragraph belongs to a sibling ruler, because the next chapter cannot
be read without it. Relax the demand that the two-slot machine be
positive, and allow a signature in which one direction reads opposite in
sign to the rest. The machine then grades directions into kinds ---
those with positive reading, those with negative, and the boundary family
reading exactly zero --- and geometry acquires a causal grammar: the
timelike, the spacelike, and the null. That modification, put before
physics in Minkowski's lecture of 1908, is the geometry in which
relativity writes spacetime, and every construction of this book ---
charts, machines, the selected connection, the loop-machine --- carries
over to it unchanged in form. This book needs the fact and not its
development; one paragraph, as promised, and no more.

Stand back once, before the final chapters, and say what the ruler did
and did not do --- because the shape of this chapter is the thesis of the
volume. The ruler created nothing. Charts, arrows, read-outs, machines,
carrying, curvature: all of it stood before a single length was
measurable, and the reader watched it stand. What the ruler did was
select and measure: it married each arrow to a read-out, elected one
carrying rule from the candidates, drew the balls, and put numbers to
lengths, angles, and straightness. The ruler is not the ground; it is the
final instrument installed on ground that stood without it. A reader who
carries only that one sentence out of this book carries the construction.

The language is now complete. Neighborhoods for validity, charts for
observers, arrows and read-outs for states and instruments, machines for
the laws, a carrying rule for comparison, a loop-machine for curvature,
and a ruler for measure --- every piece cited, every price public. What
remains is to read: the two great descriptions of the century, each
written in this language and each favoring a different one of Chapter 3's
two species, and
the boundary between them where the founding text of this series located
its deepest promise. That reading is the next chapter, and it ends at a
door.
