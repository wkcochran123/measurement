Before the argument begins, a word about a freedom this book will not spend on
the reader's behalf.

In 1904 Ernst Zermelo wrote down an axiom to prove that every set can be
well-ordered. It says a thing that sounds harmless until you hold it to the
light: given any family of nonempty sets, a function exists that picks one
element from each. Exists --- with no rule for the picking. Not "here is how to
choose," but "a chooser is out there; take it on faith." Mathematics had leaned
on the move quietly for years; Zermelo made it explicit, and the moment it was
explicit it was a scandal, because it asserts a selection no one can exhibit.

Twenty years later Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski showed what the scandal buys.
A solid ball can be cut into five pieces and reassembled, by rigid motions alone
--- no stretching, nothing added --- into two balls, each the size of the first.
Nothing is created, because the pieces have no volume to conserve: they are
non-measurable, the offspring of a selection made without a rule. The one ball
becomes two, and no law of volume is broken, because the pieces never lived
under that law. That is the cost, and the grant, of choosing without choosing
constructively.

Then the deepest turn. In 1938 Kurt G\"odel showed the axiom of choice cannot be
disproved from the other axioms of set theory; in 1963 Paul Cohen, by the method
of forcing, showed it cannot be proved from them either. Choice is neither true
nor false. It is independent --- a door mathematics leaves open on purpose, and
consistent on either side. Whether you admit the unexhibited chooser is not a
fact you discover. It is a decision you make.

This book's instrument makes the opposite decision, and makes it as content. Its
ground, in the second chapter, holds no oracle of choice: nothing anywhere in
the argument is "some element, it does not matter which." Every selection it
performs is the cheapest one that works, and it is written down by name. The
device cannot split Tarski's ball, because it owns no chooser to do the cutting.
It refuses the axiom --- not as a hardship, but because a number selected without
a rule is a number without a receipt, and this whole book is receipts.

So the ball is left where the device found it: one ball, honestly counted.
Whether it becomes two is not a question the instrument can answer, and it does
not pretend to. That decision is yours --- a \emph{tange}, a selection made by
your own lights and on your own authority: do I admit a chooser I cannot
construct? The device will not tange it for you. What it can do, it does: it
\emph{funges} --- it bags what is actually, constructibly there and hands you the
count, one ball, every time, on your behalf. You keep the free will; the
instrument keeps the honesty; neither spends the other's coin.

You will meet this exact shape again near the end, wearing a joke. The apparatus
carries a dial --- a bias dial, a knob a certified technician may crank, and this
one goes to eleven. Turn it as far as you like. The instrument's count of its own
effort will flicker as you turn; the number the book is about will not move, not
by a digit, however hard you crank. The knob is Tarski's ball rebuilt in brass:
a thing you are free to turn, that changes the label and never the count, that
the device hands you and refuses to turn for you. Cranking it to eleven is
choosing to make the one ball two. The device funges the honest reading
regardless; the choice, and the crank, are yours.

One more notice belongs beside that freedom, and the reader should hold it from
the first page rather than discover it at the last: this book models the
electron exactly in as much as the electron is the name of a mathematical
model. Nowhere in what follows does the argument touch the particle in the
world. It touches descriptions --- admissible descriptions of the electron,
their grammar, their costs, and the number their discipline pins between walls
--- and where a late chapter may seem to close the gap between the description
and the thing, read again: that gap is the one thing this book never spends.
This is not a retreat from physics. It is the price of every receipt in the
book, and by the end the reader will have watched the price buy more than any
pretense could.

This is a preface about what the argument declines to assume, and it makes no
claim of its own about sets, or volume, or the real line; those belong to the
mathematicians named here as instruments and never as authorities. The argument
that follows concerns the electron and nothing else --- the electron in the
sense just given. But it opens by handing you your freedom, because a book that
will refuse to choose on your behalf owes you fair notice, on its first page,
that the choosing is yours. Read on with it in hand.
