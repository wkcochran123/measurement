\chapter{Measurement, Decoherence, and Nonlocality}
\label{chap:measurement-decoherence-nonlocality}

The foundations of quantum measurement reduce to a single sentence, and it is the chapter's spine: a record
is committed once, and that commitment is irreversible and order-sensitive. This is the chapter where the
count, now an amplitude, meets the act of measurement, and it is naturally the richest in machinery, because
measurement is where the amplitude's strangeness becomes visible. Six effects turn the spine over, and each
brings its own piece of the apparatus: the density matrix and its decohering coherences, the bounded shadow a
set of observables returns, the singlet that fixes a partner without a signal, the tensor product and reduced
state of an entangled pair, the CHSH bound the quantum breaks, and the seam two records must share to be
jointly read.

\section{Qubit Decoherence: collapse as a discarded branch}
\label{se:decoherence}

A qubit is the simplest amplitude the instrument carries: a superposition of two states,
\begin{equation}
  \lvert\psi\rangle = \alpha\,\lvert 0\rangle + \beta\,\lvert 1\rangle, \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1,
\end{equation}
two equally admissible branches held at once. To track what measurement does to it, the instrument writes the
state not as a vector but as a density matrix, the outer product
\begin{equation}
  \rho = \lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert =
  \begin{pmatrix} |\alpha|^2 & \alpha\beta^* \\ \alpha^*\beta & |\beta|^2 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
a two-by-two table whose diagonal entries $|\alpha|^2$ and $|\beta|^2$ are the probabilities of the two
branches and whose off-diagonal entries $\alpha\beta^*$ and $\alpha^*\beta$ are the coherences --- the record
that the branches are not merely a probabilistic mixture but a genuine superposition, in phase with each
other. The coherences \emph{are} the superposition; strip them and $\rho$ describes a coin that is either $0$
or $1$ with some probability, not a state that is both at once.

There is a picture for this. A single qubit's density matrix can always be written from three real numbers,
\begin{equation}
  \rho = \tfrac12\bigl(I + \mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigr), \qquad
  \mathbf{r} = \bigl(\langle X\rangle, \langle Y\rangle, \langle Z\rangle\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\sigma} = (X, Y, Z)$ are the three Pauli observables and $\mathbf{r}$ is the Bloch vector.
The vector lives in a ball: pure states, $\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert = 1$, sit on its surface --- the Bloch
sphere --- and mixed states, $\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert < 1$, sit inside, with the completely uncertain $\tfrac12
I$ at the very center. Decoherence is the Bloch vector contracting. The off-diagonal coherences are the
components of $\mathbf{r}$ transverse to the measurement axis, and as they decay the vector collapses onto the
axis, the point sliding from the surface toward the center. The qubit losing its coherence is, in the
picture, a point falling inward off the sphere.

Decoherence is the decay of those coherences. When the qubit couples to an environment it cannot track --- a
stray photon, a jostling atom, the apparatus itself --- the off-diagonal entries shrink toward zero,
\begin{equation}
  \rho \;\longrightarrow\;
  \begin{pmatrix} |\alpha|^2 & 0 \\ 0 & |\beta|^2 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
and the superposition becomes a mixture: the state is now genuinely either $0$ or $1$, the branches no longer
in phase, the coherence gone. To the instrument this is the discarding of a branch it can no longer
reconcile. The coupling commits an event the two in-phase branches cannot both survive; a boundary pivot
eliminates the incompatible ordering, and what is left is a single branch, the coherence that recorded their
togetherness erased.

The honest floor is a no-go --- keeping the unreconcilable superposition, once the environment has committed
its event, is inadmissible. The observed rate at which the coherences decay is itself a smooth shadow of the
discrete eliminations beneath it, but the collapse is a wall. The reading is that collapse is not a process
that reaches in and selects; it is the discarding of a branch the record can no longer hold, the off-diagonals
of $\rho$ falling to zero because the superposition they encoded can no longer be reconciled.

\section{Shadow Tomography: the bounded shadow a measurement returns}
\label{se:shadow-tomography}

A measurement does not hand the instrument the state $\rho$; it hands back the expectation of an observable.
For an observable $O$, what the instrument reads is the trace
\begin{equation}
  \langle O\rangle = \mathrm{Tr}(\rho\, O),
\end{equation}
a single number averaging $O$ over the state. A bounded family of observables $\{O_1, \ldots, O_m\}$ returns a
bounded family of numbers $\langle O_1\rangle, \ldots, \langle O_m\rangle$ --- the shadow of $\rho$ on the
tests performed, its projection onto the directions the instrument actually probed. And the shadow is all that
is accessible. Two states $\rho$ and $\rho'$ that agree on every test in the family, $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho O_i) =
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho' O_i)$ for all $i$, are operationally one state: no measurement in the family distinguishes
them, however many times it is run, and even an exponentially long sequence of tests from a fixed family
cannot expose a distinction that lies outside the directions it spans.

The honest floor is a finite count obligation: the shadow is a finite list of expectation values, and two
states with the same list are identical to the apparatus. The reading is that a measurement reveals a shadow,
not a state. What is accessible is the bounded projection $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho O_i)$ onto the tests performed; the
state beneath the shadow, in the directions no test touched, is not measured and cannot be.

\section{Spooky Action at a Distance: the partner fixed without a signal}
\label{se:epr}

Prepare two qubits in the singlet,
\begin{equation}
  \lvert\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(\lvert 01\rangle - \lvert 10\rangle\bigr),
\end{equation}
and send one to a laboratory $A$ and the other, spacelike separated, to $B$. The singlet is perfectly
anti-correlated: measure $A$ and find $0$, and $B$ is fixed at $1$; find $1$ at $A$, and $B$ is fixed at $0$
--- at once, with no signal sent. The two measurements are spacelike, so their operators commute,
\begin{equation}
  E_i E_j = E_j E_i,
\end{equation}
and the order in which they are performed cannot matter to the joint record. What looks like influence is the
shared preparation showing through: the anti-correlation was written into the singlet at the source, and
committing either end simply reads out what the joint record already fixed.

That no signal can be sent is not a hope but a theorem of the formalism. Whatever $A$ chooses to measure, the
state $B$ holds by itself --- its reduced density matrix, got by tracing out $A$,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_B = \mathrm{Tr}_A\,\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert = \tfrac12 I,
\end{equation}
is the completely mixed state, the same maximally uncertain qubit no matter what $A$ does. $B$ sees $\tfrac12
I$ before $A$ measures and $\tfrac12 I$ after; nothing in $B$'s accessible statistics changes, so no message
crosses. The honest floor is a finite count obligation: the joint order is fixed by a count at the shared
preparation, and the reduced state $B$ can read is unchanged by $A$'s choice. The reading is that the
``spooky'' action sends nothing. The partner is fixed because the singlet has one consistent anti-correlated
order, committing either end reads it out, and $B$'s own statistics --- $\tfrac12 I$ throughout --- carry no
trace of $A$'s measurement. Correlation is shared at preparation, not influence carried at measurement.

\section{The Entanglement Effect: a boundary pivot resolves the order}
\label{se:entanglement}

The state of two systems lives in the tensor product of their spaces,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B,
\end{equation}
and most states in that product are not products. A separable state factors, $\lvert\psi\rangle = \lvert
a\rangle \otimes \lvert b\rangle$ --- $A$ in a definite state of its own, $B$ in a definite state of its own
--- but the singlet does not: there are no $\lvert a\rangle, \lvert b\rangle$ with $\lvert\psi\rangle = \lvert
a\rangle \otimes \lvert b\rangle$, and that failure to factor is exactly what entanglement is. The Schmidt
decomposition makes the distinction precise: any bipartite pure state can be written
\begin{equation}
  \lvert\psi\rangle = \sum_i \lambda_i\, \lvert i\rangle_A \otimes \lvert i\rangle_B,
\end{equation}
with non-negative Schmidt coefficients $\lambda_i$; the state is separable when only one $\lambda$ is nonzero
and entangled when more than one is, and the spread of the $\lambda_i$ measures how entangled it is. The
singlet has two equal coefficients, $\lambda = 1/\sqrt2$ each --- maximal entanglement.

Trace out one side and the entanglement shows as ignorance about the other. The reduced state of $A$,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_A = \mathrm{Tr}_B\,\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert,
\end{equation}
is, for an entangled pure state, a mixed state and not a pure one: $A$ alone is uncertain precisely because
its definiteness has been spent on the correlation with $B$.

The amount of that entanglement has a number, and it is an entropy. The von Neumann entropy of the reduced
state,
\begin{equation}
  S(\rho_A) = -\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\rho_A \log \rho_A\bigr) = -\sum_i \lambda_i^2 \log \lambda_i^2,
\end{equation}
measures how mixed $A$ becomes when $B$ is traced away: zero for a separable state, where $A$ stays pure, and
largest when the Schmidt coefficients are all equal. For the singlet the two coefficients are $1/\sqrt2$ each,
so the entropy is $\log 2$, exactly one bit --- $A$ alone holds one full bit of uncertainty, the price of the
correlation it shares with $B$. This is the same $-\sum p \log p$ that counted distinctions in the heat part,
here counting the definiteness one half has spent on the whole.

This is the degeneracy entanglement names --- the
order of the two wings' events in the joint history is undetermined, many orders equally consistent --- and a
boundary resolves it. A pivot on the shared boundary, not a physical process but bookkeeping, installs the
unique order compatible with the frontier, eliminating the incompatible orderings without ever scanning the
interior of either record.

The honest floor is a finite count obligation: the pivot is a count at the seam that selects the one
frontier-compatible order. The reading is that entanglement is a degeneracy of order resolved at a boundary.
The interior need not be scanned; boundary consistency alone fixes the order, and the entangled correlation is
that single admissible ordering, fixed at the edge rather than computed in the bulk --- the reduced state's
ignorance the price the edge pays for the whole's definiteness.

\section{The Bell/Aspect Tests: no local hidden variable}
\label{se:bell}

The deepest of the six is the Bell test, and it deserves its full machinery. Two spacelike laboratories share
an entangled pair. Alice can measure one of two observables, $A_1$ or $A_2$; Bob one of two, $B_1$ or $B_2$;
each measurement returns $\pm 1$. From the four correlations $\langle A_i B_j\rangle$ --- the averages of the
products of the two wings' outcomes --- form the CHSH combination,
\begin{equation}
  S = \langle A_1 B_1\rangle + \langle A_1 B_2\rangle + \langle A_2 B_1\rangle - \langle A_2 B_2\rangle .
\end{equation}
Now suppose, as any local theory must, that each outcome is fixed in advance by a hidden variable the pair
carries from the source, each wing's result a function of that variable and its own setting alone. Then for
every value of the hidden variable the four $\pm 1$ outcomes are predetermined, and a glance at the signs
settles it: the combination $A_1(B_1 + B_2) + A_2(B_1 - B_2)$ is always $\pm 2$, because one of the two
brackets vanishes and the other is $\pm 2$. Averaging over the hidden variable can only shrink that, so
\begin{equation}
  |S| \le 2,
\end{equation}
the Bell inequality --- the bound any local hidden-variable theory must obey. Quantum mechanics does not obey
it. With the singlet and the measurement directions set at the right angles, the quantum prediction reaches
\begin{equation}
  |S| = 2\sqrt{2},
\end{equation}
Tsirelson's bound, decisively past the local limit; and the Aspect experiments, and many since, measure values
at $2\sqrt2$ and not $2$. The local account is simply wrong.

Where does $2\sqrt2$ come from? The singlet's correlation between a measurement along a direction $a$ in one
wing and a direction $b$ in the other is the cosine of the angle between the settings,
\begin{equation}
  E(a, b) = -\cos\theta_{ab}.
\end{equation}
Choose the four directions so that each pair entering the CHSH combination is $45^\circ$ apart: three of the
correlations then contribute $+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and the one that is subtracted contributes $+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$
after its sign, and the four add to $4\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} = 2\sqrt2$. The violation is not a small overshoot
wrung from a lucky arrangement but the cosine correlation evaluated at its best angles, and Tsirelson's bound
says quantum mechanics can do no better.

The CHSH inequality is the physics, quoted. What the instrument proves is the no-go beneath it: there is no
fold of two independent local maps, one acting on each wing, that reproduces the joint correlations. No
assignment of separate local responses to the two sides --- no predetermined table the pair could carry ---
can match the joint update, because the update is irreducibly joint. The honest floor is a no-go, and a proved
one. The reading is that the correlations are real and the locality is not. The bound $|S| \le 2$ is what any
theory of separate local causes must obey; the quantum $2\sqrt2$ breaks it; and the instrument shows why --- the
joint update does not factor into local maps, and nonlocality here is exactly that impossibility of
factorization, not a signal faster than light.

\section{The Hall--EPR Effect: joint inference needs an agreeing seam}
\label{se:hall-epr}

Joint inference across two records is admissible only where their shared seam agrees. The lesson is the one
the Monty Hall game teaches: a conditional inference depends on the constraint actually recorded, and altering
or removing that constraint changes the inference --- the contestant's odds shift when the host's rule is
specified, because the conditioning is on what was really recorded, not on what might have been. Across a
spacelike pair, two detectors record locally consistent outcomes, and to condition one on the other, to infer
across the pair, the seam values where they meet must match. A mismatch does not merely weaken the inference;
it blocks it, because the correlation cannot be decomposed into independent local components while preserving
what was observed.

The honest floor is a no-go: you cannot condition across a mismatch. The reading is that joint inference over
two records is admissible only where their seams agree. Where the seam values disagree the conditioning is not
uncertain but inadmissible --- a wall, not a probability. Correlation can be read across the pair only through
an agreeing seam, and the unrecorded structure a careless conditioning would smuggle in is exactly what the
seam-agreement forbids.

\bigskip

The foundations of quantum measurement are one claim made six ways, now with its full machinery: a record is
committed once, irreversibly and in order. Collapse is the off-diagonals of $\rho$ falling to zero, a branch
discarded; a measurement is the bounded shadow $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho O)$ on the tests performed; a spacelike
partner is fixed by a shared preparation while the reduced state $\tfrac12 I$ sends nothing; entanglement is a
non-separable state of $\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$ whose order a boundary pivots into place; the Bell
bound $|S| \le 2$ is broken by the quantum $2\sqrt2$ and no fold of local maps can mend it; and joint
inference needs an agreeing seam. Part IV turns next to matter waves and scattering, where the amplitude meets
the lattice, a diffraction pattern is read off the counts, and the instrument proves its second loop residue.
