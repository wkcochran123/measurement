# Chapter 11 — Solving it: splines, Lanczos, partitions

## Reconstruct from samples

The trial has located the drive in principle and now has to produce it in fact. The last two chapters did the locating: the true drive is the stationary path, the one at the flat floor where the first variation vanishes, and a reading matches it exactly when the residual it carries collapses to nothing. That is where the drive lives. But the court cannot exhibit a path it has only characterized. The needle, the radar, and the GPS did not hand over a curve; they handed over a scatter of instants. The needle settled and was read, settled and was read. The radar threw a pulse down the road and timed its return — a *ping*, and then seconds later another ping, each one a single instantaneous closing-speed and nothing between. The GPS logged a *fix* — a position pinned against the satellites at one tick of its clock — and then, a beat later, another fix. What sits before the court is not the drive. It is a finite handful of marks the drive passed through: a few radar pings, a few GPS fixes, the needle's settlings, each an honest sample at one instant and silent about every instant between.

So the reconstruction is the ordinary, unglamorous thing every trip computer does without comment: thread a curve through the marks. Take the finite set of samples — this many pings, this many fixes — and supply a rule for filling the gaps between them, a single clean path that passes near every sample and bends only as much as it must. The marks pin the curve down where they fall; the rule says what the drive was doing in the dark stretches the instruments never lit. Reconstruct from samples: a finite set of instants, and a recipe for the continuum between them. The prosecution will then point at that reconstructed curve and say *this is the drive, and it is the stationary one, and its residual is gone*.

This chapter builds the recipe, and it is the largest piece of construction in the book because the recipe has parts. There is the curve itself — a cubic spline through the samples, smooth across every join. There is the solver that produces it — an iteration that consumes the samples one at a time, in some order, and is built so that it always halts. There is the working space the curve is fitted in — a real partition of the road into cells, a finite frame of element pieces, the truncation that keeps the whole thing computable. There is the geometry of the interior, where two control points in the middle of a cubic arc move at once and couple, producing a cross term the construction must name and carry forward. And there is the policy that picks the order of consumption — a cost measured on each sample, the fastest taken first. Five movements, one curve. The samples are on the table. The work of the chapter is to draw the drive back out of them.

## 11.1 — Cubic-spline sufficiency

Start with the curve the construction is allowed to draw, and the claim it wants to make about it. A *cubic spline* through the finite samples is the smoothed path of Chapter 7 returned at full weight: a curve pinned at the sample marks, built piecewise out of cubic arcs, joined so that it does not kink where two arcs meet. It is the natural object to thread through pings and fixes, flexible enough to bend through scattered instants and simple enough to compute with exactly. The construction does not need to re-derive it; Chapter 7 built the spline as a first and a second variation — the knot the response to moving a control point, the interpolant the curvature of the join — and this chapter inherits that object whole.

What this chapter adds is the question of *sufficiency*. It is one thing to draw a spline through the samples; it is another to claim the spline is *enough* — that it represents the drive with nothing left over, that the residual between the curve and the path it is standing in for has collapsed to zero. Make the claim precise by reusing the residual the previous chapters built. The spline's residual at a given variation is exactly the Euler-Lagrange residual of the path it draws: the seam's two contributions summed, the quantity Chapter 10 demanded vanish in every direction. So the spline carries the same residual the gauge does — they are not two residuals that happen to agree, they are one residual read off one object. Then there are two grades of the sufficiency claim, the weaker and the stronger, exactly the pair Chapter 9 was careful to separate. The spline is *balanced* at a path when the path admits its midpoint — the whole agreeing with the sum of its parts — and the residual vanishes in every direction. It is *closed and flat* at a path when, more strongly, the action is flat there in the closed sense and the residual vanishes. Closed-flat hands its strength straight through to balanced, as before: flatness gives admissibility and turns into the vanishing along the way. And from balanced the construction reads its headline — *the spline is sufficient* — meaning the residue the path carries collapses to truth-zero, the value that signals nothing-left-over. The spline suffices to represent the drive.

Now read the grades carefully, because the line this chapter must keep runs straight through them. That the residual *is* the Euler-Lagrange residual — that the spline's leftover and the gauge's leftover are one finite object — is built, demonstrated, register (i): an identity of two constructions, no assumption spent. The solver of the next section makes this concrete with a certificate that lays three residuals side by side and shows them equal — the spline's, the iteration's, and the gauge's, the same number for every direction, identified outright. That identification is earned. What is *not* earned the same way is the collapse: the step from *the residual vanishes* to *therefore the residue is truth-zero, the drive is matched, the sufficiency holds*. That step is the detection of Chapter 10, and it leans where Chapter 10 leaned — on the principle that the stationarity condition admits a standing verdict, that of any path handed over the construction can decide the residual has gone quiet.

> *A note on what is built and what is granted, made once for the chapter.* The constructions of these five sections are built honestly and demonstrated — the spline, the solver that always halts, the partitions and the finite frame, the coupled interior with its cross term, the measured cost. Those are register (i), and the chapter will not flag each one. But the recurring *results* — that the spline suffices, that the residue collapses to truth-zero, that the weak residual vanishes, that the fastest order is enough — all rest on the single assumption the variational band already named: that the stationarity condition is decidable, that the verdict driving the collapse is always available to be read. It is the same principle Chapter 10 flagged and no other; it is set out where the foundations are accounted, in *The Axiom of Choice*. These results are true *given* that principle; the constructions do not prove it. A reader who keeps that one register apart for the whole chapter keeps its honesty, and need not be reminded again.

So §11.1 leaves the trial with a curve and a graded claim. The cubic spline is threaded through the pings and the fixes; its residual is the gauge's residual, identified outright; and *if* the construction may decide the residual gone, then the spline suffices and the drive is represented with nothing left over. The curve is in hand. What remains is to actually produce it — to run a solver that drives the residual down — and that solver is the next thing built.

## 11.2 — Lanczos as ordered knot-consumption

The spline does not appear by fiat; something computes it, and the something is an iteration that works through the samples one at a time. Call the samples, once they are pinned as control points, the *knots* — the marks where the curve is held. The solver of this section is a *Lanczos iteration*: a tridiagonal generator that, given a residual, sweeps through the knots and drives the residual toward zero, knot by knot, in an order it consumes them. Two objects have to be kept distinct here, and keeping them distinct is the whole architecture. There is the *field of knots* — the unordered collection of samples the solver is allowed to consume — and there is the *trace* — the ordered run the solver actually performs, one knot pulled from the field at each step. The bag and the execution are separate things.

Build the field first, and here the book's second verb does its work. The knots **funge** into a *multiset*: a bag into which every sample is dropped, order forgotten, the slips made interchangeable. To funge is exactly this — to pool things by the trait they share and thereafter refuse to tell them apart, surrendering each one's standing-in-line to the bag. The multiset of slips is that bag: it records *which* knots are present and *how many*, and nothing about *when* any of them is taken. The slip that was a tenth-of-a-second radar ping and the slip that was a GPS fix sit in the bag side by side, indistinguishable to it, two members of one unordered field. That pooling is deliberate, because the order is not the bag's business — the order belongs to the trace, and the trace is where the first verb does its complementary work.

The trace consumes the bag one knot at a time, and each step **tanges** the next knot out — individuates it from the field by the deciding trait the consumption uses, holds that one slip apart, removes it, and hands the smaller bag forward. To tange is to single a thing out by a characteristic; the iteration tanges out one knot, consumes it into the tridiagonal sweep, and leaves the rest. So a trace is a sequence of tanges run against a funged field: pull one knot, shrink the bag, step; pull the next, shrink again; until the bag is empty and the run is *done*.

Now the part that is genuinely proved, and it is the spine of the section: the iteration *terminates*. This is not assumed and not waved at — it is demonstrated, register (i), and the demonstration is the clean kind. Watch the bag. Each consuming step removes exactly one knot, so the bag the step hands forward is strictly smaller than the bag it received — one fewer member, every time, with no step that adds a knot back or leaves the count unchanged. A quantity that strictly decreases at every step, and cannot decrease below an empty bag, cannot decrease forever; the run reaches the empty bag in finitely many steps and stops. That is a well-founded recursion: the strictly shrinking bag is the thing that founds it, the measure that forbids an endless trace. The iteration halts because it cannot help halting — there is no infinite descending chain of bags to ride down. The construction proves this outright, for the field as it stands, with nothing borrowed.

Be exact, though, about what termination does and does not buy, because here a careless reading would overclaim. When the trace runs to its end it reaches a *terminal residual*, and the construction states that this terminal residual is the same — collapses to the same truth-zero — *for any order the knots are consumed in*. That phrasing invites a stronger reading than the construction earns, so hold the line. The genuinely proved thing is the *termination*: any order halts, because the shrinking-bag argument does not care which knot is pulled first. What the "any order" clause about the terminal residual restates is the *sufficiency result* of §11.1 — that the residue is truth-zero — re-expressed at the end of a completed trace. It is the same residue-collapse claim, carrying the same assumed verdict, not a fresh theorem that the answer is independent of order proved from the iteration's mechanics. Order-independence of the *answer* is the sufficiency result wearing a Lanczos hat; order-independence of *halting* is the part actually demonstrated. The honest statement is: every iteration order terminates — proved — and every completed trace lands at the truth-zero residue — granted, under the chapter's one assumption.

There is one local result the trace earns cleanly that is worth marking, because the rest of the chapter uses it: on the knots the trace actually represents — the slips in its own field — the terminal residual is zero by construction of the completed trace, a support-local statement that needs no global verdict, only the finished run against its own bag. That is the foothold §11.3 stands on. The solver, then: a field funged into a bag, a trace that tanges each knot out in turn, a recursion well-founded by the strictly shrinking bag so that it always halts — and at its terminal, the residue the chapter has been chasing, gone quiet on the represented knots outright and truth-zero everywhere under the standing assumption.

## 11.3 — Partitions, the finite-element frame, and the finite truncation

The solver runs, but it has to run *somewhere* — in a working space laid over the road, and that space is the next thing the construction builds. A spline is fitted across a domain cut into pieces, and the cutting is a *real partition*. Take the stretch of road the drive covered and lay an ordered coordinate down it: a sequence of points marching forward along the axis, each no farther from the next than a fixed *mesh* — a bound on the cell width, so that no single cell is allowed to swallow more road than the construction can resolve. The partition is that ordered, mesh-bounded subdivision: cells in order, a near-edge and a far-edge to each, the whole road tiled by them with no gap and no cell too wide. This is the partition for real now, not the single interval Chapter 7 fitted inside — an actual subdivision of the domain, ordered and bounded, the trellis the spline will be woven through. The partition **tanges** each cell out by its distinguishing edge: this cell is the one whose far boundary falls *here*, individuated from its neighbors by where its mark lands on the axis, so that a sample can be located in exactly one cell and the curve assembled piece by piece.

Over the partition the construction raises a *finite-element frame* — a space of functions defined cell by cell, each function built from local pieces seated on the partition, with a *weak pairing* that lets two functions be tested against each other without ever differentiating. This is the Sobolev-style frame the whole tower was built to reach: a function space rich enough to hold the spline and its bending energy, equipped with the derivative-free pairing the Galerkin method of Chapter 7 needs, and honest about its provenance — the "real line" underneath is the partition's own ordered coordinate, not an analytic continuum smuggled in. The frame is where the weak residual lives, and the weak residual is the section's working quantity: the spline's leftover, tested against the frame's functions through the weak pairing, the thing the fit drives to zero.

But the frame cannot be the whole infinite function space, or nothing computes, and here the standing constraint of the book returns at full force. The construction works in a *finite truncation* — one finite frame, a single bounded working space carved out of the otherwise unbounded function space, large but only so large. The source has a jokey nickname for this single finite stack of working data; drop the nickname and say plainly what it is: the finite frame, the finite truncation, the one bounded space the fit must complete inside. Each admissible piece of data is assigned its own real partition, its own finite frame, and the spline is fitted there and nowhere larger. The truncation is what makes the analytic language honest — it is why the construction may speak of a Sobolev frame and a weak residual without taking a limit it has refused since the first page. The fit happens in finitely many cells, in a finitely generated frame, against finitely many test functions, and converges or fails there.

Now the bridge that makes the truncation matter, and it is a quiet identification doing heavy lifting. The finite data supplies its *support* — the multiset of candidate slips it actually carries, the knots that fall in its cells. The Lanczos trace of §11.2 supplies *its* knots — the field it consumed. The construction matches these two: the trace's bag of knots is identified with the data's bag of candidates, the same support read two ways. With that match in hand, the support-local foothold of §11.2 transfers straight across. The trace solved its terminal residual on its own knots — earned, no verdict needed — and since those knots *are* the data's candidates, the spline's weak residual vanishes on the represented data: the curve passes through the samples it was built from, leftover gone, on every knot the data actually carries. That much is clean. The stronger statement — that the weak residual vanishes *everywhere*, on every test the frame can mount and not only on the represented knots — is the residue-collapse claim again, carrying the chapter's one assumption, and it is true under that assumption and not otherwise. The construction is candid about the seam: continuum and global control of the variation are deferred — they belong to the convergence argument of the next chapter, where the partition is refined and the frame squeezed. Here the construction has built the partition, raised the finite frame, fixed the truncation, and shown the weak residual gone on the data the frame represents. The drive is fitted in a real, bounded, derivative-free working space. What it has not yet shown is that refining the frame drives the leftover to zero in the limit — and that is the climb of Chapter 12.

## 11.4 — The interior node and the coupled equations

So far the path has carried one interior point — the single slip of the variational chapters, one midpoint sliding between a fixed source and a fixed target. A cubic arc is richer: it carries *two* interior control points, a left-middle and a right-middle, between the held endpoints. This is the four-node picture the spline actually needs — source, two middle nodes, target — and it is where the chapter's most careful construction lives, because moving both middle nodes at once does something the single midpoint never could. Build it deliberately, because this is the seed the whole of Part II is climbing toward.

The four-node cost is the sum of three legs: source to left-middle, left-middle to right-middle, right-middle to target. The two boundary nodes are pinned; the two middle nodes are free, and the construction reads the cost's response to moving them. Move the *left* node alone, holding the right where it sits, and the response is exactly a one-coordinate residual — the left-middle residual, the Euler-Lagrange residual of the first sub-path, identified on the nose with the change in cubic cost when only the left node slides. Move the *right* node alone and the same thing happens on the other side: the right-middle residual, identified with the cost change when only the right node slides. Two middle equations, one per node, each the familiar seam-cancellation of Chapter 10 read at one interior point. The partitions of §11.3 derive both: the left node supported by its own finite frame, the right node by its own, each frame solving its node's residual on the data it represents. *The partitions derive the two middle equations* — register (i), built, the two one-coordinate residuals zero on supported data.

But now move *both* nodes at once, and the four-node cost does not simply add the two one-coordinate responses. There is a third bead. Build the coupled difference honestly: it is the full change in cubic cost when both middle nodes jump to new positions, the new three-leg total minus the old. Decompose it, and the decomposition is the heart of the section. The coupled difference equals the left-middle residual, *plus* the right-middle residual, *plus* a third term — the **mixed coupling** — and the mixed term is what the two one-coordinate moves miss. Look at where it comes from. Only the *middle* leg, left-middle to right-middle, depends on *both* moving nodes at once; the outer two legs each touch only one. So when both nodes move, the middle leg's cost is mis-counted by the two single-node accounts, and the mixed term is the exact correction. Write it as the cross second-difference it is: the cost with *both* nodes at their new marks, minus the cost with the left node new and the right node old, minus the cost with the left old and the right new, plus the cost with *both* old. New-new, minus new-old, minus old-new, plus old-old: a difference of differences, the cost of moving the right node *measured at the new left position* against the cost of moving it *at the old left position*. It is precisely how much the right node's effect *changes* because the left node also moved — the coupling between the two interior coordinates, the part of the response that belongs to neither node alone but to their interaction.

This mixed term is not bookkeeping debris; it is the object the rest of the book is built to name, and the construction keeps it visible on purpose. The one-coordinate theorems identify the left and right residuals exactly and send them to zero on supported data. But the coupled difference goes to zero *only* when the two middle residuals vanish *and the mixed coupling is separately solved* — set to zero, or bounded, by a condition the one-coordinate moves do not supply. The construction states this as the honest conjunction: left residual zero, right residual zero, mixed coupling solved, *therefore* coupled difference zero. Strike any one of the three and the coupled difference can stand nonzero. The mixed term is a genuine, irreducible piece of the four-node response — the cross derivative, the part of the second-order structure that the first-order seam-cancellation cannot reach. That is exactly its significance. The first variation pooled the flat directions and said nothing of curvature; the mixed coupling is where curvature first shows its face in the construction, the off-diagonal entry of the second-order response, the way one coordinate's effect bends as another moves. It is the seed of the *single invariant* — the second variation the whole of Part II is climbing toward — and Chapter 13 will gather the diagonal residuals and this off-diagonal cross term into one quantity the apparatus holds fixed. The construction names the mixed coupling here, builds its exact cross-difference form, carries it as a separate condition that must be solved rather than a residue that vanishes for free, and hands it forward. That carrying-forward is the point: the chapter does not let the mixed term collapse quietly into the one-coordinate accounts, because it is the term Chapter 13 needs whole.

## 11.5 — Selection by cost

One freedom is left in the solver, and the chapter closes by pinning it down. The iteration of §11.2 consumes the knots in *some* order, tanging one out of the bag at each step — but which one first? The construction does not leave the choice to chance or to an arbitrary rule. It selects by *cost*: each candidate knot is measured, and the iteration may consume only a *cheapest* available candidate from the current bag. The order is no longer free; it is determined, step by step, by a measurement.

The measurement is built concretely, and it is genuine — register (i), an honest cost reckoned on each knot. The construction calls the cost a *heartbeat*, and behind the name is a calibration the source runs as an electrocardiogram-style pulse: three finite operations are timed on each candidate, and the heartbeat is their total. The three are *load*, *transform*, and *binary* — load the candidate, transform it, combine it — three measured numbers, and the heartbeat selector is their sum. A candidate's cost is therefore not a guess but a profile: so many load-steps, so many transform-steps, so many binary-steps, added into one heartbeat count. The iteration consumes, at each step, a knot whose heartbeat is least in the current bag — the *fastest* candidate, the cheapest one to process. This is a real policy on a real measurement: the bag is still the unordered field, and the heartbeat supplies the ordering the trace runs by, semi-arbitrary in that any cost function would serve, but measurable and fixed once a cost is chosen.

What the measurement buys, and buys outright, is a *local uniqueness*. Suppose the cost *separates* the candidates on a given support — suppose no two distinct knots in the bag carry the same heartbeat. Then the cheapest candidate is unique: if two candidates are both heartbeat-minimal, each is at least as cheap as the other, so their costs are equal, so by separation they are the same knot. The fastest is one knot, not a tie. The construction proves this cleanly — register (i), no verdict spent — and proves it *locally*: uniqueness holds on a finite support where the heartbeat separates, not by global magic. The electrocardiogram calibration makes it concrete: load costs one beat, transform a second, the binary combination a third, so three sample knots come out at one, two, and three beats — separated, distinct, the cheapest unambiguous, the fastest the one-beat load. Uniqueness is earned on that support because the cost actually tells the three knots apart.

With the cost in hand the construction reads its last sufficiency line: among the iteration orders, the *fastest* order — consume the cheapest knot first, always — suffices. The fastest trace runs to its terminal and lands at the truth-zero residue, the same collapse the chapter has reached by every route. But read the register exactly, under the chapter's one note. The *measurement* is built and the *uniqueness* is proved — those are register (i), the heartbeat reckoned and the cheapest candidate shown unique on separating support. The step from *the fastest trace terminates* to *therefore its residue is truth-zero, the fastest order is sufficient* is the residue-collapse claim once more, carrying the same assumed verdict §11.1 named. The cost decides *which* order; the order's sufficiency — that it lands at nothing-left-over — is granted, not derived. Measuring decision-cost is real and done; the residue going quiet at the end of the cheapest run is the standing assumption doing its work.

And there the largest chapter closes, with the drive drawn back out of the samples. The pings and the fixes were threaded by a **cubic spline**, whose residual is the gauge's own. They were solved by a **Lanczos iteration** that funges the knots into a bag and tanges them out one at a time, well-founded by the strictly shrinking bag so that it always halts. They were fitted in a **real partition** of the road, a finite-element frame over a finite truncation, the weak residual gone on the represented data. The interior was opened to **two middle nodes** whose coupled response splits into a left residual, a right residual, and a **mixed coupling** the construction carried whole. And the order of consumption was fixed by a **measured cost** — three timed operations, the fastest knot first, the cheapest candidate unique on separating support. The constructions are built; the residue-collapse results are true under the one assumption the variational band named and no other. What remains is two climbs. The next chapter takes the finite truncation and *refines* it — squeezes the partition, grows the frame — and asks whether the leftover goes to zero in the limit, not merely on the represented knots: convergence, the global control this chapter deferred. And the chapter after gathers the diagonal residuals and the mixed cross term this chapter built into one quantity — the single invariant, the second variation, the curvature the whole apparatus was assembled to hold fixed. The spline is drawn; the mixed term is named; the climb from here is to convergence, and then to the one invariant the mixed term seeds.
