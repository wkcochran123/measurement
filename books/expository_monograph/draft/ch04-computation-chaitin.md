# Chapter 4 — Computation, representation, and the Chaitin barrier

## What the needle cannot resolve

The clock the last chapter built tells you how long a reading took, and that turns out to be the more uncomfortable of its two gifts. A reading takes time — an estimable, finite amount of it — and time, once it is in the tower, brings its own arithmetic of limits. There is a smallest amount of time the instrument can tell, just as there was a smallest division on the dial, and below that smallest amount the instrument does not lie. It falls silent. This chapter is about the silence: the floor beneath which the needle cannot decide, where that floor comes from, and what lives down at it.

Go back to the dashboard and look hard at the dial. The printed arc is divided into marks, and between two adjacent marks there is a gap the printer left blank because no finer division was worth engraving. The needle can come to rest anywhere in that gap, but the gap reports a single value — whatever the lower mark says, until the pointer has swung clear past the upper one. Ask the instrument which thousandth of the gap the pointer occupies and it has no answer to give. The cable that drives the needle has friction, and friction holds until a change is large enough to break it, so a difference too small to overcome the drag produces no swing at all. The pivot has play, a sliver of slack, so the pointer can sit anywhere in a band and report the same number. And the pointer, watched closely enough, never quite holds still: it jitters, a hair this way and a hair that, around wherever it means to rest. There is a width to the needle's verdict, and inside that width the reading is not a number but a smear.

This is not the instrument failing. It is the instrument telling the truth about itself. A speedometer that claimed to resolve the millionth of a mile per hour between two engraved marks would be lying, and the honest one declines to. The floor — the smallest division, the friction in the cable, the play in the pivot, the jitter in the pointer — is a fact about the instrument, as much a part of what it reports as the reading is. Every instrument has one. The question this chapter presses is not how to abolish the floor, which cannot be done, but what to make of it: how to say, precisely and without overreach, *this far the needle can decide, and no farther*. The residue of the stop is no longer the daylight between a reading and a verdict that Chapter 1 began with; it is whatever lies below the floor.

Four things live down at the floor, and the chapter takes them in order. First, the reading is the output of a computation, and a computation runs on symbols that have to come from somewhere. Second, there is a hard threshold no amount of computing can cross, against which the instrument is calibrated rather than over which it climbs. Third, there is a smallest amount the instrument can resolve at all — a machine-epsilon — and comparing two such slivers is a thing the construction deliberately does not finish, handing the last step to the reader. And fourth, out of the variance the floor permits, the chapter builds the first genuine integral in the book: an area, accumulated tick by tick, real and checkable.

## 4.1 — Computation, and where representation comes from

A reading is not handed down; it is produced. Behind the needle's settling is a process that takes something in, works on it, and puts something out, and to take the floor seriously we have to say what that process is. The construction says it plainly. A computation is one of two things. It is either a bare program — a hypothesis paired with the study that describes how it would be carried out, source code that has not yet run — or it is the program caught in the act of running: an input study, an output study, and a record of the prior computation that produced the one from the other. The first is a description; the second is the description plus its trace. That is the entire grammar of a computation here, and it is deliberately the same shape as a number — a base case and a step, a thing that has not yet run and a thing built from a thing that has.

The order on computations follows the parity engine of every prior chapter, and it is worth watching it choose its direction. Set two bare programs side by side and the one running the richer study is the larger — they are indexable, laid out in a sequence the way Turing laid his machines out, and comparing them passes straight through to the studies beneath, counting by wholes. This is the covariant case: a finished computation comes *after* the program that describes it, runs *with* it. Ask the reverse question — given a finished computation, recover the program that produced it — and the comparison turns over. Decomposition is contravariant. To run a program forward is to count up; to reverse a result back into its source is to count against the grain, the harder direction, and the order knows it. It is the single rule of the signed digit on the ticket: compare two signs, let their agreement or disagreement choose whether the whole runs with the part or against it. A computation, at this rung, is a number that has learned to execute.

Step such a thing forward and you have a Turing-style process in the ordinary mathematical sense — the construction reads the current state and produces the next, exactly as any machine advances, the interpretation of a symbol kept honestly distinct from the update of the internal state. Hand the step a bare program and it returns the program unchanged: source code, asked to advance, has nowhere to go until it is set running. Hand it a computation already underway and it feeds the current state forward into the next — the familiar seam where a *held* reading becomes a *produced* one, the fence and its posts that Chapters 1 through 3 marked on the carrier, the odometer, and the clock. We mark it once more and pass on. The step is where the process counts, and the whole content of stepping is this handing of the present state forward to become the next.

Now the genuine thesis of the rung, the thing the construction will not let pass as machinery. A Turing-style process presupposes an *alphabet*. It computes with symbols — marks that can be written, compared, told one from another — and the usual telling helps itself to those marks for free, as though mathematics had handed over an alphabet along with the numbers. The construction refuses the gift. **Representation comes from outside the mathematics.** The symbols a computation runs on are not derived from anything beneath them; they are supplied, handed in by something other than the calculation that consumes them. This is not a defect to repair and not a step the construction skipped; it is a claim it makes on purpose. Up to this rung the tower has owned all of itself — every number grown from the Fact **true = true**, no quantity borrowed, no magnitude imported. Representation is the first thing the construction admits it does not own. The marks come from elsewhere, and Turing's machine, for all its rigor, never said where; it assumed an alphabet that behaves and built atop the assumption. The construction makes the assumption visible and refuses to let it hide.

The gate at this rung does what gates do: it **tanges** a candidate by a single characteristic — can this stand as a representation at all? — can a value be made to speak through a symbol, so that the mark in the head of one person and the mark on the page and the state of the machine all carry the same value across the gap between them. Only what passes is admitted to mean anything. The construction does not **funge** representations into a pool of interchangeable marks and ask merely what kind they are; it holds each candidate up against the demand that it actually carry a value, and lets the demand decide. What we keep from the rung is the modesty of it. The construction has built a computation, ordered it, stepped it forward — all of that in its own currency. And then, at exactly the point where the computation needs an alphabet to run on, it stops and says: *this part is borrowed.* It marks the debt rather than hide it, and proceeds knowing it is owed.

## 4.2 — The Chaitin barrier, and noise

To calibrate an instrument you need a standard, and the most demanding standard imaginable is one whose every digit is perfectly definite and yet impossible to anticipate — so that agreeing with it cannot be faked, because there is no shortcut to the agreement. The construction reaches for exactly that extreme. It posits a sequence built from the halting behavior of computations: a quantity that is either *halting* — settled, its value cached and returned — or *nonhalting*, still running, pinned a little tighter only by being let run a little longer. Such a sequence is definite in every place and computable only by unbounded work; you can drive it as close as you like to its true value by spending more and more time, and you can never spend enough to finish. The construction takes a sequence of this character as the threshold — the floor of what computation can settle, the one barrier known to be real. It is the deepest possible calibration mark: not a finer division on the dial but the place past which no dial can be ruled at all.

> *A note on the barrier.* Be exact about the status of this move, because the temptation to overstate it is strong. The construction does not compute the constant. It does not produce a value for it, name its digits, or derive its existence from the rungs beneath. It *posits* a sequence of the right character — definite, approachable, never completable — and treats that posited thing as the threshold the instrument is calibrated against. The barrier is real as a *limit on computation*, in the plain sense that there is a known floor beneath which a computing process cannot settle. It is not a number this book produces. The threshold is a modeling choice — a standard the construction lays down and calibrates against — and we flag it as posited rather than let it pass for something the tower has computed. The instrument is held up to the barrier; the barrier itself is asserted, not earned, and never crossed.

Set against that threshold is a second move of the same register, and it recurs through the rest of the book, so it is worth pinning down now. No measurement is clean: every instrument has noise. The needle's smear, the cable's hiss, the jitter at the floor — there is always a disturbance riding along with the reading, and ordinarily one models it as something external, a contaminant that leaks in from the world and corrupts an otherwise pure signal. The construction declines that picture and makes a different choice. It treats the noise as *the signal's own process* — generated by the very same machinery that generates the signal, one source wearing two faces. One cannot watch the instrument settle cleanly against the threshold, because the threshold is exactly the thing one cannot watch settle; so instead of measuring the barrier directly, the construction models the noise that stands between it and the barrier, and models that noise as a computation too.

> *A note on noise as the signal's process.* This is a reading laid over the machinery, not a result wrung from it. The construction *assumes* that the process which produces the reading and the process which produces the noise are one process, the way an analog instrument's hiss and its measurement come from a single underlying physics rather than two. The motive is candidly stated in the source: keeping the noise inside the same process is what lets the construction model it at all without stepping outside itself and importing a second, foreign source of disturbance — it is also how the construction keeps from referring to itself, holding the recursion at arm's length. The disturbance becomes not a contaminant but the signal's own shadow — same hand, two gestures. We mark this as modeling. Nothing here proves that real noise *is* the signal's own process; the construction posits the identification because it is the assumption under which the floor stays self-contained, and we keep it in the register of interpretation rather than dress it as a fact the tower has established.

With both pieces in place the gate at this rung admits a measurement on what amounts to a promise. It accepts a reading as physical when the process can, in principle, resolve it against the threshold — a near-tautology, and the construction says so without embarrassment. It does not here defeat the barrier; it declares that it intends to work *beneath* it, calibrated against a floor it will not pretend to cross. That is the honest shape of the rung: a posited threshold, a posited identification of noise with signal, and an instrument that measures within the limit those two define rather than over it. Everything the rest of the book measures, it measures in that posture — beneath a barrier it respects, through a noise it owns.

## 4.3 — Machine-epsilon, and a comparison left to the reader

Beneath the Chaitin barrier sits a humbler floor, and it is the one the working instrument actually bumps against. The barrier is what *no* computation can cross; this is what *this* computation cannot subdivide — the smallest amount it can represent at all, the least sliver it can tell apart from nothing. Every finite machine has one: a smallest representable fraction, the gap between zero and the next thing up, below which a quantity simply rounds away to nothing. The construction names this smallest amount alpha, and it carries it not as a fixed number — it has no value to give it — but as a placeholder, a metavariable standing in for the smallest distinguishable amount at this depth of resolution.

The placeholder has the now-familiar two-part grammar: a base, the smallest amount fixed at the current level, and a step, which carries the placeholder one level deeper. And the lesson of the step is the one that matters here. *Resolution can be pushed.* The smallest amount is not absolute; it is relative to how hard the instrument is willing to look. Lift the placeholder to a finer level and a smaller least-amount becomes available — the gap between zero and the next thing up has narrowed — and lift it again and it narrows further, each level resolving a sliver the level above could not. There is always a smallest amount, but *which* smallest amount depends on the depth, and the depth is something the instrument can deepen by spending more of the time the last chapter taught it to count. The floor is real at every level and movable between them — the needle read under a loupe, then under a finer loupe, the least step shrinking as the instrument consents to look harder. The placeholder is less a number than a number's address, the same slot reoccupied at every resolution by a smaller tenant.

It would be natural, having built two of these smallest-amounts, to ask which is the lesser — to compare two placeholders and say which one resolves finer. Here the construction does something unusual, and it is the heart of the rung, so read it slowly.

> *A note on the open comparison.* The construction does not decide it. Asked for a general rule that takes two of these smallest-amounts and returns which is the lesser, it declines — and not by hiding the difficulty behind a name, the way a weaker construction would slip a gap past the reader by christening it and moving on. It leaves the comparison genuinely *open*, and it is candid that the slot is empty. What it supplies in place of a verdict is a *method*: bisection. Given a stick of length L, break it into two pieces of roughly L/2; you are not promised the cut lands exactly at the middle, but you are promised that the two pieces reassemble into the whole, and by halving and halving you may drive the comparison as fine as you please and read off which amount is the lesser to whatever precision you are willing to pay for. The construction hands the reader the procedure and withholds the answer. This is scaffold, not result: a deliberate empty slot, declared as such — an operation the book builds the frame for and then, on purpose, leaves for the reader to run. It is not an oversight and not a result in disguise. When a later rung needs two smallest-amounts compared, this is the slot it will draw on, and the construction has left it open by design, with the bisection sitting ready inside it. The frame is built. The verdict is yours to produce.

This candor is of a piece with everything the book has done since the ticket. A Fact is a claim with its receipt stapled on, and the construction has been scrupulous about never stapling a receipt it cannot produce. Here it reaches a comparison whose receipt it has chosen not to manufacture — and rather than fake one, it says so, points at the bisection that would manufacture it, and moves on. The smallest amount is built. The means to compare two of them is built. The comparison itself is the one thing on this rung left deliberately, honestly, unfinished — and the honesty of leaving it so is worth more than a verdict smuggled in under a confident name.

## 4.4 — Variance, curvature, and the first integral

The chapter turns, at its last rung, from what the instrument cannot resolve to how it reads change — and it opens by correcting a habit. We are used to treating covariance and its opposite as *definitions*: a thing simply is covariant, going with a change, or contravariant, going against it, the way a label is fixed to a jar. The construction insists these are not labels but *responses*. You do not know whether a thing runs with a change or against it until you apply the change — a stimulus, a shift of coordinates — and watch which way the thing moves. The specialist in differential geometry learned covariance by watching vectors transform one way and covectors the other under a change of coordinates; the variance was never in the definition, it was in the response to the stimulus. The construction builds exactly that: a thing that carries a change through its levels and records, as a response rather than a property read off at rest, which parts follow the stimulus and which oppose it. This is the trial of Chapter 3 turned on a change of coordinates instead of a passing car — a stimulus presented, a response read, the agreement or disagreement of the two settled through truth-parity as everything is. Variance is something the instrument finds out by pushing, and the construction earns it honestly — the response is built, applied, and checkable, squarely in the demonstrated register the trial established. Three specialists, three stimuli, three patterns of response, and one word for all of them: covariance is never a property an object wears at rest, but the way it answers when something is done to it.

Pushing is also where the chapter's physics names enter, and they enter as readings laid over that finite machinery, not as results drawn out of it.

> *A note on the curvature form and the friction bound.* The construction models a *slip* — the moment a thing held by static resistance gives way and begins to move — and in writing how the slip's tendency accumulates as it propagates through the levels, it produces an expression the source labels with the field-strength formula of gauge theory: a change of potential plus the potential's interaction with itself, written **F = dA + A ∧ A**. We flag the label for exactly what it is. The construction has built a recursion describing how a slip pushes a dimension forward through the levels, and has *named* that recursion after the gauge curvature it gestures toward. Nothing on this rung derives gauge theory; the formula is a name laid over a finite variance form, chosen because the shape of the accumulation echoes the shape of curvature. In the same register the construction reads an observation as a *velocity* — zero at rest, one in motion — and models the threshold of slipping as a *friction inequality*, the tangential force held under a coefficient times the normal force, **|F| ≤ μ|N|**, treating the onset of motion as an entropic event the instrument can detect even where the bare arithmetic cannot tell the object has moved. These are interpretations — physics names hung on a finite construction. The slip's recursion is built; gauge curvature and static friction and entropy are the suggestive names hung on it, marked here as names rather than theorems. The Area that follows is not in this register: it is built, and it stays demonstrated.

What the rung leaves behind, stripped of every such gloss and standing squarely in the demonstrated register, is the first genuine integral in the book. Until now the construction has *counted*: stacks of digits, ticks of a clock, trials in a study, all of it accumulation by discrete wholes. The area is something new. It is built the same way a number is — a base, a single accumulated quantity, and a step that lays one more increment of change atop the running total — but read in the opposite direction from the numbers of Chapter 1. Those were big-endian, most significant mark first; the area accumulates the other way, gathering its increments from the new end, each tick of observed change added onto what has come before. It is the same covariant-contravariant duality the whole book runs on, turned now to the business of summing change rather than stacking digits — and what it produces is a running total of area, accumulated tick by tick from the variances the floor permits the instrument to read.

This is a real construction, not a name. The area is built, it accumulates, and it can be checked against itself increment by increment, exactly as a number can. There is no continuum smuggled in beneath it and no length imported from outside: the area is finite, an honest sum of finite responses, the first object in the book that *integrates* rather than merely counts. And it is the piece the whole tower has been climbing toward. With it the apparatus has, at last, assembled every raw material of analysis out of its own counting and nothing borrowed: number, from a Fact; time, from a repeatable beat; computation, running on symbols it honestly admits are handed in; a barrier it calibrates against and a smallest amount it resolves down to; a comparison of two such amounts left open on purpose for the reader to run; variance, read as a response to a stimulus rather than a label; and now an integral, to accumulate that variance into an area. The needle's floor is fully mapped — what lies below it, what calibrates against it, what the instrument can resolve and what it cannot — and above the floor there is, for the first time, an accumulated area the construction has genuinely built. The defendant is still standing, and the verdict is still owed. But the instrument has stopped being merely a thing that counts and become a thing that integrates, and what it will do with that area — which path, of all the paths the accumulation could take, the construction is forced to single out — is the work of the part to come.
