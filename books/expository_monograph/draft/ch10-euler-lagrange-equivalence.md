# Chapter 10 — The Euler-Lagrange equivalence

The prosecution has stopped hedging. For nine chapters the claim was that the three witnesses read one number; now the prosecutor will say *which* number, and stake the case on it. The car drove one path down the road. The needle, the radar, and the GPS each read a small variation of that path — a wiggle off the true drive, reported as the drive itself — and the previous chapter located the drive among all its wiggles: it is the stationary one, the path at the floor of the basin, where every small nudge of the midpoint costs nothing to first order. That is settled. What the trial wants now is a verdict it can pronounce on a single path held up before it. Not *somewhere there is a true drive* but *this one, the one the instruments read, is it* — and the answer must be a clean yes or no, read off the path itself.

Here is the form the verdict takes. Each instrument's reading sits a hair off the true drive, and the difference between the reading and the path it read off — between what the needle swore and the drive the needle was turning on — is a *residual*: the leftover, the discrepancy the instrument carries because it sampled a variation and not the bare path. A reading matches the drive exactly when that residual is zero. While the path is wrong — while it sits on a slope, some reroute cheaper than it — the residual is nonzero, and every witness inherits the gap. But move the path to the stationary one, the drive where the first variation vanishes in every direction, and the residual collapses. There is nothing left over. The reading and the path it read off are the same path; the wiggle the instrument took has cost, to first order, nothing at all. *Match means the residual vanishes* — that is the whole of the verdict, and the rest of the chapter is the precise statement of the condition under which it is reached.

The condition has a name the calculus of variations gave it long ago: a path satisfies its *Euler-Lagrange equation*. The prosecution's claim, stated sharply, is that two things are the same thing — that the path is stationary, sitting at the flat floor the last chapter built, *exactly* when it satisfies this equation, and that at this equation the residual goes quiet. The first half of that — stationary if and only if Euler-Lagrange — is the equivalence the trial can prove, cleanly, from the instrument the previous chapter set down. The second half — that the construction can *decide*, of a path handed to it, that the residual has indeed collapsed to nothing — is a different kind of claim, and the honest part of this chapter is keeping the two apart. One is demonstrated. The other is assumed. The trial reaches its verdict on the strength of an equivalence it earns and a single principle it grants itself, and a careful juror should know which is which before the gavel falls.

## 10.1 — Stationary if and only if Euler-Lagrange

Begin with the operator, built from what the last chapter already has. The first variation read the cost's response to a midpoint wiggle as a composed difference of two legs — the *left variation*, how the leg from source to slip changed, plus the *right variation*, how the leg from slip to target changed. Those two pieces were not incidental bookkeeping; they are the whole content of the derivative, because the trip has exactly two legs and a midpoint wiggle touches exactly both. The *discrete Euler-Lagrange operator* is what you get by holding those two pieces apart and reading their sum. For a path and a chosen wiggle it returns one number: the left contribution at the midpoint plus the right contribution at the midpoint — the signed change in the first leg's price added to the signed change in the second's. This is the discrete derivative of the action from the previous chapter, written in the form that exposes the midpoint as the seam where two legs meet. No limit is taken; no continuum is assumed; the operator is a finite difference of finite leg-costs, exactly as the first variation was, now split at the joint and re-summed.

The *Euler-Lagrange equation* is the demand that this sum vanish — and vanish not for one wiggle but for every one. A path *satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation* when, for each choice of alternate slip, the left contribution and the right contribution cancel: the change the wiggle forced into the first leg is paid back, sign for sign, by the change it forced into the second. Push the midpoint one way and the source-leg gets dearer by some amount; the target-leg gets cheaper by exactly that amount; the seam absorbs the wiggle and the total does not move. That cancellation, demanded across the whole field of wiggles, is the equation. It is a condition on the path alone, read by interrogating its seam against every direction it could be nudged.

Now the equivalence, which is the heart of the section and is *earned*. The claim is that a path is stationary exactly when it satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation — and the previous chapter already did the work that makes this true, because the operator's two pieces were defined to sum to the first variation. The left contribution plus the right contribution *is* the leading account of the cost's response: this is not a theorem about them, it is what they were built to be, the decomposition of the first variation into its leg-by-leg parts. So follow the two directions. If a path is stationary — flat, its first variation zero in every direction — then in every direction the left and right contributions, which add up to that vanishing first variation, must themselves sum to zero; the path satisfies the equation. And conversely, if a path satisfies the equation — left and right cancelling in every direction — then their sum, which is the first variation, vanishes in every direction; the path is flat, the path is stationary. The implication runs both ways with nothing lost in either crossing, because the two quantities are not merely equal in value but are the same quantity assembled two ways: the first variation read whole, and the first variation read as a sum of its two legs. There is no remainder between them — the previous chapter spent its care establishing that the leading term is the whole term, that the change in the action under a wiggle *is* the first variation with nothing left over — and so there is no slack between *stationary* and *Euler-Lagrange*. No estimate is made and no limit is taken; the equivalence holds on the nose, an identity read two ways, because the quantity on the one side and the quantity on the other are the same finite difference of leg-costs assembled in two orders. They are one condition wearing two faces, the geometric face that says the action is flat here and the algebraic face that says the seam's two contributions cancel here. *Stationary if and only if Euler-Lagrange.* This is register (i): demonstrated, finite, borrowed from nothing the construction has not built.

With the equivalence in hand the operator becomes a selector, and here the book's first verb does its work. The Euler-Lagrange condition **tanges** the stationary paths out of the whole field of paths. To tange is to individuate by a deciding trait — to hold each candidate against one question and keep apart exactly those that answer it — and the question now is sharp and local: held at this path, does the Euler-Lagrange derivative vanish? Do the two contributions at the seam cancel in every direction? Every path is asked it. The ones that answer yes are the stationary paths, separated clean; the rest are held back. This is the same cut the previous chapter made by the trait *flat here*, now read off the seam instead of the basin — and the equivalence is precisely the guarantee that the two cuts coincide, that *Euler-Lagrange derivative zero here* tanges out the very same paths that *flat here* did. The stationary paths are not a soft neighborhood of good drives. They are the exact set the trait carves, and the operator is the blade.

And at each such path, the book's second verb completes the picture. Fix a path that satisfies the equation and look at the directions in which the derivative vanishes — every wiggle whose left and right contributions cancel. Singly these are distinct wiggles, each sliding the slip to a different mark. But the construction does not keep them apart. It **funges** them: it drops every such direction into one bag and treats them as interchangeable, because the only thing it cares about is the trait they share — *this direction leaves the action stationary*, the seam absorbs it, the cost does not move. A wiggle pushing the midpoint one way for no first-order cost and another pushing it elsewhere for no first-order cost: the operator cannot tell them apart, and does not try. They funge into a single pool, the *kernel* of vanishing directions, the flat directions quotiented by the relation the operator does not see. The two verbs are the two faces of one fact, exactly as in the previous chapter: the Euler-Lagrange condition tanges the stationary paths out of all paths by the trait that the derivative vanishes, and at each such path it funges the vanishing directions into one kernel, pooled by the very trait the tanging selected. A cut on paths; a quotient on directions; one flatness read from both sides.

So far everything is earned. The operator is built, the equivalence is proved, the stationary paths are tanged and their kernel funged — all of it finite, all of it demonstrated from the first variation. Now the trial wants the thing it actually came for: a *verdict*. It does not want to know that stationarity and the equation coincide in the abstract. It wants to take this path, the one the witnesses are reading, and have the construction announce — yes or no — that its residual has collapsed to nothing.

Read carefully what that demand involves. The detection the trial wants is a two-channel reading. The first channel is the coarse one: the *direct residual*, the gap between the path read whole and the path read through its midpoint — the admissibility check of the previous chapter, the whole agreeing with the sum of its parts. That channel is a plain comparison of two costs; the construction computes it outright and reads off whether it is zero. The second channel is the fine one: the Euler-Lagrange residual, the seam's sum, demanded to vanish across *every* direction. And here is the asymmetry the chapter turns on. The first channel is a finite computation. The second is a claim about every wiggle at once — a quantifier ranging over the whole infinite field of alternate slips — and the construction cannot, by computing, sweep an infinite field and confirm that the seam cancels in all of it. It can check any one direction, and a thousand, and find them all flat, and still not have ruled out the one direction it did not check. It can prove the equivalence that *characterizes* the condition — can say precisely what stationarity means and that it is the very same thing as the equation — but it cannot, of an arbitrary path handed to it cold, *compute* the standing answer, because the answer ranges over more directions than any computation visits. To pronounce the verdict — to say *this residual has collapsed to truth-zero* — the construction needs the condition to be decidable: it needs a verdict to be available, a procedure that, asked of any path, returns yes or no. And that availability the construction does not prove. It grants itself.

This is the line the chapter exists to draw, and it must be drawn without blur. The *equivalence* — stationary if and only if Euler-Lagrange — is proved, register (i), earned from the first variation with no remainder and no assumption. The *detection* — the construction deciding, of a given path, that the residual has collapsed to nothing, so that what the reading leaves over is truth-zero exactly when both channels go quiet — rests on a principle the construction adds for its own purpose: that the stationarity condition admits a standing verdict. Given that principle, the collapse follows cleanly: when both channels read zero, the residual the construction was carrying is replaced by the value that signals nothing-left-over, the truth-zero a match leaves behind, and the witnesses' readings and the drive they read off are confirmed the same path. But the principle is not free, and it is not derived. It is the one place in this band of the construction where the work helps itself to something it could find nowhere else.

> *A note on the verdict.* The equivalence of this section is demonstrated; the collapse of the residual to truth-zero is not demonstrated but *assumed*, and the two must not be confused. Proving that a path is stationary exactly when it satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation is one thing, and the construction does it outright. Deciding, of a path handed over without warning, that the condition holds — producing the verdict that drives the detection — is another, and it leans on a principle the construction grants itself: that the stationarity condition is decidable, that a verdict is always there to be read. This is the single load-bearing assumption of the variational band, the one custom thing the development asks the reader to allow rather than derive. It is named, and its reach is bounded, where the four accepted principles are set out — see *The Axiom of Choice*. The detection theorems that announce the residual gone quiet are true *given* that principle; they do not establish it. The equivalence is earned; the collapse is granted. A reader who keeps those two registers apart keeps the whole honesty of the chapter.

So return to the trial with the line drawn. A path is held up, the one the needle and the radar and the GPS are all reading. The prosecution claims a match — the readings agree with the drive, the residual is zero — and asks the construction to back it. What backs it is two things, and they are not the same kind of thing. One is an equivalence the construction proves: that the path's being stationary, the flat floor of the last chapter, is the very same fact as its satisfying the Euler-Lagrange equation, the seam's two contributions cancelling in every direction — earned, finite, exact. The other is an assumption the construction grants itself: that this condition can be *decided*, so that the verdict the trial needs is available to be pronounced. On the strength of the first, the stationary paths are tanged from all the rest and their flat directions funged into one kernel; on the strength of the second, a specific path's residual is read as collapsed to truth-zero and the match is confirmed. The residual that the trial cares about — the leftover between reading and drive — is zero exactly here, at the path the equation tanges, decided by the verdict the construction assumed it could give.

And that is not the end of the climb but the floor of it. The Euler-Lagrange equivalence reports *where* the action is flat; it pools the flat directions and says nothing of how the cost bends once you stand at a stationary path. The verdict it reaches is a first-order verdict, the leading term and no more. What the residual collapses to — that truth-zero — is the seed of the single invariant the whole of Part II is climbing toward: the curvature the remainder would have carried if it were not zero, the way the action turns around the stationary drive, the *second* variation that the construction is built to name and that a later chapter will reach. The instrument of this chapter is the rung beneath it. Stationary if and only if Euler-Lagrange is the equivalence that locates the drive; that the residual there can be *declared* gone is the one thing the construction asks to be allowed; and on those two — one earned, one granted, kept honestly apart — the single invariant will stand.
