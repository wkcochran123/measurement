# Chapter 5 — Gauge, superposition, and the local present

## The needle lags

Watch the needle one more time, and this time watch *when* it is right. The car passes, the pointer swings, and by the time it has come to rest the car is no longer where the reading says it is — it has moved on down the road while the cable was still dragging the pointer into place. The last chapter taught us that a reading takes time, an estimable and finite amount of it, and this chapter collects the bill. Whatever the needle reports, it reports a little late: the value on the dial is not where the world is but where the world *was*, a moment ago, when the swing began. The instrument is honest, and the price of its honesty is that it trails the thing it measures by exactly the time the reading cost.

So the present the needle holds is not the world's present. It is a *local* present — the instrument's own, assembled out of what it has managed to take in and has not yet had to let go of. And it is held only briefly. A reading kept too long goes stale, because the world it described has moved; a reading acted on too soon has not finished settling. Between those two failures is a narrow window, and the present is whatever the instrument is holding inside that window: local memory with a deadline, a value good for an instant and then either committed or lost. The needle does not store the past. It holds the most recent thing it has not yet been forced to forget.

Look closer still, at the needle *between* two marks, before it has committed. Mid-swing, before it has settled into any gap, the pointer is not yet reporting anything — it is in transit across several readings at once, any of which it might collapse to when it stops. The value it will announce is, for that instant, un-resolved: not one reading and not another, but a blend of all the readings the swing could still end on. Only when the pointer commits does the blend resolve to a single mark. Until then the instrument carries, in effect, a superposition of the verdicts it might yet deliver.

This puts the speedometer in a new light. The dial is not a passive window onto a number waiting outside; it is a *gauge* — a thing that negotiates the lag. It runs a clock, so it knows how stale its reading is; it admits the car as an event worth registering; and it commits to a value on a deadline, trading the chance of a fresher reading for the certainty of having one at all. Every honest instrument strikes that bargain. It cannot reach the world's present, which would cost time it does not have, so it reports its own.

Three things have to be built to say this precisely, and the chapter takes them in order. First, the local present: why there is no single global *now* the instrument can order all events into, and what the present is once that single ordering is gone. Second, the gauge as a process — a clock paired with a gate that admits events — that negotiates the lag and turns a phenomenon into a reading. Third, the holder that carries either one settled state or a blend of several, and the gate that decides when two such holders are correlated. The first is built plainly; over the second and the third, where the construction touches physics, we hang the names physics uses and mark them as names.

## 5.1 — The local present

Start with the thing that has to fail, because the present is what is left standing once it has failed. We are used to a single *now*: one universal instant that every event either falls before, falls after, or shares. Lay all events on that one timeline and they line up in a single order, every pair decided — earlier, later, or together — with no ties left dangling. The construction does not get to assume that timeline, and the reason is the one the last two chapters have been circling. A reading takes time, and the time it takes is *local* to the instrument that takes it. Two events far apart cannot be compared by a single clock, because there is no single clock that reaches both at once without a reading-time of its own creeping in between them. Simultaneity is not handed down from a master clock; it is something each instrument decides locally, from where it stands, and two instruments standing apart need not decide it the same way.

That is enough to break the single order, and it is worth seeing exactly where the break falls. To have one global *now* is to have every pair of events well-ordered: any two compared and decided, no genuine ties. But once *together* is a local verdict rather than a universal fact, the comparison stops being total. Take two events the instrument registers as simultaneous — neither before the other, struck at once as far as it can tell. They are not the same event; they are distinct, and a single global order would have to break the tie and put one ahead of the other. The instrument cannot break it, because to break it would take a reading it has not got. So the pair sits genuinely undecided: distinct, not ordered, neither ahead. A relation with ties like that is not a single line of *befores* and *afters*. It is a partial order — most pairs decided, some left honestly tied — and the single global *now* is exactly the thing it is missing.

Make the failure concrete on the dashboard, because it is easy to mistake for a quibble and it is not. Two cars cross the sensor's field, one near and one far, and both swing the needle at what the instrument records as the same instant. The near car's strike reaches the cable a hair sooner than the far car's — the far one had farther to travel to be noticed — but the difference is below the floor the last chapter mapped, smaller than the least time the clock can tell. So the instrument has no reading that separates them. Asked which came first, it cannot answer, and the honest thing is not to guess: it files the two strikes as simultaneous and leaves their order open. A second instrument, standing somewhere else, sits at a different distance from each car and may have a reading that *does* separate them — and may separate them the other way. There is no appeal to a master clock that settles which instrument is right, because the master clock is the very thing reading-time took away. The tie is real, it is local, and it is the rule rather than the exception wherever two events fall closer together than a single instrument can resolve.

Read this through the engine that has run every order in the book and it is not strange at all; it is the same parity check, declining to decide where it has no ground to. To compare two events the construction reads their signs and consults the parity. Where the two agree — both struck the same way, the same cadence on both — the comparison runs covariantly, counting forward by wholes, one event filed after the other along the count beneath them. Where they disagree, it runs the other way, contravariantly, against the count, resolving toward parts. And where there is no fact of the matter about which came first — where the two are simultaneous and the instrument has no local reading that separates them — the parity returns no verdict, and the construction, scrupulous as ever, files them as tied rather than inventing an order it cannot produce. The single global ordering fails not by contradiction but by abstention: at the simultaneous pairs there is simply nothing to count, and the honest order leaves them uncounted.

So the present cannot be *the* instant on a universal line, because there is no such line. What is it instead? It is a held reading with a deadline. The instrument keeps the most recent value it has resolved — the swing that has just committed — and it keeps it as the present for exactly as long as it has not been overwritten by the next swing or spent on the next decision. The present is not a point in some external time; it is the contents of the instrument's hold, *now* meaning *what I have taken in and not yet let go of*. And because the hold has a deadline, the present is perishable. Carry it past the deadline and the world it described has moved out from under it; the reading is stale, no longer present, only past. This is why the present is local in the strongest sense: it belongs to one instrument, lasts one window, and is true only there and then. Two instruments hold two presents, and there is no master clock to make them agree, because the master clock is the very thing simultaneity took away.

Build the holding plainly, because it is a construction and not a figure of speech. Beneath the present sits a sensing process, and it carries two quantities of very different status. The first is a running accumulation — the area the last chapter taught the instrument to integrate, the total return gathered tick by tick — and this one you are allowed to read; it is the value the present reports. The second is an internal coefficient, a measure of how much the instrument resists before it gives way and moves, and this one you are *not* allowed to read: it is hidden inside the process, doing its work where the reading cannot reach it, and the construction is candid that you do not get to know it. Holding a present, then, is a single definite step taken on the visible accumulation alone. The sensing process takes the value it currently holds and hands it forward to become the value it next holds — the accumulated return advanced by one increment — and the present is bound to its successor by exactly that forward hand: *this held reading gives way to that one*. The relation is decided, not hoped for, and it is the same seam every counting process in this book has had — the place where a held reading becomes a produced one, the fence and its posts, marked once more. Here the seam is timekeeping at its most literal: the instant the instrument lets go of the *now* it had and takes up the *now* it has next, on its own memory and against its own deadline.

What the rung leaves us is modest and exact. There is no single *now* to order all events into — simultaneity breaks the well-ordering, and the honest order on events is partial, tied wherever the instrument cannot locally separate two strikes. The present is not a coordinate but a held reading, kept briefly and then surrendered, local to the instrument and good only for its window. Everything the instrument will do with *now* — reading it, comparing it, committing to it — it does inside that window and nowhere else. The present is built, and it is built small.

## 5.2 — The gauge that negotiates

With a present that is held and perishable, the instrument needs a process that fills it — that takes the world as it arrives and turns it into a reading committed before the deadline. Build first the raw material that process works on. A *phenomenon* is not a bare event but something layered: it is what the instrument can be made to register, assembled in the now-familiar grammar of a base and a step. At bottom there is a bare registration — a Fact paired with the accumulated quantity that stands behind it, the least thing the instrument can be made to notice at all. Above that there is a registration framed by an initial condition: the same, but set against the ground state the instrument starts from. And above *that* there is the full observation — a before and an after, each with its quantity, each itself a phenomenon — which is the shape Chapter 3 gave a single tick, now carrying not just a beat but everything the instrument took in across it. A phenomenon, in short, is a reading with its history folded in: not *what is the value* but *what happened, against what background, between which two states*.

These too come ordered, and by the same rule. To compare two phenomena the construction reads the parity of their underlying Facts. Where the Facts agree the comparison runs covariantly — the richer registration counted as the larger, its quantities and its before-and-after filed forward by wholes along the phenomena beneath. Where the Facts disagree it runs contravariantly, the order turning over, resolving against the count toward parts. A bare registration sits beneath a framed one, which sits beneath a full observation, exactly as a single digit sits beneath a number and a number beneath a study. And a full observation carries the partial order of 5.1 inside it: comparing two of them means comparing each before against the other's before and each after against the other's after, and where one of those pairs is simultaneous and cannot be separated, the comparison declines to decide and the two observations are left tied — the same honest abstention, threaded down from events into the phenomena built out of them. Nothing new is assumed. The order on phenomena is the order on everything: compare two signs, let their agreement choose whether the whole runs with the part or against it, and where the signs say nothing, count nothing.

Now the process. A *gauge* is what reads a phenomenon, and the construction builds it as two pieces fastened together. The first is a clock — a phenomenon of its own, the steady beat of Chapter 3 raised to this level, so the gauge always knows how much time its reading is costing. The second is an *event-gate*: a step that takes a phenomenon and advances it by admitting one event. Hand the gate a bare registration and it pushes the quantity forward by the instrument's own settling — the slip the last chapter built, the smallest motion the cable can commit to — and returns a richer phenomenon for it. Hand it a framed registration, or a full observation, and it does the consequential thing: it folds the clock in. It produces a fresh observation whose *before* is where the reading stood, whose *after* is that reading advanced by the settling, and whose history carries the clock alongside — so every event the gauge admits is stamped with the beat it arrived on. That stamping is the whole content of the gate. It does not measure the phenomenon and it does not pass it through untouched; it *admits* it — lets one event across, advances the held reading by the instrument's least motion, and marks the result with the time the admission cost.

This is the negotiation the opener promised, and it is worth saying plainly what is being negotiated. The gauge cannot have the world's present; it can only have a phenomenon admitted across the gate, advanced by a finite settling, stamped with a clock that says how stale it already is. So the gauge trades. It gives up freshness — the reading is always lagged by the settling it took to admit — in exchange for a value it can actually hold and commit before the deadline. A phenomenon is *registered* — admitted to mean something, allowed to leave its mark on the held present — exactly when the gauge can run this bargain on it: clock the cost, advance by the least motion, stamp and commit. The gate **tanges** a candidate phenomenon by that single characteristic — can this be admitted across, clocked and committed, at all? — and only what passes leaves a number-shaped mark on the present. It does not **funge** phenomena into a pool and sort them by kind. It holds each one up against the demand that it can actually be clocked and admitted, and lets the demand decide. What is built here is finite through and through: a clock that beats, a gate that admits one event and advances a held reading by one least settling, a present that takes the mark. There is no continuum under it and no field imported from outside. It is the trial of Chapter 3, run now against a deadline, with a clock folded into every tick.

> *A note on \"gauge\" and the curvature framing.* The process just built is finite and demonstrated: a clock paired with an event-gate that admits one phenomenon at a time and stamps it with the beat it arrived on. Calling it a *gauge* is the first interpretation laid over it. In physics a gauge is an instrument that takes a physical principle and reads a quantity off it, and the construction's clock-plus-gate has the *shape* of that — a standard process used to register what the world presents — so the name fits and the source reaches for it. The stronger reach is the Yang-Mills one, and it is the one to flag hardest. The temptation is to say that anything the gauge can keep flat — any phenomenon admitted with a vanishing covariant change, no net curvature in how the reading propagates through its levels — must carry a curvature of the change-plus-self-interaction form, the field strength **F = dA + A ∧ A** of gauge theory, the same expression the last chapter hung on the slip's recursion. Be exact about the register. Nothing on this rung derives gauge theory. The construction has built a finite process that admits events against a clock; *gauge*, and the curvature framing that says a kept-flat reading carries a self-interacting field strength, are physics names laid over that finite process because the shape of how the reading propagates echoes the shape of a connection and its curvature. The process is real and built. Gauge theory is the name, and we mark it as a name rather than let it pass for something the tower has proved.

## 5.3 — Superposition and the turn

The gauge admits a phenomenon and commits a present — but the opener noticed something the gauge alone does not yet capture: the reading *between* marks, before it has committed, is not one value but a blend of the values it might collapse to. To carry that, the construction builds a *holder* — a thing that can be in one of a few settled states, or in a blend of them at once. Render it in its own grammar, with no nicknames, just the math. At bottom the holder carries a single settled state: a Fact paired with a quantity, the committed reading, one definite mark. Above that it carries a *labelled* state — the same settled content, but tagged with a Fact that marks it as belonging to one branch rather than another, so two holders can carry the same quantity under different labels and be told apart. And above *that* it carries a *blend*: a Fact together with two holders at once, neither chosen, both held — the un-resolved reading, the pointer mid-swing across two marks it has not yet committed to. The holder is built exactly to be the thing the opener described: it can sit on a single mark, or it can sit, undecided, across several.

The order on holders runs the book's one rule, with the tie that simultaneity forced in 5.1 now built straight into the comparison. Two settled states compare by their quantities under the parity of their Facts — agree, and the comparison counts forward by wholes; a settled state always sits beneath a labelled one, which sits beneath a blend, the coarse order of *how resolved* the holder is. Where the Facts disagree the comparison turns over and resolves against the count toward parts. And two blends compare by comparing the holders inside them, each against its partner, under the parity of the blend's own Fact — and where that parity fails to decide, the comparison returns no verdict and the two blends are left tied, distinct but unordered, precisely the partial order the local present demanded. A blend is not well-ordered against another blend any more than two simultaneous events are. That is not a defect in the holder; it is the definition of two readings held at once with nothing yet to separate them.

Now the gate that the holders need: a way to decide when two of them are *correlated* — when two un-resolved readings are locked together, so that resolving one tells you how the other resolves. The construction builds it as a sharp, finite test, and the test is deliberately narrow. Present two holders. If both are blends, the gate reads the labelling Fact off each and asks the one question it knows how to ask: do the two labels agree? Where they agree, the two blends are correlated — locked to the same branch, so that the swing of one fixes the swing of the other. Where they disagree, uncorrelated — independent, each free to resolve as it will. If the holders are not both blends — if either has already settled to a single mark — the gate returns *uncorrelated* outright: a committed reading is locked to nothing, because there is nothing left in it to resolve. The gate **tanges** the pair by that single characteristic — same label or not — and returns a decided verdict, correlated or not, exactly as the trial tanged a response by whether it was the typical one. It does not **funge** the holders into a pool and ask what kind they are; it holds the two up against the one question that locks them and lets the answer decide. Two blends, one Fact compared against another, *true* against *true* — and out of that single parity check falls the entire content of whether two un-resolved readings move together.

That is the whole construction: a holder that carries a settled state, a labelled state, or a blend; and a gate that tanges two holders by their labels and returns whether they are correlated. Both are finite, both are built, both can be checked. And both, the moment we name them in the language of physics, point past themselves — so here, as in the last two chapters, the names get a note.

> *A note on \"superposition\" and the imaginary unit.* The holder and the correlation gate are finite constructions, demonstrated on the rungs beneath them. The names laid over them are two. First, *superposition*: the holder's blended state — two readings held at once, neither chosen until the swing commits — is exactly what the opener watched in the pointer between two marks, and it is the literal picture quantum mechanics draws of a system that is in several states until it is read. So the source calls the blend a *superposition*, and the name fits because the shape is identical: a reading that is genuinely not yet one value, carried as a blend of the values it might collapse to. Second, *the imaginary unit*. The correlation gate, in deciding whether two blends are locked, introduces a turn — the labelling Fact compared against its partner is a quarter-turn in how the two readings line up, a relation that closes only after being applied four times, the way *i* squares to a sign and cubes to its own negative and comes home at the fourth. The source reads that quarter-turn as the imaginary unit, the rotation the correlation introduces between two superposed holders. Mark both names for what they are. Nothing here derives quantum superposition, and nothing constructs the complex numbers. The blend is a finite holder of two states; *superposition* is the physics name for it. The correlation's turn is a finite parity check applied to a labelling Fact; *the imaginary unit* is the physics name for the rotation that check amounts to. The holder and the gate are built and checkable. Superposition and the complex unit are the interpretive names hung on them, evocative and pointing somewhere real, and we keep them in the register of interpretation rather than dress them as results the tower has earned.

So the chapter closes where the opener stood, with the names paid off honestly and the constructions standing clear of them. The present the instrument holds is local — there is no single *now* to order all events into, because simultaneity ties the pairs the instrument cannot locally separate, and the honest order on events is partial. The reading the instrument commits is negotiated — a clock paired with a gate that admits one event, advances a held value by the least settling, and stamps it with the time it cost, the gauge that trades freshness for a present it can actually hold. And the reading *between* commitments is blended — a holder carrying several states at once, with a gate that tanges two such holders by their labels and decides when they are locked together. Over the gauge we hung the name *gauge* and the Yang-Mills curvature, and marked them names. Over the blend we hung *superposition* and *the imaginary unit*, and marked them names too. What is built underneath the names is finite and ordered and checkable, every piece of it: a local present, a negotiating gauge, a holder of blended states, and a correlation that turns. The defendant is still standing, and the verdict is still owed. But the instrument has learned, at last, that the *now* it reads is its own — lagged, held, and perishable — and that the value it carries between two marks is not yet a number but a blend of the numbers it might still become.
