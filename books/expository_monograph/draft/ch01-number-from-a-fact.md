# Chapter 1 — Number from a Fact

This is the arraignment. The ticket is a single sheet, and on it there is a sentence. The sentence reads: *the defendant was speeding.* It is not a question and not an estimate. It is a claim, and the officer has signed it. Beneath the signature, in a box ruled for the purpose, is a reading: **83**. The two are stapled together — the claim and the number — and the staple is the whole point. You are not handed a sentence to weigh and, separately, a measurement to admire. You are handed one object: a proposition with a receipt clipped to it. That object has a name we already know. It is a *Fact*: a claim that arrives carrying its own decision, a sentence that does not merely assert but settles.

The needle did the settling. Somewhere behind the dashboard a thin metal pointer swung across a printed arc and came to rest, and where it rested was read off and copied into the box. That interior reading — the needle against its scale, before anyone wrote anything down — is the instrument in focus here. Everything downstream is a transcription of it. The number 83 is what the needle said; the sentence is what the officer made of what the needle said; the staple is the promise that the two belong to each other.

But notice what the staple does *not* do. It pairs a number with a claim. It does not, by itself, earn the claim. A needle reading 83 is a fact about a needle. *Guilty* is a verdict about a person. Between them lies a gap that no amount of stapling closes: the limit is posted as 65, and somewhere in the machinery a comparison must be made — is 83 past 65? — and that comparison has to be *built*, not assumed. The receipt certifies that a reading happened. It does not certify that the reading was past a threshold, nor that being past the threshold means what the sentence says it means. A number has been recorded. A verdict has not yet been earned.

That residue — the daylight between *a needle reading 83* and *guilty* — is what this chapter is about, and it is smaller and stranger than it looks. To close it we will need an order on numbers: a way to say one reading is past another. To get an order we will need numbers. And to get numbers we will need only the Fact we already have in hand. We will build the whole apparatus out of the single stapled object on the ticket, and we will find, when we are done, that the order we needed was hiding inside the sign of the number the entire time. Start with the receipt. Everything else is transcription.

## 1.1 — Fact and truth

Take the Fact off the ticket and apart, gently, into its two pieces. There is a claim — call it the *truth* of the Fact, the proposition it asserts. And there is the receipt: a procedure that, handed the claim, returns a verdict for it. The verdict is not a separate belief we hold about the claim; it travels *with* the claim, stapled on, so that to possess the Fact is already to possess its settlement. The canonical example, the one everything here is built from, is the claim that **true equals true**, with the obvious receipt: it does, plainly, and the procedure that checks this returns *settled* without hesitation. That is the simplest Fact there is. It asserts the least and earns its keep immediately.

Make the two pieces sharp, because the whole construction leans on their being genuinely two. The claim alone is inert: a sentence can be written down whether or not anyone can settle it, and a great many can be written that no procedure settles at all. What the receipt adds is not a second sentence but a *capability* — a method that, presented with the claim, halts and returns one of two outcomes, *settled* or *refuted*. A Fact is the marriage of the two: a claim wedded to a method that decides it. A Fact is not \"a true thing\"; it is a thing whose truth-value can be produced on demand. Strip the receipt and you have a proposition, which may dangle forever. Keep the receipt and you have something that pays out. This is why the order we are eventually after is reachable at all — every comparison we build will, at bottom, consult receipts, and receipts are precisely the things guaranteed to answer.

We could stop with one Fact, but the construction needs to see more than one kind, and here the source is precise about something easy to miss. When we let the claim inside a Fact be an equation between truth-values, three symbols appear, not two. We can assert **true = true**. We can assert **false = false**. And we can assert **true = false**. The first two are settled affirmatively; the third is settled negatively — its receipt returns *refuted* — but it is a perfectly good Fact, a claim that carries its decision, and the decision happens to be *no*. So the raw material is three: two agreements and one disagreement, three sentences each with its receipt clipped on.

Three is one too many for a binary digit, and the resolution is the first genuinely structural move in the book. Read the two agreements — **true = true** and **false = false** — not as two facts about two different truth-values but as *the same event seen on two clocks*. Picture a quantity and its complement running together: when one hand reads *true* the complementary hand reads *false*, and the equation **true = true** on the first clock is the very same cadence as **false = false** on the second. They agree about agreement. They are one beat, not two. The genuine distinction is not among the three symbols but between two cadences: the cadence where the two clocks *match* — call it *same* — and the cadence where they *fail to match*, which is exactly **true = false** — call it *different*. The complement reading collapses three symbols onto a single binary axis: **same versus different**.

This is worth dwelling on because it is the hinge for everything that follows, and it is easy to walk past as a triviality. We did not *choose* a binary truth-system and then notice it had two values. We started with three honest symbols — three equations, each with its receipt — and a complement reading folded the two affirmative symbols into one, leaving a clean two-way distinction: *same* or *different*. The fold is not a loss of information; it is a change of question. We stop asking *which truth-value* and start asking *do the two clocks agree*, and that question has exactly two answers. Hold onto *same* and *different*. They are about to stop being a fact about clocks and start being the sign of a number.

Return to the ticket for a moment, because the abstraction has a concrete shadow. The needle, at rest, is in one of two postures relative to anything you care to compare it against: it agrees, or it disagrees. *Same* and *different* are not yet *more* and *less* — we have not built order, only distinction — but they are the seed of it. The receipt on the ticket says a reading happened. The *same/different* distinction is the first thing a reading can be *about*. Everything in 1.2 is the work of turning a distinction into a direction.

## 1.2 — Number as signed binary

The box on the ticket holds 83. Pull its digits apart and you see the shape of every number in this construction. A number, here, is a stack of digits, and it is built from the top down — big-endian, most significant first, the way you read a printed numeral left to right rather than the way an adding machine carries from the right. There are two ways to extend a number. You can plant a single digit and stop — a number with nothing beneath it, a base case. Or you can take a number already built and put another digit on top of it, growing the stack by one. That is the entire grammar: a number is either a lone digit or a digit sitting on a shorter number. Read the most significant digit first and you are reading the number the way the page is written.

Now the move that makes this more than bookkeeping. Each digit does not merely hold a bit; it *carries a Fact*, and the Fact it carries is read as the digit's **sign**. This is why we spent 1.1 collapsing three symbols to two. The Fact stapled to a digit settles to *same* or to *different* — to one of the two cadences of the complement clock — and that settlement is the sign. A digit is not a naked 0 or 1. It is a 0-or-1 *together with a posture*: agreeing or disagreeing, same or different. The number, then, is a stack of signed digits, and its meaning will turn out to depend on how the signs at corresponding positions relate to one another. The sign is not decoration on the digit. The sign is the thing the order will read.

So consider the order. We want to decide, of two numbers, whether one is *at most* the other — the very comparison the ticket needed and could not assume. Begin at the base: a number that is nothing but a lone bottom digit is taken to be *at most* anything at all. The bottom is the floor; it sits under everything. And a number that has genuine structure above the floor is *not* at most the bare floor — having more than nothing puts you above nothing. Those two clauses fix the extremes. The whole content of the order lives in the remaining case, where two numbers each have a top digit and a shorter number beneath, and we must compare them position by position. Here is where the signs do their work, and here is the single rule that drives the entire book.

Compare the signs of the two top digits. They are each *same* or *different*; so either the two signs **agree** with each other, or they **disagree**. This is *truth-parity*: not the value of either sign in isolation, but whether the two signs match. And the parity selects which of two opposite orders we use on the numbers underneath.

**When the signs agree, the order counts by wholes.** Agreement means the two digits are pulling the same way, and pulling the same way means the comparison passes straight through to the shorter numbers beneath, in the same direction: the first number is at most the second exactly when the part beneath the first is at most the part beneath the second. Stack agreeing signs all the way down and you are climbing 1, 2, 3, and on — each added digit, agreeing with its neighbor, makes the number *larger*. This is the covariant case: the order on the whole runs *with* the order on the part. Counting up by ones lives here, in the agreement of signs.

**When the signs disagree, the order counts by parts.** Disagreement flips the comparison: the first number is at most the second exactly when the part beneath the *second* is at most the part beneath the *first* — the inequality underneath turns over. Now stacking digits does not climb toward larger wholes; it descends through smaller parts: a half, then a third, then a quarter, the sequence pressing *down* toward zero. This is the contravariant case: the order on the whole runs *against* the order on the part. To name the n-th part rather than the n-th whole, you let the signs disagree, and the same machinery that climbed by ones now divides toward nothing.

Foreground this, because it is the engine and there is only one of it. We did not write two number systems, one for whole numbers that grow and one for fractional parts that shrink. We wrote *one* rule — compare the two signs; if they match, count with the order beneath, if they clash, count against it — and the single rule produces both behaviors out of a single parity check. Wholes and parts are not two kinds of number. They are one kind of number read under two parities of sign. The covariant ladder up and the contravariant ladder down are the same ladder, traversed in directions chosen by whether two stapled receipts happen to agree.

It pays to see the rule run once, slowly, on agreeing signs, because the recursion is the whole mechanism. Take two numbers whose top digits carry signs that match. The rule says: the first is at most the second exactly when the shorter number beneath the first is at most the shorter number beneath the second — so we strip the matched top digits and ask the same question one level down. If those next signs also match, we strip again, and again, descending the stacks in lockstep until one of them runs out. The number that reaches its floor first is the smaller one; the floor sits under everything, so a shorter agreeing stack is at most a longer one. That is counting by wholes laid bare: each agreeing digit you add is one more rung climbed, and comparison is just the race to the floor. Disagreeing signs run the identical descent with the inequality turned over at the step where they disagree, so that the longer stack becomes the *smaller* number — counting by parts, the same race read backward. And from *at most* we get *strictly less* for free, in the ordinary way: one number is strictly below another when it is at most the other and the other is not at most it. No new machinery; the strict order is the non-strict order consulted twice and refused once.

The ticket asked one question: is 83 past 65? We can now see what answering it requires. It requires laying the two numbers digit against digit and, at the topmost place where they could differ, reading the parity of their signs — agreement to count one way, disagreement to count the other — and letting that parity decide which number is at most which. The needle's reading is not past the limit because someone declared it so. It is past the limit, or it is not, according to a comparison built entirely out of stapled receipts and the parity between their signs. The order the chapter opened by missing is now in hand. What remains is to ask where the digits — the signed readings themselves — come from. They come from a sensor.

## 1.3 — The carrier as a sensor

The needle was the moving part. We have been handing ourselves signed digits as though they fell from the sky; they do not. Each one is reported by a *carrier* — the moving part of the apparatus, the component that actually produces a reading and presents it for use. A carrier is the mathematical analogue of the needle: not the number it points at, but the mechanism whose state *is* a number-in-progress. To specify a carrier is to specify what such a mechanism reports, and it reports exactly three things.

First, a **sign** — a Fact, settling to *same* or *different*, the posture of the reading. This is the carrier's polarity: which way it is leaning, which cadence of the complement clock it is presently keeping. Second, a **value** — a number, in the precise sense built in 1.2, a stack of signed digits. This is the reading proper: the position the carrier currently holds, expressed in the number system we just constructed, and notably *not* a borrowed counting number from elsewhere but one of our own, grown from the Fact **true = true** rather than from any prior notion of quantity. Third, a **step** — a rule for producing the next reading from the current one. A static reading is a snapshot; a carrier is a process, and the step is what makes it one. Hand the step a reading and it returns the reading that comes after.

> *A note on provenance.* It is tempting to picture the carrier as something delivered to us from outside, supplied ready-made on request for us to inspect. That framing belongs to the earlier machinery of Chapter 00 and the scaffolding around it; it is not part of the mathematics. In the body, a carrier is simply a mathematical sensor, defined by what it reports. Nothing hands it to us. It is a single moving part with three readings bundled into it: a sign, a value, and a step.

Look closely at the step, because it carries a small lesson that will matter later. When the step is handed a reading that is nothing but a bare bottom digit — the floor — it returns a fresh floor stamped with the canonical Fact, the receipt for **true = true**. The process, asked for the successor of nothing, answers with the simplest settled thing there is: ground truth, the affirmation from 1.1 that needs no argument. And when the step is handed a reading with structure above the floor, it returns a reading built from the carrier's own value placed atop the same sign — the mechanism feeds its current value forward into the next position, so that the reading about to be produced is *covered in advance* by the value the carrier already holds. This is the familiar hazard of any counting process: the place where the *current* reading becomes the *next* reading is exactly the seam where an off-by-one error would hide, the fence and its posts. We name it here not to fix it but to mark it: the step is where a sensor turns a held reading into a produced one, and that turning is the act of counting itself.

There is a structural symmetry worth naming between the three components, because it is what makes the carrier a self-contained sensor rather than three loose parts. The sign says *which way*; the value says *how far*; the step says *what next*. Direction, magnitude, and dynamics — the three things one needs to specify any quantity that moves and is read off in motion. And crucially, all three are expressed in the same currency, with no part borrowed from outside the construction. The sign is a Fact, settling to *same* or *different*. The value is a stack of signed digits, each of which carries a Fact of exactly that kind. The step produces values of exactly that kind, stamping fresh readings with the canonical receipt. There is no foreign material anywhere in a carrier: no borrowed number line, no imported notion of quantity, nothing that did not grow from the Fact **true = true**. A carrier reports about numbers using parts that are themselves made of the same stuff numbers are made of, which is what lets the construction close on itself rather than lean on something outside.

So the carrier is a sensor in the full sense. It has a polarity (the sign), it has a present reading (the value), and it has a dynamics (the step) that drives it from one reading to the next. The needle on the ticket reported a single value and fell silent; a carrier reports a value *and the rule for what it would report next*, which is what lets a single mechanism generate a whole sequence of readings rather than one. Out of carriers we will eventually assemble counting itself — but counting requires one thing more, and it is a thing we have so far taken for granted. To count carriers, we must first be able to tell one carrier from another.

## 1.4 — Telling carriers apart

The needle settled on 83, and the ticket recorded 83 rather than 82 or 84. Here is what that recording quietly assumes. To count a collection of carriers — to enumerate them, to say *this one, then this one, then the next* — you must be able to say, of any two, whether they are the *same* carrier or *different* ones. If you cannot tell two carriers apart, you cannot count them; you might count one twice, or two as one, and the enumeration collapses. Counting rests on distinction, and distinction is not free. It must be *built*, and built so that the distinction it draws actually returns a verdict — so that *are these two the same or not?* is a question with a settled answer, not an open one.

This is the work of the verb we have so far only invoked. To **tange** two carriers is to select them apart by a characteristic — to individuate them, to make the difference between them into something a procedure can read off and settle. The construction tanges two carriers apart by attaching to each a *distinguishing label* and then asking whether the labels differ. The label is a characteristic borne by the carrier, a mark that travels with it; and *different label* is a claim that, like every claim worth the name here, comes with its receipt. The difference between two carriers is itself a Fact — a proposition (*these labels are not the same*) stapled to a procedure that decides it. We do not merely hope the carriers differ. We tange them, and the tanging hands back a verdict, the way the affirmed Fact of 1.1 hands back its verdict on **true = true**: claim and decision fastened together.

> *A note on the label.* The internal source draws its distinction by a bookkeeping index that the surrounding machinery happens to track — a tag the scaffolding keeps for its own purposes. That index is an implementation detail, not a mathematical feature of carriers, and dwelling on it would point at apparatus we have agreed not to look at. What matters mathematically is only this: each carrier bears a *distinguishing label*, a characteristic by which it can be individuated, and the difference between two such labels is decidable. Read *label* wherever the temptation arises to read anything more specific. The label is whatever mark makes one carrier tangeable from another.

Notice that tanging is the *opposite* gesture to funging, and the contrast sharpens both. To funge a carrier would be to drop it into the bag of everything sharing its characteristic — to make it interchangeable, to ask not *which one* but *what kind*, to form the pool in which individual identity dissolves. Tanging refuses the pool. It holds a carrier up against another and insists on the characteristic that separates them, the label that this one has and that one lacks. Where funging asks *are these the same kind?* and answers by pooling, tanging asks *are these the same one?* and answers by distinguishing. This chapter only tanges. Counting needs the second question: you cannot enumerate a bag; you can only enumerate things you have first tanged apart.

And the distinction must *decide*. It is not enough that two carriers happen to differ; the difference must be one a procedure can settle, returning *yes, distinct* or *no, identical* on demand — a decidable difference, in the plain sense that the question has a method and the method terminates with a verdict. This is the same insistence we have carried since the first page: a claim is only a Fact if it arrives with its receipt, and *these two carriers are distinct* is exactly such a claim. To tange is to manufacture that receipt — to turn a vague sense that two things are not the same into a settled, individuating, decidable mark. The sensor that could only move can now register that it has moved *to something new*, because the new reading is tanged from the old.

With that, step back to the courtroom and count what has actually been built. We have a Fact: a claim with its decision stapled on, the **true = true** that everything was grown from. We have, out of the three truth-symbols and the complement clock, a clean *same/different* sign. We have numbers built as stacks of signed digits, ordered by a single rule of truth-parity that counts by wholes when signs agree and by parts when they disagree. We have carriers — sensors that report a sign, a value, and a step. And now we have the ability to tange one carrier from another, the decidable distinction without which none of the readings could be counted. The needle reported 83, and 83 is genuinely 83, distinguishable from its neighbors: a finished, honest number, built from a Fact and nothing else — no quantity assumed, no magnitude borrowed from outside, only claims that carry their own verdicts, stacked and signed and told apart.

What has *not* been built is the verdict on the ticket. The residue from the opener stands exactly where it stood: a needle reading 83 is a finished, honest number, but *guilty* is a separate Fact, and we have not stapled it on. The reading is earned; the conviction is owed. We have taught the instrument to count, and to know that it has counted, and to say which count is larger — and that is the whole of the first half of a ticket. The defendant is still standing. The number waits in its box, true to itself, and altogether silent on the question of the crime.
