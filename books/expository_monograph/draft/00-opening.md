# Preface — What this book explains

This book explains one Lean development. From a single primitive it builds measurement, then number, then a calculus of variations, and finally a small finite gauge theory; and once that scaffold stands, it reads physical content back out of the construction — an object the source calls an electron, a distinction between matter and antimatter, a quantity it names boundary radiation. The development is concrete: it lives in source files and compiles, or is meant to. Our task is to say what it does, and to argue that it is mathematically coherent.

The single primitive is worth naming at the outset, because everything rests on it. The development never works with a bare proposition. It works with a proposition bundled together with a procedure that decides it — a claim that arrives with the means of checking the claim attached. We will call such a bundled object a *Fact*. A Fact is not "something true"; it is "something whose truth-value can be computed on demand." That distinction is the hinge of the entire construction. Counting, arithmetic, the variational machinery, and the gauge theory are all assembled by composing Facts, never by appealing to abstract truth.

The stance of this book is the part most likely to be misread, so we state it flatly. A proof assistant has an elaborator: the component that takes a written declaration and decides whether it goes through. We treat that elaborator as a physical measuring instrument. When the source speaks of *measuring how true `true = true` is*, it is not making a joke about tautology. It is asking an empirical question about a real device: given this exact claim, what does the elaborator do, how much does deciding it cost, and what does deciding leave behind? An assertion that costs nothing and one that exhausts the machine are both true in the abstract, but they are not the same measurement. The development is, on this reading, a long sequence of such measurements, arranged so the later ones depend on records the earlier ones leave.

We take the construction seriously as mathematics. That carries two commitments. The first is that we report what is there, not what one might hope is there: where the code proves something, we say what was proved and what it was proved from; where it assumes something, we say so. The second commitment is a discipline of labeling, and it runs through every chapter. Three kinds of claim look alike in print and must never be conflated:

- A **definition** stipulates a meaning. It cannot be false; it can only be more or less useful. When the development declares what its "electron" is, that is a definition, and no experiment can refute it.
- A **theorem** is a consequence that the elaborator has checked. It is true relative to the axioms it leans on, and part of honesty is naming those rather than hiding them. A theorem about a finite predicate is a theorem about that predicate and nothing larger.
- A **modeling choice** is a bridge from the formal object to the physical word stapled onto it. To call a defined finite quantity an "electron," or a defined residue "radiation," is interpretation: the object is what the theorems pin down; the physical name is not. This is the book's most interesting move and its least protected: it earns no certainty from the machine, and its load-bearing strength is exactly that of the analogy, no more.

When a chapter heading promises an electron or a field equation, the body will say plainly which part is definition, which is theorem, and which is the physical reading laid over the top — the modest, checked statement kept apart from the sweeping name.

A word on what we will *not* do. We do not reproduce the source's running comedy — the contest with the compiler, the meter that measures nonsense, the menagerie of nicknames. Each of those is a figure for a precise technical object, and we translate the figure rather than retell it. The reader owed an explanation here is a mathematician or a physicist, and the explanation should read as exposition, not performance.

One narrative does survive, because it carries the book's central intuition better than any abstract preamble could. A driver is pulled over and accused of a specific speed, and the rest of the book is the trial of that number. No specific result is asserted here. That is deliberate. We begin on the shoulder of a dark road, before any line of construction is opened.

# Prologue — The Stop

It is late, the road is empty, and a car is brought to the shoulder by a light in the mirror. The officer walks up and states a proposition: *83 in a 65*. Note the shape of what has just happened. A number has been asserted, out loud, by an authority, with consequences attached. Nothing has yet been shown. The whole of what follows — the entire proceeding this night sets in motion — is the attempt to make that asserted number survive scrutiny, or to fail.

A measurement, before it is anything else, is a *decision*. The officer did not contemplate the car's speed; an instrument compared it against a threshold and returned a verdict. Sixty-five is the line; the reading either clears it or does not. This is the first thing to hold onto, because it is the shape of every claim in this book: not a quantity floating free, but a question put to a device that comes back with a checkable answer. A claim that arrives with such a built-in means of checking — a receipt one can present and re-present — is what we keep calling a Fact. The ticket in the officer's hand is meant to be exactly that: a proposition, *the defendant was speeding*, with the reading stapled to it as proof.

Three witnesses will be called, and all three claim to report the same single quantity — the car's speed. They will not look alike, and the difference between them is the architecture of everything ahead. None of the witnesses owns that speed; each only reads it, from where it stands.

The **speedometer** is the *interior* reading. It is the apparatus measuring itself: the car's own drivetrain, counting its own turning, certifying its own motion from the inside. It needs no outside reference and trusts none. Whatever it can swear to, it swears to about itself — and the limit of an instrument that can testify only about its interior will matter later. The speedometer carries the opening stretch of the book, where the construction measures itself.

The **radar gun** is the *sign and boundary* reading. It does not count anything inside the car. It stands at the roadside and bounces a signal off the moving body, and what it recovers is a shift — a Doppler sign that says *approaching* or *receding* before it says how fast. It is a reading taken at a boundary, between an observer on one side and a thing crossing on the other; its meaning depends on which way the crossing goes, and on the calibration fixing its zero. Direction first, magnitude second. The radar gun carries the middle of the book, where signs and boundaries do the work.

The **GPS** in the driver's phone is the *outside and frame* reading. It ignores the car's internals and ignores the roadside entirely. It fixes the vehicle against a global frame — positions over time, referred to satellites overhead, with the corrections moving clocks demand — a coordinate system that contains the car rather than rides in it. It is the view from no particular instrument on the scene, the speed as the whole world would tabulate it. The GPS carries the late chapters, where the frame itself becomes the subject.

Interior, boundary, frame. The book has three reading-modes, and these are they, in that exact correspondence; the three witnesses are chosen so the reader meets the modes as testimony before machinery. That all three are reading *one* quantity is the claim the trial will test hardest. We only foreshadow it here: there is a single underlying invariant that all three are each approximating from their own side. What that invariant turns out to be — and why three such different instruments are entitled to call it the same thing — is settled far downstream, and we will not pretend to settle it now.

The court, finally, accepts a small number of things without proof. We flag it because so much rests on so little: strip the *central charge* — the one the whole construction is built toward — to its foundation, and it rests on just *two principles accepted without proof*. A few further principles enter along the way; we name each where it appears. Everything else is supposed to be earned.

What does the stop leave behind? A residue, and it is double. There is the technical residue — measurement minus story, what is left when the reading is separated from the narrative around it, the part independent of who tells it. And there is the legal residue the trial itself deposits: doubt, conviction, precedent, the record that outlasts the night. A number has been asserted. From here on, the book is the work of finding out what of it remains.
