# Chapter 7 — Arithmetic, linear algebra, Galerkin, splines

## The trip reconstructed

The trial is over for the moment, and the clerk is doing arithmetic. Spread on the table is not a single reading but the whole drive: a strip of figures the instrument logged as the car came up the road, **79**, **81**, **84**, **83**, **85**, one after another, each the output of a swing or a tick, each honest and each alone. No one of them is the speeding. The ticket charges a course of conduct — so much road covered in so much time — and to make that charge the clerk has to turn the pile of separate readings back into the drive that produced them. That is reconstruction, and it is ordinary arithmetic done with care.

Two operations do the bulk of it, and you already know them from the road. The first is addition. The car covered a stretch, and then another, and then another; to get the distance you lay the stretches end to end and *accumulate* them — each new piece added onto the running total, the odometer's whole turns gathered into a single length. The second is multiplication. Over any short interval where the needle held nearly steady, the distance is the rate times the time: hold 83 for a few seconds and the road that goes by is the product of the two. Add up the products, interval by interval, and you have rebuilt the trip — the total road as a sum of rate-times-time pieces, distance recovered from a strip of speeds and a clock.

But the strip is jittery. The readings do not lie on a smooth curve; they jump — 84 down to 83 and back to 85 — because each is a separate settling of the needle, each carrying the floor's worth of noise the earlier chapters mapped, and the car did not actually lurch the way the figures do. The real drive was smoother than its record. So the clerk does one more thing, the thing a trip computer does silently every time it draws a speed-versus-time trace: it *smooths*. It threads a curve through the jittery marks — not hitting each one dead on, but passing near them, bending only as much as it must, so that what comes out is a single clean path the noisy readings were scattered around. The lurches average away; the trend survives. The reconstructed trip is that smoothed curve, and the distance is the area beneath it.

This chapter is exactly those three motions, built from the Fact and nothing else: the addition that accumulates, the multiplication that scales, and the smoothing that turns a pile of readings into a path. It builds them in four movements — values and their addition; multiplication as scaling, with the sign-flip and orthogonality it brings; the linear algebra underneath, a basis and a null space and a small machine that jumps and calls; and the smoothing made precise, polynomials fitted without a derivative over a single finite frame, and the spline built as a first and a second variation. The strip of figures is on the table. The work of the chapter is to add it up, scale it, and smooth it into the drive it came from.

## 7.1 — Values and addition

Begin with a value, because a value is what every reading on the strip already is and what every operation in this chapter consumes and returns. A value, in the currency the book has used since the first page, is a quantity that carries its Fact — a magnitude with a settled direction, the thing the needle resolves and the readout posts. It is not a result sitting at the end of a computation; it is a thing built to survive being carried *through* one. The strip's figures are values: each is a magnitude with a sign the instrument settled, and the whole of this section is about laying them together.

Laying them together is **addition**, and the construction builds a sum the way it built the area in Chapter 4 — not as a label slapped on two figures, but as an accumulation grown tick by tick. A sum is written in the grammar the book has used throughout: a base case and a way of extending it. At the base sits a *zero* — an origin, a Fact paired with the accumulated quantity standing behind it, the running total before anything has been laid down, the floor the accumulation starts from. From there the sum grows by *adding*: each step takes a Fact together with two quantities — a piece and the place it lands — and stacks them onto the sum already built, so that the new total is the old total with one more increment of area laid on top. A sum is therefore a stack of laid-down pieces, read from the new end, each increment gathered onto what came before. This is the accumulated area exactly: distance recovered not by one grand stroke but by adding stretch onto stretch, the odometer's turns summed into a length.

These sums come ordered, and the order is the one engine the whole book runs on. To compare two sums the construction reads the parity of their Facts and lets the parity decide which way the comparison runs. Two origins compare by their quantities under the truth of their Facts — both settled the same way, the comparison passes through to the totals beneath and counts forward by wholes. An origin sits beneath an accumulated sum whenever the sum's Fact is true: the coarse, obvious order of *how much has been laid down*, nothing-yet at the floor and a real accumulation above it. And two accumulated sums compare by comparing the pieces inside them under the parity of their Facts — agree, and the order runs covariantly with the parts beneath, total against total in the natural direction; disagree, and it turns over and runs contravariantly, against them, resolving toward parts. Agree resolves toward wholes, disagree toward parts: the same truth-parity that signed the very first number, now ordering accumulated areas. The construction is candid, as ever, that this order decides what it must and leaves untouched what it has no ground to decide; what addition needs from the order — a floor, an accumulation above it, and the parity rule between two accumulations — it has.

There is one more thing addition forces on us, and the strip makes it plain: sometimes one value simply swamps another. Lay a long fast stretch beside a brief crawl and the crawl contributes nothing the total can feel — the big piece **overwhelms** the small one, and the sum is, to any resolution the instrument has, just the big piece over again. The construction builds this as a definite relation, a test of when one value dominates another. Two origins can dominate one another only when their underlying Facts agree — same direction, and then the larger quantity carries it. An origin can never dominate an accumulated sum: a bare floor cannot overwhelm a timeline that has actually moved on, so the floor always yields. An accumulated sum always dominates a bare origin, provided its Fact is true — the timeline that has gathered real area overwhelms the floor it started from. And when two accumulated sums meet, the construction reads their parity once more: agree, and the larger total dominates in the natural direction; disagree, and the order turns over and the domination runs the other way. This is **magnitude** in the operational sense the book needs — not a length imported from outside, but the settled question of which of two values, laid together, the sum will actually register, and which it will swallow whole. With the sum built, ordered, and equipped to say when one piece overwhelms another, the accumulation of the strip is in hand. The distance is the sum; the section that follows turns from laying pieces end to end to scaling one piece by another.

## 7.2 — Multiplication, scaling, and orthogonality

Addition lays values end to end. **Multiplication** does something different in kind: it takes a value and *scales* it — stretches it, grows a length into an area, an area into a volume, the rate over an interval into the road that interval covers. Over any short stretch where the needle held steady, distance is rate times time, and the construction builds that product not as a looked-up table of answers but as a spatial growing: a thing that starts from nothing, expands into a first extent, and then, scaled again, stretches that extent further along.

Build the product in the same grammar. There is an *origin* — a Fact alone, the seed before any extent exists, the product's nothing. There is a *base unit* — a Fact paired with a quantity, the first dimension drawn out of the origin, a single extent standing where there had been only a seed. And there is a *scaled volume* — a Fact together with two quantities and the product beneath, an extent stretched along by another, a bounded box grown from a smaller one. Multiplication is the engine that climbs this ladder. Handed the origin, it seeds the first extent — the seed expands into a base unit, the first tick of length drawn out of nothing. Handed a base unit, it draws the extent into a bounding box — the single dimension becomes a scaled volume bounded by where it started and where the running accumulation has reached. And handed a scaled volume, it stretches the leading edge forward — it keeps the anchor where the extent began and pushes the far edge onward by one more increment of the accumulated sum from 7.1. Multiplication, on this construction, is literally the stretching of an extent's leading edge along the area built in the last section: to multiply is to grow the box by advancing its forward boundary through accumulated time. Rate times time becomes road exactly this way — the rate anchors the near edge, time pushes the far edge forward, and the product is the swept length between.

Now the order on products, and here a new thing appears that addition did not have: the comparison can *flip sign*. The seed sits below every real extent — nothing is at most any positive magnitude. But a base unit can sit below the seed, below nothing, precisely when its parity is inverted — a magnitude carried with the opposite sign falls beneath zero, where a positively-signed one never could. And when two scaled volumes are compared, the parity rule does its familiar double turn: agree, and the boxes grow covariantly, the bigger volume above in the natural direction; disagree, and the geometry inverts — the comparison runs contravariantly, the boxes ordered against each other, the larger magnitude now sitting *below* because its sign has turned the order over. This is the same covariant-contravariant duality that has run every order in the book, but multiplication is where it first shows as an actual sign on a product: a thing that was positive, scaled by an inverted partner, comes out negative, and the order on products records the flip.

> *A note on the "vacuum / antimatter flip."* The sign rule just built is genuine and finite: when two scaled products carry agreeing parity the order runs one way, and when their parity disagrees the order turns over, so that a product can come out below the seed — below the vacuum, with its sign inverted. That is demonstrated arithmetic. The source reads this turning-over as a *vacuum / antimatter flip* — the seed as the vacuum state, the sign-inverted product as antimatter looking back across the vacuum, the contravariant case as matter and antimatter exchanging order. The name points at real physics, and the shape is suggestive: a quantity and its inverted twin, separated by a state that is neither. But be exact about the register. Nothing on this rung derives antimatter or the structure of the physical vacuum; what is built is a finite sign rule on a finite product, deciding when a scaled volume falls below its own seed. *Vacuum* and *antimatter flip* are the physics names hung on that sign rule, kept here in the register of interpretation, not let pass for something the construction has proved.

The last thing multiplication gives us is the case that matters most for the smoothing to come: a product that **cancels to nothing**. Two extents can be laid against each other so that their product contributes zero — they are **orthogonal**, and the construction builds orthogonality as a definite relation. The seed is orthogonal to everything: nothing scaled against anything contributes nothing. Two base units are orthogonal when they occupy *different moments* — extents anchored at distinct ticks do not overlap, and their product collapses. And two scaled volumes are orthogonal when their leading edges fall at different moments, or when their parities clash so completely that the space between them is destroyed — a box stretched one way and a box stretched the inverted way cancel where they meet. This is destructive interference written as arithmetic: not two values that happen to sum to zero, but two extents whose product *is* zero because they share no overlap or because their signs annihilate. Orthogonality is the tool the rest of the chapter leans on. To fit a curve to the strip, the construction will build pieces that are orthogonal to each other — pieces whose products cancel — so that each can be measured without the others interfering, every component read clean against a partner it does not overlap. The product that cancels to nothing is what makes a basis possible, and a basis is the next thing built.

## 7.3 — A basis, a null space, and a little machine

A **basis** is a set of directions out of which every value can be built — the independent extents the orthogonality of 7.2 lets you measure one at a time. Building one is the book's two verbs doing linear algebra. The construction **funges** the directions that contribute *nothing* — the ones that scale to zero — pooling them into a single bag, every blind direction interchangeable with every other because the only thing that matters about them is that they vanish; that pooling is a quotient, and the bag it makes is the **null space**. And it **tanges** the directions it *can* see: it selects each independent extent out by the characteristic that sets it apart — its independence, the fact that it cannot be built from the others — individuating them one at a time. So the basis is built from two complementary moves, and it begins, pointedly, with the funge. In the same grammar as ever: the **null space** is a Fact alone; an *origin direction* is a Fact paired with a product, a first theoretical extent seeded out of the void. And there is a *basis extent* — a Fact together with two products and the basis beneath, an independent direction tanged out by its distinguishing characteristic and stacked onto the directions already standing. A basis is therefore a stack of tanged-out extents grown up out of a funged null space, each new direction laid atop the span built so far.

The order on a basis carries one feature worth naming, because it is the null space doing real work. The null space sits below every real direction — the directions that contribute nothing are at the floor, and a basis can be *excited* up out of them into a real extent. But a real direction falls back into the null space only under inverted parity: a state collapses back into the void exactly when its sign has turned over, the same contravariant flip 7.2 made visible. Two real extents compare under the parity rule once more — agree and they scale covariantly, disagree and the geometry inverts. The null space is not a missing case the construction forgot to fill; it is a built floor, the directions a measurement is blind to, sitting beneath everything it can see.

Now the little machine. To run a basis — to step from one direction to the next, to descend into a sub-computation and come back — the construction builds a small jump-machine: a thing that holds a current direction and, handed an instruction, either steps forward or branches. It does precisely three things. Handed the null space, it stays put — there is no geometry to push off of, no direction to jump from, so the machine holds. Handed an origin direction, it performs an outright **jump**: the current theoretical extent collapses into a fresh basis direction whose tail is the null space — a jump with *no return address*, the timeline ending at the new direction, nothing kept of where it came from. And handed a basis extent, it performs a **call**: an ongoing direction processes a fluctuation and steps forward into the direction beneath it, the tail preserved so the machine can carry on — a step into a sub-computation that returns to its caller. A jump that abandons its origin, a call that keeps it: this is the whole control flow of a little stack machine, built out of the basis and nothing more.

> *A note on "scattering" and "the propagator."* The two motions just built are genuine finite control flow: one instruction discards its return point and lands in a fresh direction with the null space as its tail; the other keeps its return point and steps into the direction beneath, resuming where it left off. That is a jump and a call, demonstrated. The source reads them in the language of particle physics — the jump-without-return as a *scattering* event (a thing collapsing into a new state with no memory of the old, the timeline ending at the interaction) and the call-that-returns as a *propagator* (a virtual loop, a fluctuation processed and resumed, the Feynman line that carries an interaction forward and comes back). The names point at the S-matrix and the diagram. But the register is interpretation: nothing here derives scattering amplitudes or Feynman propagators. What is built is a jump and a call — a control transfer that keeps no return address and one that does. *Scattering* and *the propagator* are the physics names hung on those two finite motions, marked as names. The reading that follows — that these directions form an eigenstructure — is *not* in that register; it is built.

For there is a genuine eigenvector reading here, and it is the point of the machine. The construction supplies a test of when one direction *decodes* another — when running the machine on a direction returns that same direction back, a state the machine leaves fixed. A direction out of the null space carries no signal: the void decodes nothing. But a real direction that the machine carries to itself — same direction in, same direction out, the stepping having found a state it does not move — is exactly an **eigenvector** of the machine: a direction stable under the operation, the place where the stepping settles and stops moving. The construction builds the test case by case, reading two directions against each other through the parity of their Facts, and what it isolates is precisely the fixed directions of the little machine — the eigenstructure of the operation, built honestly as the directions the stepping carries to themselves. A basis grown from a null space, a machine that jumps and calls, and the eigenvectors that machine leaves fixed: that is the linear algebra the smoothing will run on.

## 7.4 — Polynomials, Galerkin, and splines

Now the smoothing itself — the curve threaded through the jittery strip. It is built in three steps: the curves the construction is allowed to use (polynomials), a way of fitting them without ever differentiating (the Galerkin method, over a single finite frame), and the spline that results, built as a first and a second variation. This is the heart of the chapter, and every piece of it is genuine finite mathematics.

Start with **polynomials**, because a polynomial is the simplest curve flexible enough to bend through scattered marks and simple enough to compute with exactly. The construction builds one in the grammar it has used all along, atop the basis of 7.3. There is a *constant* — a Fact alone, a flat curve, the background level the strip is scattered around. There is a *monomial* — a Fact paired with a basis direction, a single pure term, one bend in one direction. And there is a *factor* — a Fact together with two basis directions and the polynomial beneath, terms combined into a richer curve, one bend stacked onto another. A polynomial is a stack of such terms, each a direction out of the basis, ordered by the parity rule exactly as everything before it: constants compare by their Facts, pure terms by their directions, and combined terms turn the order over when their Facts disagree — destructive interference between terms, the same flip 7.2 built, now telling two polynomials apart. The curves are in hand; what remains is to fit one to the strip.

Here is where the construction does something quietly remarkable: it fits the curve **without taking a derivative**. The ordinary way to fit a curve and demand it be smooth is to differentiate — to write down slopes and curvatures and force them to match. But a derivative needs a continuum beneath it, a limit of vanishing differences, and the construction has refused the continuum from page one. So it uses the **Galerkin method** instead, the derivative-free approach the whole tower has been built to allow. The idea is the one orthogonality handed us in 7.2: rather than demand the curve match slopes, demand only that the *error* — the gap between the curve and the readings — be orthogonal to every direction in the basis. If the leftover error cancels against each basis extent, contributes nothing when scaled against any direction the fit is allowed to use, then the curve is the best the basis can do, and no derivative was ever taken. The construction builds this as a single definite motion: a *scale-and-shift* that takes a polynomial and promotes it one step — a constant shifts to a pure term anchored on the null space, a pure term scales to a combined term by anchoring on the current basis direction and jumping the state forward with the little machine of 7.3, and a combined term advances by shifting its interacting direction forward while preserving the history beneath. Each step scales the curve and shifts its anchor along the basis, fitting term by term, the error driven orthogonal to one more direction at every step — Galerkin's derivative-free fitting, built out of scaling and the jump-machine and nothing continuous at all.

And all of this runs in a **single finite frame**. This is the standing constraint the construction never relaxes: there is exactly one working space, a finite truncation of an otherwise unbounded object, and the fit must complete inside it. The construction is candid about what the frame can and cannot do. The frame is singular — there is only the one — and it is only *so big*, yet it can grow enormous in a few steps, which is precisely the danger. So the construction builds a test of when a transform *stays inside the frame*: moving from any interaction down to a constant is finite — the energy has dissipated to the background, the fit has converged. A pure term carried to the exact same pure term is finite — the fit has found a fixed point, an eigenstate of the fitting itself, the arm-waving stopped. Moving between combined terms is finite only if their Facts stay invariant — the strain unchanged across the step; let the Fact flip and the transform jumps the frame, a singularity the frame cannot hold. And injecting energy backward — a grounded constant excited back into a combined term — is disallowed, an infinite injection the finite frame refuses. The fit is the sequence of scale-and-shifts that stays finite: that converges to a constant background or settles on a fixed term, never flipping its strain or re-exciting what has already grounded. The Galerkin fit is exactly this — a derivative-free promotion of the curve, run to convergence inside a single finite frame.

What converges is the **spline** — the smoothed curve itself — and the construction builds it as a first and a second variation, the load-bearing object of the whole chapter. Build it in the grammar one last time. At the base is an *observation* — a Fact alone, a single reading off the strip, the raw mark before any curve is drawn. From there the spline grows two ways, and the two ways are the two variations. The first growth is a **knot** — a reading together with a polynomial and the spline beneath: a point where the curve is pinned and a local polynomial piece is attached, the curve made to pass near that mark and bend there. The knot is the **first variation** — the curve's response to moving one control point, how the path shifts when one reading is nudged, the slope of the fit with respect to the data. Pin a knot, jog the reading, and the curve answers: that answer is the first variation, built as the knot's polynomial piece responding to its observation. The second growth is an **interpolant** — a Fact together with two polynomials and two splines beneath: a piece that joins two neighboring knots into one smooth arc, the left polynomial and the right polynomial blended across the join so the curve does not kink where they meet. The interpolant is the **second variation** — the curvature of the fit, the bending energy, how the *slope itself* changes across the join, the weak second derivative the construction builds without ever differentiating. It is built as a weak boundary condition: the interpolant contains a knot exactly when the knot's polynomial satisfies the matching conditions at both edges of the join, left and right, so the arc is smooth across the knot rather than broken. First variation, the knot — the response to moving a point; second variation, the interpolant — the response of that response, the curvature, the smoothness of the join.

The two are ordered, naturally, and the order is the construction's last use of its one engine. An observation sits at the floor — a raw reading is the least thing, beneath any knot, because a knot is an observation plus the work of pinning a curve to it. A knot sits beneath an interpolant when its polynomial meets the join's boundary conditions — a pinned point is contained by the smooth arc that interpolates through it. And two interpolants compare by descending into their polynomials and the splines on either side, smooth arc against smooth arc, the deeper comparison passing through to the pieces beneath — the convergence of one fitted path toward another, checked piece by piece. The construction even gives the spline its own stepping motion, a *reticulation* that refines the curve: handed a raw observation it pins a first knot with the fitted polynomial; handed a knot it carries the polynomial forward against the current best guess; handed an interpolant it advances the join, blending the next polynomial piece into the arc. Reticulation is the smoothing run one more pass — the trace refined, the kinks pulled out, the curve drawn nearer the marks without ever touching the continuum.

And there the chapter closes, with the strip turned back into the drive. The separate readings have been **added** — accumulated tick by tick into a distance, a sum that is an area, with one value made to overwhelm another when it must. They have been **multiplied** — each rate scaled against its interval into a swept length, a product that can flip sign across the vacuum and can cancel to nothing when two extents are orthogonal. They have been organized into a **basis** grown from a null space, run by a little machine that jumps and calls and leaves certain directions fixed. And they have been **smoothed** — fitted by the derivative-free Galerkin method inside a single finite frame, into a spline whose knots are the first variation and whose interpolants are the second, the curvature of the fit built without a single derivative taken. Over the product's sign-flip we hung the names *vacuum* and *antimatter*, and over the machine's two motions *scattering* and *the propagator*, and marked all four as names. Everything else is built: a sum, a product, an orthogonality, a basis, a null space, an eigenvector, a polynomial, a Galerkin fit, and a spline with its first and second variation. That second variation — the curvature, the bending energy of the fitted path — is not a flourish. It is the seed of the single invariant the book is climbing toward, the one quantity a later chapter will show the whole apparatus is built to hold fixed. For now it is enough that the trip is reconstructed: the pile of jittery readings is a smoothed path, the distance is the area beneath it, and every piece of the reconstruction was grown from a claim that carries its own decision and nothing else.
