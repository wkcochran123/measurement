# Chapter 9 — Stationarity and the first variation

There is one true speed. The trial has now heard from three witnesses, and each has been reading all along. The needle on the dashboard counted the drivetrain from inside the car and swore to its own turning. The radar at the roadside read a shift off the moving body, a sign before a magnitude. The GPS box ignored both and fixed the car against a frame of satellites overhead. Three instruments, three vantages — interior, boundary, frame — and from the arraignment onward the prosecution has insisted they are all reading the *same* number. Part I closed on why that claim is hard: an instrument can only swear to itself, and none of these three can step outside its own reading to confirm it agrees with the others. Part II is the work of getting the outside reading Part I could not supply — and it begins inward before it turns outward. Before we can read more than the interior swears to, we must know what the interior instruments are all reading. The claim Part II will make good is that they read one quantity.

The way in is to notice what each witness is actually doing. The car drove some particular path down the road — a definite drive, the thing that happened. No instrument sees that path bare. Each sees a *variation* of it: the path slightly nudged, a reading taken a hair off the true drive and reported as though it were the drive itself. The needle lags and overshoots; the radar samples a fraction of a second late; the GPS interpolates between fixes. Every reading is the true path plus a small wiggle. And here is the fact that organizes everything ahead: the path the car actually drove is the one where those wiggles *cost nothing to first order*. Nudge the true drive a little, this way or that, and the quantity each instrument reads does not change. The true path sits at the bottom of a basin, flat underfoot, and small steps off it are free.

That is what it means for a path to be *stationary*, and it is the whole of why the three witnesses can agree. They disagree in their wiggles; each wiggles in its own way. But they are all wiggling around the *same* stationary path, and at a stationary path the wiggle does not register. The one true speed is not any instrument's reading. It is the value the action takes at the path where every reading's small error is, to first order, no error at all. The three witnesses are three small variations around one stationary drive, scattered around it the way the jittery strip of Chapter 7 was scattered around the smooth drive beneath it — and the number they converge on is the number that is flat there.

This chapter builds that idea from nothing borrowed. We construct a finite path, a finite action that values it, and the stationary paths where the action is flat; then we build the *first variation* — the derivative of the action along a wiggle — as a plain composed difference, and show it vanishes precisely at the stationary paths. The construction is clean, finite, and assumes nothing it cannot check: no continuum, no limit, no derivative taken in anger. It is the analytic spine on which the rest of Part II hangs. There is one true speed. It sits at the stationary path, and this chapter locates it.

## 9.1 — The finite gauge action and stationary paths

Start with the path, stripped to the least thing that can carry the argument. A drive down the road, made finite and checkable, is three things: where it began, where it ended, and the one waypoint in between the car passed through on the way. Call these the *source*, the *target*, and the *slip* — the source the position a reading is taken *from*, the target where it arrives, the slip the single midpoint the drive routes through. The whole path is fixed by those three marks. This is deliberately the smallest object that can carry a variation at all: a beginning and an end held rigid, and one interior point free to move — the middle-point picture Part I made admissible, pared to its bones so the variational argument can run with no continuum underneath. The drive that happened pins all three; the drives we compare against it keep the source and target and slide only the slip. Everything in this chapter is the study of what happens when you move that one middle point and ask what it costs.

Now value the path. An *action* is a functional — a rule that hands each path a single number, its cost — and the action we build values a path from one primitive: a *segment cost*, a rule that, given any two marks, returns the cost of the single leg between them. From the segment cost the action reads the path in two different ways, and the difference between the two readings is the engine of the chapter.

The first reading is *direct*. It ignores the middle point and charges the path the cost of the one leg from source straight to target — the drive as though no waypoint existed, the road covered in one stroke. The second reading is *composed*. It honors the slip: it charges the leg from source to slip, then the leg from slip to target, and adds the two — the path's cost taken *through* its middle point, leg by leg, the way the drive was actually built. This is the same step-by-step composition the carrier has performed since the first chapter, where a held reading becomes a produced one one segment at a time, and the accumulation Chapter 7 built, cut down to two legs. Direct is the path read whole; composed is the path read through its parts.

When the two readings agree — when the direct cost equals the composed cost — the path *admits* its middle point. The waypoint is honest: routing through it costs exactly what going straight costs, so the slip sits on the real drive rather than padding the bill, a faithful subdivision not a detour. This is the truth-parity engine of Part I returned at the level of paths: two readings of one trip, and we ask whether they match — the whole agreeing with the sum of its parts. Where they agree the middle point may stand; where they disagree the slip is an excursion the whole trip would not have taken. Admissibility is the coarse, obvious thing — the midpoint lies on the drive, read off by comparing two costs and nothing more. We will need it, but it is not yet the condition the chapter is about: a path can admit its midpoint and still sit on a slope.

The condition the chapter is about is flatness, and it lives in the composed reading alone. Hold the source and target fixed and slide the slip to any other mark — any alternate waypoint at all, sweeping the middle across every place it could have fallen. Each choice gives a varied path with its own cost. A path is *stationary* when its own composed cost is the least of all of them: no alternate waypoint does better, the path sits at a minimum, every neighboring drive costs at least as much. The slip the drive chose is the cheapest middle point there is — the genuine drive the three instruments are reading off. That is stationarity in its plain form, a path at the floor of the basin with nowhere lower to step.

There is a stronger condition standing just behind it, and the distinction is the careful heart of this section, because the book will later lean on the stronger one. A path is *flat* when sliding the slip does not change the composed cost *at all* — not merely fails to lower it, but fails to move it in either direction, every reroute costing precisely the same. Flatness says the composed action *cannot detect the slip coordinate*: put the middle point wherever you like and the cost reads the same. Where stationarity says *no alternate is cheaper*, flatness says *no alternate is different*. Flatness is plainly the stronger claim, and it implies the weaker one at once: if every alternate slip leaves the cost exactly equal, then in particular none lowers it, so a flat path is a stationary path. The converse fails: a true minimum can curve upward around itself without being level. So the construction names both. A *reducible slip* admits its middle point and is stationary there — the waypoint honest *and* sitting at a minimum. A *closed, flat* drive admits its middle point and is flat there — honest *and* level. The closed-flat condition is the stronger, and it hands its strength straight through: it gives admissibility forward and turns flatness into stationarity along the way, so closed-flat implies reducible-slip. The residue results downstream will demand the stronger thing, and the construction marks now that it asks for it.

The construction *tanges* the stationary paths out of the whole field of paths, and it is worth saying precisely by what characteristic. To tange is to individuate by a deciding trait — to hold each candidate against a single question and keep apart exactly those that answer it. The question here is whether the action is flat at this path: does sliding the slip leave the composed cost unmoved? Every path is asked it; the stationary ones answer yes and are separated out, the rest held back. We do not pool the paths and ask only what kind they are. We hold each against the flatness test and read a decided verdict — stationary or not — the way the very first gate held one carrier against another and read *distinct* or *identical*. The stationary paths are not a vague neighborhood of good drives; they are the precise cut the trait *flat here* picks out, tanged clean from all the rest. That cut is the true drive — and the next section builds the instrument that reads the trait, the first variation, whose vanishing is exactly what *flat here* amounts to.

## 9.2 — The first variation

A path is stationary when no wiggle of the midpoint lowers the cost — a statement about all the varied paths at once, and as it stands expensive to check, since you would sweep the midpoint everywhere and compare every reroute. The first variation makes the check local. It is the leading account of how the cost responds to one wiggle, built so stationarity reduces to a single vanishing, and built — the discipline the whole book has kept — without ever taking a derivative in the continuum sense, because there is no continuum here.

Start with the wiggle, made into an object. A *variation* is nothing more than a choice of alternate slip — the same source, the same target, a different midpoint — the move carrying a path to one of its varied neighbors. Apply a variation and the path is rerouted through the new midpoint; apply the *identity* variation, the one carrying the slip already there, and the path comes back unchanged, the wiggle of zero size that moves nothing. The source and target stay pinned and only the interior point moves. Each variation is one direction you could nudge the drive, and the question the first variation answers is: nudge it this way, and to leading order, what happens to the cost?

Build the answer the way the cost was built — in two pieces, one per leg, then add — because the trip has two legs and a midpoint wiggle touches both. Move the slip, and the first leg changes: its cost was source-to-old-slip, now source-to-new-slip, and the *left variation* is exactly that change, the new first leg's cost minus the old, carried as a signed difference so it records an increase or a decrease honestly. The second leg changes too — old-slip-to-target becomes new-slip-to-target — and the *right variation* is the new second leg minus the old, the same signed difference for the leg into the target. The first variation is the two added together: how much the first leg's price moved, plus how much the second leg's, when the midpoint slid from where it was to where the wiggle put it. It is a signed quantity — a leg can get cheaper or dearer, so each piece can run either way — and the construction tracks the sign honestly, the way the order on products in Chapter 7 first had to record a flip. The leading account of the cost's response is this composed difference of two legs, assembled from the two pieces the path is made of.

Now the fact that makes the first variation a genuine derivative and not merely a label. Compute the first variation of a wiggle and you get *precisely* the composed value of the varied path minus the composed value of the original — the composed difference. The two constructions coincide identically: one assembles the change locally, leg by leg, while the other subtracts the whole composed cost of the original from that of the varied path in one stroke, and they are the same number every time, for every path and every variation, because the composed value is the first leg plus the second, and the change in a sum is the sum of the changes. There is no error in this accounting. Set up the comparison a derivative is meant to satisfy: take the varied cost, subtract the original, subtract off the first variation as the predicted linear change, and read what is left — the *remainder*, everything the linear term failed to account for. For an ordinary derivative that leftover is small but nonzero. Here it is not small. It is *zero*, identically, for every variation — the difference of a quantity with itself. The first variation does not approximate the change in the action; on this finite path it *is* the change in the action, the remainder vanishing identically. The leading term is the whole term. That the remainder can be nonzero — that the cost can bend — is the business of the second variation, chapters on; here, at first order, it is flat and exact, and that exactness is what we build on.

With the remainder gone, stationarity collapses to one clean criterion. A path is flat when every wiggle leaves the composed value unchanged — and the change under a wiggle is exactly the first variation, with no remainder — so a path is flat precisely when its first variation vanishes in every direction. To check stationarity you no longer sweep the midpoint everywhere and compare costs; you ask one question of the leading account: is the first variation zero? Where it is, the path is stationary — the true drive, the one the instruments read. Where it is not, some direction moves the cost, and the path is not the genuine one. *Stationary if and only if the first variation is zero*: the geometric condition of 9.1 — the action flat at the path — and the analytic condition of this section — the derivative zero in every direction — are one condition wearing two faces, because the remainder that would have separated them is identically nothing. The tanging of 9.1, which selected the stationary paths by the trait *flat here*, is now equally their selection by the trait *first variation zero here*.

Here the book's second verb does its work, the complement of the tanging that closed 9.1. Fix a stationary path and look at the directions in which the first variation vanishes — all the wiggles that, applied to it, return zero. Singly they are distinct wiggles, sliding the slip to different alternate marks. But the construction does not keep them apart. It *funges* them: it drops every such direction into one bag and treats them as interchangeable, because the only thing that matters about any of them is the trait they share — *this direction leaves the action stationary*. A wiggle pushing the midpoint one way and costing nothing to first order, and one pushing it another way and also costing nothing — the first variation cannot tell them apart, and does not try to. To funge is exactly this: to drop a thing into the pool of all that bear a characteristic and thereafter care only that it bears the characteristic, surrendering its individuality to the trait. They funge into a single pool, the *kernel* of flat directions, the quotient of all wiggles by the relation the first variation does not see. The construction tanges the stationary paths out of all paths by the trait that their first variation vanishes; and at each such path it funges the flat directions into one kernel, pooled by the very trait the tanging selected. The two verbs are the two faces of one fact: stationarity is a cut on paths and a quotient on directions, the individuating characteristic and the pooled kernel reading the same flatness from opposite sides.

So the first variation is the instrument Part II needed and Part I could not supply from the inside. It is a reading of the drive — finite leg-costs, signed, exact, its remainder identically zero — that returns one number per direction and says, of any path, whether the cost is flat there. Where it vanishes in every direction the path is the true one, and the three witnesses on the road are three small wiggles off that one stationary drive, which is why they converge: each reads a variation the first variation cannot see, scattered around a single speed the stationary path holds fixed. The needle, the radar, and the GPS are reading the kernel from three sides. But the first variation is deliberately the floor and not the ceiling: it reports *where* the action is flat, and pools the flat directions, but says nothing about *how* the action curves once you stand on a stationary path. That curvature — the bending the remainder would carry if it were not zero — is the *second* variation, the single invariant the whole book is climbing toward, built directly atop the instrument this chapter just set down and the kernel this section assembled. That is the next thing the construction builds.

> *A note on the two conditions.* Flat and stationary are kept distinct on purpose. Stationary asks only that no alternate slip be cheaper; flat asks that no alternate slip be different at all. Flat implies stationary and not conversely, and the residue results downstream lean on the stronger, closed-flat form. Where this chapter speaks of stationarity through a vanishing first variation, it is the flat condition doing the work, because only flatness makes the change in cost vanish in *every* direction rather than merely fail to lower it.

> *A note on the remainder.* That the first-order remainder is identically zero, rather than small, is a feature of the finite construction and not a sleight. The first variation was built as the genuine difference of composed costs, so it equals that difference exactly, and the remainder is the difference between a quantity and itself. No limit is taken and no continuum assumed. The derivative here is a finite, checkable object, and its exactness is what lets stationarity and the vanishing first variation be the same condition with no error term between them.
