# Chapter 6 — A stored-program computer from the Fact

## The digital readout

Pull the swinging needle off the dashboard and put a digital readout in its place. The arc and the printed marks are gone; where the pointer used to travel there is a window, and in the window a number stands: **83**. It does not swing. It holds steady, and then, when the car has gained a little, it does not slide up to the next value — it *changes*, all at once, 83 to 84, the old figure replaced by the new with nothing in between. There is no transit across the gap, no smear, no mid-swing blend of the kind the last chapter watched the pointer carry between two marks. The digital readout is never between two values. It shows one, and then it shows another, and the change from the one to the other is a single discrete event.

This is the same reading. The car is the same car, the limit is the same 65, the question on the ticket is unchanged: is the number past the threshold? What has changed is how the number arrives. Behind the analog needle was a cable and a pivot, a continuous mechanism settling under friction toward a rest. Behind the digital window is something else — a process that holds a state and, on the next step, replaces it with the next. The figure in the window is not where a pointer came to rest. It is the *output* of a computation, posted and held until the computation runs again and posts the next.

And here is the claim the chapter makes good on. That little computer behind the window — the thing that holds a state, takes a step, and posts an output — can be built out of nothing but the Fact. Not imported as hardware, not assumed as a given mechanism the way the needle was simply there behind the glass, but *grown*, the way every object in this book has been grown: from a claim that carries its own decision, and the truth-parity that reads two such claims against each other. The digital speedometer is a stored-program computer, and every part of it — a state it can hold, a smallest motion it can make, encodings it carries, a way of turning those encodings into a procedure, and a way of running that procedure to produce a result — is constructible from the single stapled object on the ticket.

Watch the readout once more and you can see the seed of it. At bottom the change in the window is one digit turning over — a single place that is in one condition and then, at the tick, in the other. Two conditions, and the place holds exactly one of them at any instant; it is never both, and never neither. That lone place, flipping between two states it cannot occupy at once, is the binary digit, and the binary digit is where the computer starts. The chapter builds the computer in three movements: first, the binary digit with its exclusion and the digital process — a zero and a tick — that drives it; second, the stored program proper, encodings compiled into a procedure and executed to yield an output; third, a precaution that keeps the machine from naming itself, abstracting the very order relation it runs on so the computer it builds cannot turn around and refer to the computer that built it. The needle is retired. The number in the window is the output of a little computer, and the little computer is about to be assembled from the Fact.

## 6.1 — Equivalence, and the binary that excludes

Start with what it means for two things to be the same, because the binary digit falls out of getting that right. The looser notion of sameness — two things are equal when they simply *are* equal, standing side by side identical at rest — is not the one the construction needs. It needs something with a procedure in it. Two things are equivalent, here, when the *same procedure produces them*: not equal as they sit, but equal in that running the machine arrives at the one exactly as it arrives at the other. Equivalence is equality with a process attached — a sameness that has to be earned by a computation that gets there, rather than read off two objects held up against each other. The distinction matters because everything in this chapter is produced by a running thing. We do not get to inspect two states and declare them the same; we get to run the procedure and see that it reaches them by the same road.

Build the carrier of this equivalence in the grammar the book has used since the first page — a base case and ways of growing it. At bottom there is a bare equivalence: a Fact paired with the accumulated quantity standing behind it, the least thing the procedure can be made to produce at all, sameness in its simplest settled form. From there the construction grows it two ways. It can plant a *zero-like* equivalence — a Fact set atop an equivalence already built, marking a ground condition, the procedure's account of nothing-yet-produced. Or it can plant a *one-like* equivalence — a claim together with two equivalences beneath it, the procedure's account of a first step taken, of something rather than nothing. Zero-like and one-like: the two conditions the procedure can be in, the having-produced-nothing and the having-produced-a-first-thing, each carrying its Fact and the equivalences it was grown from.

These come ordered, by the one rule. To compare two equivalences the construction reads the parity of their Facts. Two bare equivalences compare by their quantities under the truth of their Facts — both settled the same way, and the comparison passes through to the quantities beneath, counting forward by wholes. A bare equivalence sits beneath a one-like one, which is the coarse order of *how far the procedure has gotten*: nothing-produced is the floor, a first step stands above it. And two one-like equivalences compare by comparing the equivalences inside them, under the parity of their own claims — agree, and the order runs covariantly with the parts beneath; disagree, and it turns over and runs against them, resolving toward parts, exactly as the signed digit on the ticket did. The construction is candid that this order is not yet everything one could want — there are equivalences it does not pretend to compare. What it needs from the order, it has: a floor, a step above the floor, and the parity rule deciding the rest.

Now read the two conditions — zero-like and one-like — as the two states of a single place, and the binary digit appears. A binary digit is a slot with exactly two conditions it can be in: the zero state and the one state, nothing-produced and something-produced. And it carries, built into it, a restriction the slot cannot violate. *It holds exactly one of the two at a time.* It is never in both states at once and never in neither; the place is occupied, and singly. This is the **exclusion** — two states, one occupant, a slot that cannot be doubly filled. The exclusion is not laid on from outside as a rule the slot is asked to obey. It is what makes the slot a binary digit rather than something vaguer: a thing that, asked which of its two states it is in, returns one answer, because it is in one state, because the two states exclude each other.

With the slot built and its exclusion fixed, give it motion — make it a *digital process*. A digital process is the slot together with two things: a starting condition and a way to step. The starting condition is the *zero* — a designated equivalence the process holds before it has moved, the figure the readout shows on power-up, the procedure's nothing-yet. And the step is the *tick* — a single discrete advance that takes the slot's current state and returns its next. The tick is the whole motion of the digital process, and it does precisely three things, one for each state it might be handed. Handed a bare equivalence, the tick passes it forward unchanged — the settled quantity is carried across the step intact, nothing produced from nothing. Handed the zero state, the tick produces a one state built on the process's own zero — the place that held nothing now holds a first something, the slot flipping from its zero condition to its one condition. Handed the one state, the tick produces a zero state stamped with the canonical settled Fact — the place that held something returns to nothing, the slot flipping back. Zero to one, one to zero: the tick is exactly the turning-over the digital readout showed, the single place advancing from each of its two states to the other, and nowhere holding both.

That is the digital process, complete and finite: a slot with two excluding states, a zero it rests in, and a tick that flips it. It is the analog needle's discrete twin — where the pointer swung continuously through a smear, this place sits in one state, then in the other, with a single discrete tick between and no transit. Out of the Fact and the parity rule, with no continuum beneath and no mechanism imported, the construction has built the binary digit that the readout's lowest place turns over. Everything the rest of the chapter stacks — encodings, compilation, execution — stacks on this: a slot that excludes, started at zero, advanced by a tick.

> *A note on "Pauli exclusion" and "the neutrino."* The exclusion just built is real and demonstrated: a binary slot holds exactly one of its two states, never both and never neither, and the tick moves it from each to the other. The names laid over it belong to physics. The source reads the slot's restriction — two states, one occupant, a thing that cannot share its place — as the *Pauli exclusion principle*, the rule that two identical particles cannot occupy the same state at once, because the shape is the same: one occupant per slot, doubly-filling forbidden. And it reads the state the tick leaves out — the condition excluded at each flip, the one the slot is *not* in — as *the neutrino*, the left-out, barely-registered partner of the transition. Be exact about the register. Nothing on this rung derives the Pauli principle, and nothing constructs the neutrino. What is built is a finite binary fact: a slot with two mutually excluding states and a tick between them. *Pauli exclusion* is the physics name for the one-occupant restriction; *the neutrino* is the physics name for the excluded state. The exclusion is built and checkable. Pauli and the neutrino are the interpretive names hung on it, pointing at real physics, and we keep them in the register of interpretation rather than let them pass for something the tower has proved.

## 6.2 — Opcodes, compilation, execution

A digital process that only ticks is a clock, not a computer. What makes it a *stored-program* computer is that the procedure it runs is itself data — written down, carried alongside the state, and consulted to decide what the next step does. This rung builds that: a way of encoding instructions, a way of compiling an encoding into a runnable procedure, and a way of executing the procedure to produce an output. All of it grown from the digital process just built, and all of it finite.

Begin with the encodings, the instructions written in the machine's own alphabet. An *encoding* is built in the familiar two-part grammar, with three constructors rather than two, because a stored program needs to name three things. There is a *boot* encoding — a Fact paired with an equivalence, the instruction the machine starts from, the first word of the program. There is a *zero* encoding — a Fact and an equivalence set atop an encoding already written, the instruction that extends a program by one word in its zero register. And there is a *one* encoding — a Fact together with two equivalences and two encodings beneath it, the instruction that extends a program by a word carrying a branch, two sub-programs held at once. These three are the **opcodes**: boot, zero, and one — the start word, the straight extension, and the branching extension. A program is a stack of them, written in equivalences and Facts, exactly the currency the rest of the book is written in. Nothing foreign is admitted into the instruction set; an opcode is a Fact and the equivalences it stands on, no more.

A program written is not yet a program that runs. Between the written encoding and the running machine sits **compilation**, and the construction builds it as a single definite step. To compile is to fasten a written program onto a digital process — to take the stack of opcodes and bind it to the slot, the zero, and the tick of the process built in 6.1, producing a thing that has both a procedure to follow and a machine to follow it on. The compiled process carries three things bound together: the digital process underneath, the source it was compiled from, and a designated opcode — the instruction currently in hand, the word the machine is about to act on. Compilation is the act of binding the written instruction to the running slot. Before it, the program is text; after it, the program is a machine with that text loaded, a slot that knows which opcode it is holding and a tick standing ready to act on it.

Now **execution**, where the loaded program actually does something. Execution takes the opcode currently in hand together with an input encoding, and produces an output encoding — it runs one instruction against one input and returns the result, the next word the machine posts. The construction builds this as a careful matching: it reads the opcode in hand and the input side by side, and the pair decides the output. If the opcode is a boot and the input is a boot, execution consults whether both their Facts are settled and produces a one encoding built on the process's own zero — the start word, fed a start word, branches. If the opcode is a boot and the input is anything else, execution produces a zero encoding carrying the boot's content — the start word, fed something other than a start, extends straight. If the opcode and input are both zero encodings, execution again consults both Facts and produces a one encoding carrying their equivalences — two straight words combine into a branch. If the opcode is a zero encoding and the input is not, execution extends straight, passing the opcode's content forward. And if the opcode carries a branch — a one encoding — execution reads the branch and collapses it to a zero encoding, carrying the relevant equivalence forward and folding the branch back down to a straight word. Each case is decided by reading the opcodes against each other through the truth of their Facts — the same parity check that has decided every comparison in the book, now deciding which output an instruction produces from an input.

There is one more piece, and it is the one that makes the output trustworthy rather than merely produced: a way of saying when an output is the *right* output for an opcode. The construction supplies it as a test that takes an opcode and a candidate output and settles whether they correspond. A boot opcode corresponds to a boot output exactly when their Facts are the same. A boot corresponds to nothing else, and nothing else corresponds to a boot — the start word answers only to the start word. A zero opcode corresponds to a zero output when their Facts agree, and a zero never corresponds to a one or a one to a zero — a straight word answers only to a straight word, a branch only to a branch. And a one opcode corresponds to a one output when both Facts are settled and the equivalence carried by the opcode is at most the equivalence carried by the output — a branch answers to a branch, in the order on equivalences built in 6.1. This correspondence test is the gate at this rung. It **tanges** a candidate output by the single characteristic that matters — *does this output correspond to the opcode that was run?* — and returns a settled verdict, admitting only what genuinely answers the instruction. It does not **funge** outputs into a pool and ask what kind they are; it holds each candidate up against the one opcode in hand and lets the correspondence decide.

Step back and count what stands. There is an instruction set — boot, zero, one, the three opcodes, written in Facts and equivalences. There is compilation — the binding of a written program onto the digital process, producing a machine with the program loaded. There is execution — the running of one opcode against one input to produce an output, every case decided by parity. And there is a correspondence test that tanges an output by whether it answers its instruction. This is a stored-program computer in the full and ordinary sense: a procedure written as data, compiled onto a machine, executed to yield a result, with the result checkable against the instruction that produced it. Every piece of it is finite, every piece grown from the digital process of 6.1, and that process grown in turn from the single Fact. No hardware was imported. The computer behind the readout's window is built, opcode and compilation and execution, out of claims that carry their own decisions.

## 6.3 — Abstracting the order, to not refer to itself

The computer of 6.2 has a hazard built into it, and the last rung of the chapter is the construction noticing the hazard and stepping around it. Here is the hazard. The machine runs on encodings, and an encoding can describe anything the machine can do — including the order relation the machine uses to compare its own equivalences and outputs. So the machine can, in principle, be handed a program that describes the very ordering it is using to run that program. The moment it can, it can be made to refer to itself: to name, in its own instruction set, the procedure by which it decides what is at most what. And a machine that can name its own deciding procedure can be walked into a contradiction.

The contradiction has a name the construction borrows for motivation, not as a result. *The Berry paradox*: consider "the smallest positive integer not nameable in fewer than twelve words." That phrase names a number in eleven words — so the number it picks out is, by its own description, not nameable in fewer than twelve words, yet here it has just been named in eleven. The phrase defines a thing it cannot consistently define. The trouble is not the number; it is that "nameable" was allowed to refer to the very naming the phrase is doing. A definition that quantifies over its own definability eats itself. The construction's machine faces the same hazard wherever the order it runs on can be named by a program that order is used to run — the deciding procedure quantifying over its own decisions.

The construction's defense is to *abstract the order itself*. Rather than let the machine work with the specific ordering it has been using — the concrete comparison of equivalences and encodings built rung by rung — it lifts the order to an abstract thing the machine handles without being able to look inside. It builds, in the same two-part grammar, an *abstraction*: a carrier whose constructors name not numbers or equivalences but the operations of the machine themselves. There is a base abstraction — a Fact standing alone, the bare placeholder. There is a *compile* abstraction — a Fact and an encoding set atop an abstraction already built, the act of compilation lifted to an abstract step. And there is an *execute* abstraction — a Fact and an encoding atop an abstraction, the act of execution lifted likewise. The abstraction is a record of *what the machine does* — it compiles, it executes — with the concrete order it does these things under deliberately not exposed. The machine now manipulates "the order" as an opaque token rather than the specific relation it was using, and a token cannot be unfolded into the procedure that handles it.

Build the order *on* abstractions, because that is where the precaution actually bites. The construction defines, on these abstract operations, exactly enough comparison to run and no more. Two base placeholders compare by whether their Facts are the same. A base placeholder compares to a compile or an execute by the same equality of Facts — the bare placeholder sits flush against the operations grown from its Fact. Two compile abstractions are taken as comparable outright, and a compile sits beneath an execute — compilation comes before execution, the natural order of doing the two. Two execute abstractions compare by descending into the abstractions they were built on, one against the other, the deeper comparison passing through to the steps beneath. And an execute is *not* taken beneath a compile — execution does not run backward into compilation. The strict version of the order is built alongside, carrying the same shape: a compile strictly precedes an execute, one execute strictly precedes another when the abstraction beneath the first strictly precedes the abstraction beneath the second, and the base placeholder strictly precedes nothing — it is flat, the floor that begins no descent.

The point of building the order this way — on abstract operations rather than on the concrete equivalences — is precisely that the abstract order is *not defined everywhere*. The construction does not supply a verdict for every pair of abstractions; it supplies just the comparisons the machine needs to run, and leaves the rest undefined on purpose. This is the same honesty the last chapter showed at the open comparison: a slot deliberately left empty rather than filled with a verdict the construction has not earned. Here the empty slots do work. Because "less than" on abstractions is only a partial thing — defined for the pairs the machine consults, undefined for the pairs that would let it name its own deciding — the machine cannot quantify over its whole ordering. The procedure it uses to decide is not, itself, a thing the procedure can fully name. The Berry hazard is foreclosed not by detecting self-reference and rejecting it, but by building the order so incompletely that the self-referential program cannot be written: there is no total ordering for it to quantify over. The order decides what the machine needs and abstains on what would let the machine turn on itself.

That is the whole of the precaution, and it is built, not asserted. The machine of 6.2 ran on a concrete order; the machine of 6.3 runs on an abstraction of that order — the operations of compiling and executing lifted to opaque tokens, with a comparison defined just far enough to drive the machine and left undefined exactly where defining it further would let the machine name its own deciding procedure. The *Berry paradox* is the motivation, the hazard the precaution guards against; it is named, not derived, and nothing here claims to resolve self-reference in general. What is demonstrated is narrower and real: an order built deliberately partial, so that the computer grown from the Fact cannot be handed a program that refers to the computer. The machine decides what it must and declines to name itself.

So the chapter closes with a computer standing where the needle stood. The digital readout in the window is no metaphor: behind it is a stored-program computer the construction has built piece by piece from the single Fact. There is a binary digit — a slot with two states that exclude each other, one occupant and never two, started at a zero and advanced by a tick, the digital process that turns the lowest place over. There is a stored program — opcodes written as data, compiled onto that process, executed to produce an output, with a correspondence test that tanges each output by whether it answers its instruction. And there is a precaution — the order abstracted and left partial, so the machine cannot refer to itself and be walked into the Berry contradiction. Over the slot's exclusion we hung the names *Pauli* and *the neutrino*, and marked them names. Everything else is demonstrated: a binary digit, a digital process, a compiler, an executor, and an order careful enough not to name its own deciding. The defendant is still standing, and the verdict is still owed. But the number in the window is no longer a pointer's resting place. It is the output of a little computer — and the little computer was grown, every opcode and tick of it, from a claim that carries its own decision and nothing else.
