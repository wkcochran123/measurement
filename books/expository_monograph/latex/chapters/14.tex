\chapter{One Result, Two Proofs, and the Evaluated Middle}
\label{chap:two-proofs-evaluated-middle}

The second part of the book named a single invariant and proved it one object reached three ways. The
third part puts that invariant to work in concrete settings --- real operators, real records, and
eventually a physical name or two laid over the result. This chapter opens the part with two findings
that are modest in scale and large in what they say about the construction's method. The first is that
the central condition of the variational story can be proved by two independent routes that arrive at
the identical proposition --- and yet the two proofs, as physical acts, can be told apart by their cost.
The second is that the interior of a path is not free but determined: its middle node is fixed, exactly
and uniquely, by its endpoints. Both findings are small theorems; together they are the construction
beginning to read its invariant rather than build it.

\section{One result, two proofs}
\label{se:one-result-two-proofs}

The condition that the coupled difference vanishes --- that a path is, in the full second-order sense,
solved at its interior --- can be reached two ways. One route runs through the mixed coupling directly;
the other runs through the second variation named in the previous chapter. They are different
derivations, taking different intermediate steps, and the construction proves the obvious and necessary
thing about them: they prove the same proposition. The coupled-zero condition obtained by the mixed
route and the coupled-zero condition obtained by the second-variation route are, as propositions,
equal --- a proof irrelevance, the statement that once a proposition is proved, the road taken to it
leaves no trace on what was proved. The construction funges the two derivations together by the one
characteristic that matters for truth: that they establish the same fact.

It is worth being concrete about what the two routes are, because their difference is real even though
their conclusion is not. The mixed route reaches the coupled-zero condition by working with the cross
term directly --- it forms the mixed coupling of the interior node and asks when that term vanishes. The
second-variation route reaches the same condition through the named invariant of the previous chapter
--- it forms the second variation and asks when \emph{it} vanishes. Because the second variation was
\emph{defined} to be the mixed coupling, the two are bound to agree, and the proof that they do is
almost a formality. But the routes are not the same proof: one manipulates a cubic difference, the
other a named quadratic form, and they pass through different lemmas on the way. The point of carrying
both is not redundancy for its own sake but the discipline of triangulation --- a result reached by two
roads that share no intermediate steps does not depend on the peculiarities of either road, and that
independence is what the experimental library means by corroboration.

This is a confirmation of exactly the kind the experimental library is built around. Two independent
routes to one result is a triangulation, and when both routes close on the same proposition, the
result is corroborated in the only way a finite construction allows --- not by a single authority but
by the agreement of independent witnesses. That the two proofs use entirely different machinery and
still land on the identical claim is evidence that the claim is a feature of the action and not an
artifact of one derivation, the same standard the construction has applied to invariants since the
first chapter. Proof irrelevance is the formal form of that agreement: the two roads are interchangeable
because the destination does not remember which one it was reached by.

\section{But the proofs are measured}
\label{se:but-the-proofs-are-measured}

Proof irrelevance says the two proofs establish the same proposition. It does not say they are the same
act, and the construction is careful to keep the distinction, because keeping it is the whole thesis of
the book. A proof is not only true or false; it is something a finite device performs, at a cost, and
the cost can be measured. The two derivations of the coupled-zero condition take different amounts of
work, and the construction forms the difference between their costs and asks the only question a finite
instrument ever asks of a difference: is it above the noise floor or below it?

The answer is a clean dichotomy, and it is the chapter's sharpest point. Measured against a calibrated
floor --- the smallest cost difference the instrument can resolve --- the two proofs are either
\emph{equivalent up to noise}, their costs differing by less than the floor and so indistinguishable as
measurements, or they are \emph{distinguishable}, their costs differing by more than the floor and so
genuinely telling apart. The construction proves it must be one or the other. As propositions the two
proofs are funged into one; as measurements they are tanged apart by exactly the trait of exceeding the
floor. This is the opening chapter returning at full strength. There, the question was how much it cost
to decide that true equals true, and the answer was that even a tautology is an act with a price. Here
the same lens is turned on a real theorem with two real proofs: they agree on what they prove and may
disagree on what they cost, and the disagreement, if it clears the calibration floor, is a fact about
the proofs as physical events even though it is no fact at all about the proposition. A measurement of a
proof is still a measurement, with a resolution below which two proofs are simply the same reading.

The calibration floor is not a metaphor; it is the same object the eighth chapter built when it checked
an instrument against a known reference. Every instrument has a smallest difference it can resolve, and
below that difference two readings are one reading --- not because they are equal, but because the
instrument cannot tell them apart, which for a finite observer is the same thing. Applied to
proof-cost, the floor says: do not claim that one proof is cheaper than another unless the gap between
their costs clears what the timing can actually resolve. A construction that ignored the floor would
manufacture distinctions out of noise, reporting one proof faster than another on the strength of a
difference too small to be real. By committing the floor first and comparing against it second, the
construction keeps the measurement of its own proofs as honest as its measurement of anything else.
That it bothers to measure its proofs at all is the unusual part, and it follows directly from the
first chapter: if deciding a proposition is a physical act with a cost, then a proof, a chain of such
decisions, has a cost too, and a cost is a thing one measures rather than assumes.

The practical upshot is sharper than it first looks. If two proofs of one theorem can be told apart by
cost, then a construction that must \emph{do} the proving --- not merely contemplate it --- has a real
reason to prefer one over the other, exactly the selection-by-cost the eleventh chapter built into its
solver. The cheaper of two equivalent proofs is the one a finite device should run, and knowing which
is cheaper, to within the calibration floor, is knowing how to spend the heartbeat well. Proof
irrelevance frees the construction to choose either proof without fear of changing what is proved;
proof-cost tells it which choice is thrift. The two facts together are the whole of why a finite
mathematician cares about the form of a proof and not only its conclusion: the conclusion is shared, the
cost is not, and the cost is what gets spent.

\section{The evaluated middle}
\label{se:the-evaluated-middle}

The second finding concerns the interior of a path, and it is the first concrete operator-like fact of
the part. A cubic path has a middle node, and one might imagine that node free --- an independent degree
of freedom to be chosen. It is not. The construction proves that, given the path's endpoints, the
middle node exists, is unique, and is changed only by changing an endpoint. The interior is determined
by the boundary: fix where the path begins and ends, and its interior joint is fixed with it, evaluated
rather than chosen.

A small picture makes the determinacy concrete. Pin the two ends of a cubic path at fixed points and ask
where its middle joint can sit. The answer is: in exactly one place. A cubic through two endpoints with
the smoothness the construction requires has no remaining freedom at its interior --- the conditions
that make it a single smooth piece, rather than two pieces meeting at a kink, use up the interior degree
of freedom entirely, and the middle is solved for. Try to move it and one of two things happens: either
the path develops a kink and is no longer the smooth object it was required to be, or an endpoint moves
to accommodate the new middle. There is no third possibility in which the middle shifts freely while the
ends stay put and the path stays smooth. This is why the construction can speak of \emph{evaluating} the
middle: it is not read off independently but computed from the endpoints by the smoothness conditions, a
function of the boundary with no input of its own.

This is the discrete face of a principle the experimental library states for whole regions. The
ideal-ledger experiment reads a bulk full of independent refinement threads and finds that the only
place they reconcile is the boundary --- that the aggregate behavior of an uncorrelated interior is
read off, and determined at, the region's edge, the way a gas's pressure is the rate at which its
boundary settles the accounts of its bulk. The evaluated middle is that principle shrunk to a single
node: the interior is not an independent reservoir of freedom but a quantity the boundary already
fixes, and to learn the middle one need only commit the ends. That a change in the middle forces a
change at an endpoint is the same statement read backward --- there is no moving the interior secretly,
without a record at the boundary, because the boundary is where the interior is determined and so where
any change in it must show. The construction is here learning to compute a value of its structures from
their edges, which is the skill the concrete operators of the next chapters are built on.

There is a thread here worth pulling, because it runs through the whole construction. To fix the
boundary and have the interior follow is the same shape as fixing a finite condition and having the
object it points to follow --- the endpoints are a finite commitment, and the smooth interior they
determine is what that commitment decides. The construction has been making this move since it built a
real by deciding, against the rationals, a below and an above: a finite decision at the edge, an object
determined throughout. The evaluated middle is that pattern at its smallest and most concrete --- two
committed endpoints, one determined interior node --- and recognizing it as the same move keeps the
concrete operators of the coming chapters tethered to the principle the whole book runs on: a structure
is fixed by what is committed at its boundary, and committed nowhere else.

This determinacy is the seed of an operator, and it is worth naming the connection before the next
chapter draws it. An operator, in the sense the part is heading toward, is exactly a rule that takes what
is committed at a boundary and returns what is thereby determined inside --- give it the edges, it
returns the interior. The evaluated middle is the smallest such rule: a map from two endpoints to one
node. The stiffness form of the next chapter is the same rule grown to several nodes, returning not a
single value but a whole interior profile from its anchored edges, and its definiteness --- like the
energy's two chapters ago --- will again be something earned at the boundary rather than assumed. The
construction is learning, in miniature here and in earnest next, to package determinacy-from-the-edge as
an object it can apply, compose, and measure.

\section{What is demonstrated, and what is assumed}
\label{se:demonstrated-assumed-ch14}

The separation is brief, and as throughout the latter half of the book it adds nothing to the standing
assumption.

What is demonstrated is two small, exact theorems and their consequences. The coupled-zero condition
has two derivations that prove the identical proposition, a proof irrelevance. The cost difference
between those two proofs is, against a calibrated floor, either below it or above it --- equivalent up
to noise or distinguishable --- with no third option. And the middle node of a cubic path is determined
by its endpoints: it exists, it is unique, and it changes only when an endpoint changes. Each is a
checked theorem about finite objects.

\begin{quote}
\emph{A note on the labels.} The one interpretive move here is small and worth marking. Calling the
cost difference between two proofs a \emph{measurement}, with a calibration floor and a noise threshold,
treats a proof as a physical event rather than a timeless object --- which is the book's governing
stance, declared in the first chapter and applied here to the proofs of the variational story
themselves. The mathematics is the proof irrelevance and the determinacy of the middle; the reading of
proof-cost as a measured quantity is the bridge, and it carries exactly the weight the opening chapter's
instrument gave it.
\end{quote}

What is assumed remains only the standing oracle of the tenth chapter, present here in the background
because the coupled-zero condition is a condition on stationarity. With two proofs shown to agree on
what they prove and to be measurable in what they cost, and with the interior of a path shown
determined by its boundary, the construction has the two habits the rest of the part requires: reading
a result through more than one route, and computing an interior from its edges. The next chapter uses
both to build the first genuinely concrete operator --- a stiffness form on three nodes, whose
definiteness, once again, will have to be earned at an anchor.
