\chapter{The Split, and Radiation at the Boundary}
\label{chap:split-boundary-radiation}

Generating a gauge field from geometry left the construction with two sectors --- a geometric one and a
gauge one --- and the obvious next question is how they relate. This chapter answers it with a clean
structural fact and draws a physical consequence from it that is among the most suggestive in the book.
The two sectors are orthogonal: they split, and their cross-term, the place where geometry and gauge
would couple, vanishes in the interior. With the interior closed, the coupling has only one place left to
live, the boundary --- and the coupling is what radiates. So the construction derives, from orthogonality
alone, that radiation is a phenomenon of the boundary and not of the bulk. The fence, as ever for the
heaviest physics: what is proved is a theorem about a finite split and a discrete shadow of the Clifford
structure; that the boundary coupling it isolates \emph{is} the radiation of horizons and vacuum is the
modeling bridge, named where it is crossed.

\section{The split}
\label{se:the-split}

The construction packages the two sectors into a single certified object: a finite certificate of the
split, carrying the geometric sector, the gauge sector, the pairing between them, and a representative of
each. The decisive property is that the pairing of the two is zero. Measured against each other through
the construction's inner product, the geometric and gauge sectors are orthogonal --- the dot product
vanishes --- and the construction proves it on the three-node certificate directly. This is not the
orthogonality of one sector being empty; both sectors are nontrivial, each carrying genuine content, so
the split is a real decomposition into two live, perpendicular pieces and not a trivial one with nothing
on one side. And the two do not collapse into a single finite sum: there is no finite combination that
fuses geometry and gauge into one undifferentiated total. They are two, they are orthogonal, and they
stay two.

The phrase \emph{no finite sum} deserves unpacking, because it is where the split stops being a mere
labeling and becomes a fact with consequences. To say geometry and gauge could be fused into a finite
sum would be to say there is a single finite object --- one combined sector --- that holds everything
both of them hold, so that the distinction between them was only a way of writing one thing in two parts.
The construction proves there is no such object: the two sectors cannot be collapsed into one finite
total without losing content, because the orthogonality that separates them is not a choice of
coordinates but a structural fact the certificate carries. So the split is irreducible in the same sense
the earlier chapters' invariants were real: it cannot be defined away by a cleverer bookkeeping, only
acknowledged. Geometry and gauge are genuinely two perpendicular things, and a theory that wants both
must carry both, separately, paired but never summed.

The product through which they are measured is the construction's discrete shadow of the Clifford
algebra --- the structure that, in the continuous theory, multiplies the components of a Dirac field and
encodes how geometric and gauge quantities combine. Here the fence wants stating early. The construction
has a Clifford shadow, a finite operation with the multiplication rule the Clifford algebra would
impose, and it is a shadow in exactly the sense the geometry was: the right shape, none of the
continuous theory's analytic content. What the construction proves about orthogonality and the split is
a theorem about this finite shadow; the Dirac field it shadows is not built, only echoed.

The choice to keep this a shadow rather than reach for the Dirac field itself is the same discipline the
geometry chapter showed, and it is worth seeing what it costs and buys. The continuous Clifford algebra
carries analytic content the finite shadow does not: a Dirac field is a section of a bundle over a
continuum, with all the machinery of differentiation and integration the continuum supports. The
construction's shadow keeps only the algebra --- the multiplication rule, the way components combine, the
anticommutation that gives the sign its place --- and drops the continuum underneath. What this buys is
that every statement about the split is finite and checkable; what it costs is that the construction
cannot claim to have a Dirac field, only an object that multiplies the way one would. The split, the
orthogonality, the boundary coupling: all of these are facts about the multiplication rule, true of the
shadow whether or not any continuous Dirac field realizes it. The construction proves what the algebra
forces and leaves the field it shadows to the modeling note.

\section{The interior is closed}
\label{se:the-interior-is-closed}

Orthogonality has an immediate and sharp consequence: there is no interior mixed term. The scalar that
would measure a coupling between geometry and gauge in the bulk is the cross-term of the pairing, and the
pairing is zero, so the cross-term is zero everywhere in the interior. The construction states this as an
obstruction, which is the strong form of the claim: a nonzero interior mixed scalar could not merely be
absent by happenstance; for one to exist, at least one of the structures that make the split true --- the
pairing, the orthogonality, the certificate --- would have to change. The vanishing is not a coincidence
of the particular configuration but a consequence of the architecture, and the obstruction is the proof
that nothing admissible can carry an interior coupling. Orthogonality tanges the interior mixed term
straight to zero: it selects, by the decidable fact of perpendicularity, exactly the bulk coupling and
sends it to nothing.

It is worth dwelling on how strong an \emph{obstruction} is, as against a mere computation of zero. To
compute that the mixed term is zero on this configuration would settle this configuration and leave the
next one open. An obstruction settles all of them at once: it shows that the zero is forced by a feature
the whole admissible class shares, so that asking for an interior coupling is asking for a configuration
that violates the very orthogonality that defines the split. This is the same shape of argument the
seventeenth chapter used for parity and the nineteenth used for the matter--antimatter sign --- a zero or
a sign held in place not by accident but by an obstruction no admissible move can get around --- and it
is the construction's most characteristic tool. The interior, by it, is closed: nothing radiates from
the bulk because nothing admissible can.

\section{Radiation lives at the boundary}
\label{se:radiation-lives-at-the-boundary}

If the coupling cannot live in the interior, and the coupling is what radiates, then radiation has
exactly one place left to be read: the boundary. This is the chapter's physical payoff, and the logic is
just elimination. The mixed term is the only thing that can carry energy between the sectors; the
interior mixed term is obstructed to zero; the boundary is the only remaining locus; therefore the
construction's radiation is a boundary quantity, and the construction proves further that it is finite
--- a definite, bounded reading at the edge, not a divergence. Radiation, in this account, is not emitted
by the bulk and is not infinite; it is a finite phenomenon of the boundary, forced there by the closure
of the interior.

The finiteness is worth as much attention as the location, because together they make the result a
physical statement rather than a formal one. A coupling forced to the boundary could still, in principle,
be infinite there --- a divergence at the edge, the kind of thing that makes a theory return nonsense and
need to be renormalized by hand. The construction proves instead that the boundary reading is finite: a
definite, bounded number, the actual size of the coupling the interior could not hold. So the picture is
not merely \emph{radiation happens at the edge} but \emph{radiation is a finite quantity the edge
carries}, a measurement the boundary returns the way the integrator returned a reading on a path. The
interior contributes nothing, the boundary contributes a finite amount, and the total is the finite
boundary reading --- a clean accounting with no infinity to subtract away, which is exactly the kind of
finiteness the whole construction has been built to deliver.

The physical resonances are strong enough that the experimental library is worth reading alongside this,
with the fence held. The experiment under Hawking's name finds radiation emerging from a horizon --- a
boundary --- where the bulk spacetime is empty; the radiation is a property of the edge, not of the
interior it bounds, which is precisely the structure the construction derives. The experiment on the
Casimir effect finds a real force arising from the vacuum energy between two plates --- two boundaries
--- with nothing in the bulk between them but the vacuum; again the boundary carries what the interior
does not. And the holographic reading, under the names of 't~Hooft and Susskind, makes the general claim
the construction's elimination argument quietly instances: that the content of a bulk can be read off its
boundary, because the boundary is where the otherwise-closed interior deposits what it has. In each case
the construction's theorem is the same finite fact --- interior closed, coupling at the edge, finite ---
and the physical name is the analogy. That horizons radiate and plates attract is the world's business;
that an orthogonal split forces its coupling to the boundary is the construction's, and the second is
offered as the shape of the first, not its derivation.

What makes the resonance more than a coincidence of vocabulary is that all three experiments share the
construction's exact logical move: close the interior, read the boundary. Hawking's horizon radiates
because the bulk it bounds is empty and the edge is where the field's accounts come due; the Casimir
force exists because the bulk between the plates is vacuum and the plates are where the vacuum's energy
is felt; the holographic reading is the general statement that a closed interior deposits its content on
its boundary and nowhere else. The construction did not set out to model any of them; it proved an
orthogonal split forces its coupling to the boundary, and found the proof had the shape of all three.
That convergence is the reason to take the shadow seriously as a shadow --- the finite structure keeps
landing on the architecture the physics has --- and it is also exactly why the fence matters, because a
structure that resembles three different physical phenomena at once is plainly capturing something about
their common shape and just as plainly not deriving any one of them in particular.

\section{The flat vacuum, completed}
\label{se:the-flat-vacuum-completed}

One case anchors all of this: the flat one, the vacuum. On the flat configuration --- no bending, no
charge, the geometry trivial --- the residual is zero. There is nothing to radiate because there is no
coupling to send to the boundary, and the construction proves the flat residual vanishes exactly. This
is the baseline the rest is read against: radiation is the departure of the boundary coupling from its
flat-vacuum value of zero, the way every measurement in the book has been a departure from a baseline
the apparatus reads as nothing. The construction files the flat case as a completed certificate ---
the flat pair, finished and closed --- so that the vacuum is not an open question hanging over the theory
but a settled, funged-shut object the radiating cases can be compared to. The flat tower is funged into
one completed certificate, and the vacuum is thereby a thing the construction holds rather than awaits.

There is a quiet importance in having the vacuum as a held object rather than an open one. Through the
whole book a measurement has been a departure from a baseline the apparatus reads as zero --- the first
mark against an unmarked tape, the residue against a story that almost closes, the strain against an
undisturbed form. Radiation is now another such departure: the boundary coupling read against its
flat-vacuum value of zero. For that reading to mean anything, the zero has to be genuinely fixed, not
merely assumed, and the completed flat certificate is what fixes it: a proof that the vacuum radiates
nothing, filed and closed, so that any nonzero boundary reading is a real departure and not an artifact
of an unsettled baseline. The vacuum, made into a finished certificate, is what lets radiation be
measured rather than merely noticed --- the still point the moving quantity is read against.

\section{What is demonstrated, and what is assumed}
\label{se:demonstrated-assumed-ch21}

The separation here is clean, and the physics sits entirely on the named side.

What is demonstrated is the split and its consequence. The geometric and gauge sectors are proved
orthogonal on the finite certificate, both nontrivial, with no finite sum fusing them. The interior mixed
term is obstructed to zero --- not computed away on one configuration but forced to vanish across the
admissible class. The boundary coupling that remains is proved finite. And the flat vacuum's residual is
proved zero and filed as a completed certificate. Every one of these is a checked statement about the
finite split.

\begin{quote}
\emph{A note on the labels.} Two bridges carry the physics here. The Clifford structure the sectors are
paired through is a \emph{shadow} --- a finite operation with the Clifford multiplication rule, echoing a
Dirac field the construction does not build. And the identification of the boundary coupling with
\emph{radiation} --- with the emission of horizons, the force between Casimir plates, the holographic
deposit of a bulk on its edge --- is the modeling claim, an analogy of real strength but an analogy. The
proved thing in the middle is sharp and worth not losing in the hedging: an orthogonal split forces its
coupling out of the interior and onto the boundary, finitely. That horizons radiate is the world saying
something with this shape; the construction supplies the shape, audited, and stops there.
\end{quote}

What is assumed, then, is the Clifford shadow and the radiation identification, together with the
imported completion the flat certificate leans on and the standing oracle above it all. With radiation
placed at the boundary and the vacuum filed shut, the field-theoretic picture is structurally complete:
two sectors, orthogonal, coupling only at the edge. The next chapter brings the two sectors back into
relation in the one way the split permits --- as a limit --- and in doing so reaches the place where the
construction can finally name the forcing that has been running underneath it from the first sample.
