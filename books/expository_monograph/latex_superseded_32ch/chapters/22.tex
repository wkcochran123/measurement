\chapter{Unification, Counting, and the Name of the Forcing}
\label{chap:unification-counting-forcing}

The split left two sectors perpendicular and uncombined. This chapter brings them back into relation in
the one way the split allows --- as a limit --- and in doing so reaches a place the book has been
approaching since its first page without naming. It does three things. It shows that the gauge theory is
the limit of the geometric one as the geometric scale vanishes, which is the construction's unification
statement. It returns to the number tower it started from and builds genuine arithmetic on it, counting
to three by a real successor and a real induction. And it finally names the structure that has been
running underneath every real the construction ever built: Cohen forcing, made finite, decided up to a
tolerance. The name has been withheld on purpose until the construction itself earned it; here it does.

\section{The gauge is a limit of the geometry}
\label{se:the-gauge-is-a-limit-of-the-geometry}

The geometric and gauge sectors cannot be summed, but they can be related by a limit, and the relation
the construction proves is a strong one. The geometric program carries a scale --- call it the geometric
tolerance, the discrete analogue of how fine the geometry is resolved --- and as that scale is driven to
zero, the gauge program converges. The construction proves the convergence is of the constructive,
controlled kind: the sequence of gauge readings is Cauchy, with a rate the same tolerance governs, so
that for any precision one names there is a finite stage past which the readings agree to that precision.
The gauge theory, in other words, is the limit of the geometric theory as the geometric scale vanishes
--- the construction's version of a unification, with electromagnetism emerging as the fine-scale limit
of geometry.

The Cauchy form is the honest form and worth holding onto. The construction does not assert that a limit
exists and name it the gauge theory; it exhibits a sequence and proves it Cauchy, which is the
finitist's way of having a limit --- a convergence certified by a rate rather than a completion claimed
from above. So the unification statement is not ``the two theories are secretly one'' but the more exact
``the one is the controlled limit of the other, and here is the rate.'' And here the fence holds as it
has through every chapter of this part: what converges are the construction's QED-shaped and GR-shaped
shadows, finite structures with the form of the physical theories and none of their measured content.
That a gauge shadow is the vanishing-scale limit of a geometric shadow is a real theorem with the shape
of unification; it is offered as that shape, audited, and not as a claim that the electrodynamics and
the gravity of the world stand in this relation.

It is worth being precise about what kind of unification a limit is, because it is a subtler thing than
the word usually promises. To unify two theories is often imagined as finding the single larger theory
they are both special cases of --- a roof over both. A limit unifies differently and more honestly: it
says one theory is what the other becomes at the edge of a parameter, so that they are not two faces of a
third thing but a single thing seen at two scales. The construction's claim is of this second kind. There
is not a master theory above geometry and gauge; there is the geometric program, and the gauge program is
what it converges to as its scale shrinks. The electron, the gauge field, the radiation at the boundary
--- all of these, which arrived in the gauge sector, are recovered as the fine-scale limit of geometric
data. That is a tighter and more falsifiable claim than a roof would be, because a limit comes with a
rate, and a rate is a thing one can check.

\section{Counting to three}
\label{se:counting-to-three}

Having built a charged particle, a gauge field, and a radiation law, the construction does something
that looks like a step backward and is in fact the closing of its longest loop: it returns to the
natural numbers and builds arithmetic. It began, chapters ago, by building a sample --- a finite object
standing in for a real number, scaffolding it could compute with. Now it builds the naturals properly,
on its own foundations: a genuine successor that takes each number to the next, an induction principle
that lets it prove statements about all numbers by the canonical ladder of base case and step, and, as
the worked token of all this, the theorem that one and one and one make three.

That last theorem is smaller than anything else in the chapter and is there for a reason. To prove
$1+1+1=3$ from a successor and induction is to show that the construction's number tower is not a
borrowed convenience but a thing it owns --- that it can define the successor, climb it three times from
zero, and certify by its own induction that the result is the canonical three. The construction that
spent twenty chapters reading physics off an invariant pauses to show it can also count, rigorously, from
the same foundations. This matters for what comes next, because the forcing the chapter is about to name
lives on exactly these naturals: the conditions will be finite lists indexed by them, the tolerances will
be naturals, and the whole apparatus of the generic will be built on the number tower the construction
has just made honest.

It is worth walking the small theorem once, because its smallness is the point. Three is defined as the
successor of the successor of the successor of zero --- climb the ladder three rungs from the bottom. The
sum $1+1+1$ is computed by the successor too: one is the successor of zero, and adding one is taking a
successor, so $1+1+1$ unfolds, step by step, into the same three-rung climb. The two sides meet not
because a calculator agrees but because the construction's own definition of addition and its own
successor drive them to the identical canonical form, and its induction principle certifies the identity
is not an accident of small numbers but the way the ladder is built. A reader who has watched the book
build samples, invariants, an electron, and a gauge field might find it strange to see it pause over
$1+1+1$. The point of the pause is sovereignty: the construction is showing that the numbers it has used
throughout are numbers it can define and reason about from the ground, not borrowed from outside, and
that the forcing about to be built on them rests on arithmetic the construction owns.

\section{The name of the forcing}
\label{se:the-name-of-the-forcing}

Now the construction names what it has been doing. From the beginning, a real number in this book was
never a completed object handed down from above; it was approached by finite specifications, each
committing to a little more than the last, and the number was whatever they converged to. The
construction now makes that structure exact and gives it its name. A \emph{condition} --- the
construction calls it a spline condition --- is a finite list of atoms, each pinned below a tolerance
$\varepsilon$: a finite, partial specification of a real, a cut that says \emph{here is what I have
decided so far} and leaves the rest open. The conditions are ordered: a stronger condition extends a
weaker one, containing all its commitments and adding more, and the construction proves this order is a
genuine one, reflexive and transitive, a partial order of finite cuts. Any bounded question --- any knot
within the tolerance --- can be settled by a single finite extension, one more atom added to decide it.
And any two compatible conditions have a merge, a common extension that honors both. Finite cuts, ordered
by extension, with one-step decisions and merges of the compatible: this is Cohen forcing, and the
construction's own notes call the result Cohen-shaped. The book has finally earned the name it withheld.

Each piece of that structure had a quiet career earlier in the book, and it is worth collecting them now
that they have a name. The finite condition is the sample of the first chapters --- the finite
specification that stood in for a real, here seen as a cut that commits to part of the number and leaves
the rest open. The extension order is the refinement that drove every convergence: a finer sample, a
deeper tower, a stronger condition, all the same act of committing to more. The one-step decision of a
bounded knot is the reason the generic is total where it is approached --- any question within the
tolerance can be answered by adding a single atom, so no bounded question is left permanently open. And
the merge of compatible conditions is the consistency the construction has insisted on everywhere: two
partial specifications that do not contradict can be held at once, pooled into one that honors both. None
of this is new machinery. It is the machinery the book has run on from the start, gathered under the name
that has always fit it.

What this reveals is that the generic --- the real the conditions approach --- was the forcing's generic
all along, the object every sufficiently rich family of conditions drives toward, never a completed
totality but the limit of finite commitments. And the book's two verbs turn out to be the two acts of a
single condition. To lay down a condition is to \emph{tange}: to select, by the decidable test of the
tolerance, exactly which atoms one commits to. And to combine compatible conditions is to \emph{funge}:
to pool them into the merge that honors them both. Forcing is tanging and funging --- deciding atoms and
pooling the compatible --- and every real the construction built was a tower of such acts. The
experiment that carries the names of Cantor, Godel, and Cohen marks the lineage: Cantor's continuum,
Godel's proof that its size cannot be refuted, and Cohen's that it cannot be settled, the independence
established by exactly this method of finite conditions approaching a generic. The construction stands in
that line, with one deliberate difference it never hides.

That the book's two verbs are the two acts of forcing is worth pausing on, because it explains why they
have been everywhere. A condition does exactly two things, and a measurement does exactly two things, and
they are the same two. To tange is to decide --- to select, by a test the construction can actually run,
which atoms a condition commits to, which is the selecting half of every reading the apparatus has taken.
To funge is to pool --- to merge the compatible into a common refinement, which is the gathering half of
every count the apparatus has made. Every chapter that distinguished a tange from a funge was, it turns
out, distinguishing the deciding act of a forcing condition from its pooling act, without yet having the
word forcing to hang them on. The verbs were the construction's way of naming the two motions of the
generic before it was ready to name the generic itself.

The difference is the tolerance. Set-theoretic Cohen forcing reaches the full generic, an object whose
existence needs the completed infinite. The construction reaches only \emph{up to $\varepsilon$}: every
condition is finite, every decision is one step, every bound is a natural number, and the generic is
approached to within whatever tolerance one fixes and no further in any single finite stage. This is
Cohen forcing made finite --- the constructive face of the method, decided up to a tolerance rather than
completed. It is also exactly the one grant the book has carried openly since the tenth chapter, now
seen for what it is. The single thing the construction is given, the genericity of the law of motion, is
the genericity of this forcing: the assurance that the dense families of conditions are met, that the
generic the finite cuts approach is actually decided. The construction builds every condition, every
extension, every merge; what it is granted is that the approaching reaches its generic, up to epsilon.
Naming the forcing does not add an assumption. It identifies the one the book always had.

This is the deepest payoff of the name, and it deserves to be said plainly. Through twenty-one chapters
the book has had exactly one assumption it could not discharge, carried openly and called by various
provisional names --- the oracle, the granted decision, the genericity of the law of motion. Naming the
forcing collapses all of those into a single, classical, well-understood object: the genericity of a
Cohen forcing, the assurance that the dense families of finite conditions are met. The construction does
not need this for the conditions themselves, which it builds, nor for the extensions or merges, which it
proves; it needs it only for the one step from \emph{approached} to \emph{reached}, from the tower of
finite cuts to the generic they converge to. To grant the law of motion was always to grant exactly that
step. The book's single act of faith, it turns out, is the single act of faith of forcing --- and a
remarkably small and precise one, set against everything the construction builds without it.

\section{What is demonstrated, and what is assumed}
\label{se:demonstrated-assumed-ch22}

This chapter's separation is unusually satisfying, because naming the forcing sharpens exactly what the
one assumption is.

What is demonstrated is the limit, the arithmetic, and the forcing. The gauge readings are proved Cauchy
as the geometric scale vanishes, with a controlled rate --- the unification, as a constructive limit.
The successor and induction are built and $1+1+1=3$ is proved, the number tower made the construction's
own. And the conditions, their order, their one-step decisions, and their merges are all built and
proved, the whole structure shown to be Cohen forcing, decided up to a tolerance. Every piece is finite
and checked.

\begin{quote}
\emph{A note on the labels, and on the name.} Two things here are of different kinds. The unification ---
gauge as the limit of geometry --- is a theorem about the construction's shadows, and its identification
with the unification of the physical theories is the modeling bridge this whole part has been marking. By
contrast, naming the forcing is not a modeling claim at all; it is a piece of mathematical recognition.
The conditions, the extension order, the merges, the generic decided up to $\varepsilon$ --- these are
literally a Cohen forcing, not a structure resembling one, and calling them by their name adds nothing
and assumes nothing. The reason the name waited until now is only that the construction had to build
enough of the forcing, openly, before the word would be earned rather than imposed. The one assumption
the book has carried --- the genericity granted to the law of motion --- is the genericity of this
forcing, and that is the whole of what is assumed, now stated in its proper vocabulary.
\end{quote}

What is assumed, then, is the standing grant, which the forcing's name has now made precise, together
with the modeling identification on the unification side. With the gauge placed as a limit of the
geometry, the number tower rebuilt, and the forcing named, the construction has shown both what it builds
and what it is given, in the same language. The next chapter takes the forcing one step further and
proves that its ledger of conditions is countable --- that the finite cuts can be enumerated --- which is
the last structural fact the completion will need.
