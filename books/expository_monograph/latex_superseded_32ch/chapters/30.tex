\chapter{Matter and Antimatter Are Baseline-Relative}
\label{chap:matter-antimatter-baseline-relative}

The last chapter made velocity relative; this one does the same to matter and antimatter. The pair the
book has carried since the electron --- the plus and the minus, the orientation that cancels --- turns
out to read differently depending on the baseline an observer brings to it. Over one baseline the same
pair is an electron; over another it is a positron. Whether a thing is matter or antimatter is not, in
the construction, an absolute property of the thing; it is a reading relative to a baseline, exactly as
velocity was a reading relative to a frame. The chapter draws this out on a concrete pairwise trial, ties
charge to the holonomy of a loop, and ends with a consequence worth stating carefully: over the baseline
native to the construction, there is pure matter and no antimatter, so antimatter requires a tilt --- a
finite reading with the shape of the puzzle of why the universe is made of matter, offered as that shape
and fenced as such.

\section{The baseline-relative flip}
\label{se:the-baseline-relative-flip}

The construction first ties the orientation to the relativity of the last chapter: the relative velocity
of a pair is proved equal to its second variation, so the pair's matter-or-antimatter reading and its
relative velocity are the same invariant seen twice. Then it runs a pairwise sign trial. It fixes a set
of pairs and reads them over two baselines: a flat one and a tilted one. Over the flat baseline the trial
returns an electron and no positron --- the pair reads as matter, with no antimatter event anywhere in it.
Over the tilted baseline, the same pairs, a positron appears. The construction proves all of this
directly: no positron over the flat path, an electron over the flat path, a positron over the tilted
path. The conclusion is the baseline-relative flip: one pair, two baselines, matter over the one and
antimatter over the other.

The trial uses three pairs and no more, and the choice is not arbitrary. The construction has shown its
apparatus resolves at most three tags --- a hard finiteness, proved by the fact that any four tags must
collide, two of them forced to share a value. Three distinguishable readings is the whole budget, and the
matter--antimatter trial spends it exactly: a tag for the matter reading, a tag for the antimatter
reading, and a tag for the neutral or absent case, with no room for a fourth. So the orientation does not
live in a rich continuum of types but in a space of three values, the readings funged down to what three
tags can carry. This finiteness is not a limitation the construction apologizes for; it is part of the
claim. A measurement, the book has insisted from its first page, returns finitely much, and the
orientation reading returns at most three tags' worth --- enough to tell matter from antimatter from
neither, and not one distinction more.

So matter and antimatter join velocity as baseline-relative readings. The orientation an observer reads
is not carried by the pair absolutely; it is selected by the baseline against which the pair is read, and
a tilt of that baseline can flip an electron into a positron. The experiment on chirality is the physical
counterpart held at the right level: handedness is real and irremovable, but which handedness one calls
matter is read against a reference, and shifting the reference shifts the reading. The construction adds a
finiteness to this: its apparatus resolves at most three tags. Any four tags collide --- two of them must
share --- so the orientation reading lives in a space of only three distinguishable values, the matter
and antimatter readings funged down to what three tags can hold. The flip is not a continuum of shadings
but a discrete reassignment among finitely many tags, and three is all the apparatus has.

It is worth walking the flip slowly, because its strangeness is easy to miss. Take one definite pair ---
the same physical objects, the same orientation between them --- and read it twice. Read against a flat
baseline, the apparatus tags it electron: matter, charge of one sign, no antimatter anywhere in the
events. Now tilt the baseline and read the very same pair again, changing nothing about the pair itself,
only the reference it is measured against. Now the apparatus tags a positron: antimatter, the opposite
sign, present where before there was none. Nothing in the pair moved; the baseline did. The apparatus
tanges the orientation out of the pair by comparing it to the baseline, and a different baseline selects a
different orientation from the identical pair. This is what it means for matter and antimatter to be
relative rather than absolute: the property is not in the pair waiting to be read but in the relation
between the pair and the reference, and the reference is the observer's to set.

\section{Charge lives on a loop}
\label{se:charge-lives-on-a-loop}

The construction next locates where the charge that flips actually resides, and the answer is the
holonomy of the geometry chapter, returned. Charge is the sign of a loop holonomy: a closed loop carries
charge, and an open path carries none. The orientation is not a property of a point or a segment but of a
circuit --- what a charge accumulates going around and returning, the same loop-wise quantity the
Aharonov--Bohm reading measured. An open path, with no circuit to close, has no holonomy and so no charge;
only when the path closes into a loop does a charge appear, its sign the orientation of the loop.

That charge should live on a loop rather than at a point is less strange than it first sounds, and it
explains a good deal. A point has no inside and no outside, nothing for a circuit to enclose; a loop does,
and the holonomy measures exactly what the loop encircles. So a charge is not a little marble sitting at a
location but a fact about a circuit --- how much a quantity transported around the loop fails to return to
itself. This is why open paths are uncharged: an open path can be deformed straight, its ends pulled
apart, with nothing trapped inside, and a thing with nothing trapped carries no holonomy. The
construction's charges are all of this kind, loop quantities, and the orientation that flips with the
baseline is the sign of a loop's holonomy read against that baseline. To change the baseline is to change
what counts as the loop's enclosed reference, and so to change the sign the holonomy reports --- the flip
of the previous section, seen now as a re-reading of a circuit rather than a relabeling of a point.

This is why detection is frame-relative, and the construction proves it so. Whether a positron is
detected depends on the frame in which the loop is read, because the holonomy that carries the charge is
read against a baseline, and the baseline is part of the frame. Tilt the frame and the loop's holonomy
reads differently, and the positron that was there is not, or the electron that was there becomes its
opposite. Charge on a loop, read against a baseline, in a frame: each layer adds a relativity, and the
upshot is that detecting antimatter is no more absolute than measuring a velocity. The thing detected is
real --- a loop with a holonomy --- but the sign it is detected with is read, not given.

The layering of relativities here is worth naming, because it is the chapter's structural core. A velocity
was relative to a frame; a charge is relative to a baseline; and the baseline is itself part of a frame,
so a charge inherits the frame-relativity of velocity and adds its own. An observer who fixes a frame has
not thereby fixed every reading: within the frame, the baseline against which a loop's holonomy is
measured can still be tilted, and tilting it flips the charge. So matter and antimatter sit one level
deeper in the relativities than velocity does --- read against a reference that is itself read against a
frame --- which is why the same pair can be matter to one observer and antimatter to another even when the
two agree on every velocity. The construction does not collapse these layers; it keeps them distinct, a
velocity relativity and a baseline relativity stacked, and the orientation flip lives in the upper one.
That stacking is the reason the chapter could not simply repeat the last: charge is relative in a second
way, on top of the relativity velocity already had.

\section{The split, counted}
\label{se:the-split-counted}

The chapter's last move counts the split and finds an asymmetry with a familiar shape. Over the baseline
native to the construction --- the symmetric one, untilted --- the antimatter count is zero. The
construction proves it: native antimatter is nothing, the symmetric baseline yielding pure matter and no
antimatter at all. Antimatter is not native; it requires a baseline asymmetry, a tilt away from the
symmetric reference, to appear at all. The construction proves this complement too: antimatter needs
asymmetry. The split, counted over the native baseline, is all matter; the antimatter side is empty until
a tilt fills it.

This has the shape of the deepest puzzle in cosmology --- why the universe is made of matter, with almost
no antimatter, when the two seem so symmetric --- and the resemblance is worth stating precisely, with
the fence firm. The construction does not explain the baryon asymmetry of the world. It does not derive
the observed matter excess, fix its size, or identify the physical mechanism that tilted the cosmic
baseline. What it shows is a structural fact about its own readings: that a perfectly symmetric baseline
yields pure matter, so that any matter--antimatter imbalance is the signature of a baseline asymmetry
rather than a property of the pairs themselves. The puzzle, read through the construction, becomes the
question of why the cosmic baseline is tilted --- which the construction does not answer, but which it
reframes, moving the asymmetry from the matter to the baseline it is read against. That reframing is the
contribution and the whole of it; the cosmology is named, not derived.

The reframing deserves to be valued for what it is, neither more nor less. To move the asymmetry from the
matter to the baseline does not explain the asymmetry; the universe is still lopsided, and the
construction still cannot say why. But it relocates the mystery to a place where it may be more tractable,
or at least more clearly posed. A world of pure matter is not, on this reading, a world where something
suppressed the antimatter; it is a world read against a symmetric baseline, and a world with an antimatter
component is one read against a tilt. The question \emph{where did the antimatter go} becomes \emph{what
tilted the baseline}, and the second question, while no easier in fact, is at least about the reference
rather than about a missing substance. Whether that relocation is the right way to see the cosmic
asymmetry is not something the construction can settle; that it is a coherent way, with a finite model
behind it, is what the construction establishes, and it leaves the cosmology to those equipped to test
it.

\section{What is demonstrated, and what is assumed}
\label{se:demonstrated-assumed-ch30}

The demonstrated facts are finite and the assumed bridge is one cosmological shape, clearly marked.

What is demonstrated is the relativity of the orientation. The pair's relative velocity is proved equal
to its second variation. The same pairs are proved to read electron over a flat baseline and positron
over a tilted one --- the baseline-relative flip --- with the apparatus resolving at most three tags.
Charge is proved to be the sign of a loop holonomy, open paths carrying none, and detection proved
frame-relative. And over the symmetric native baseline, antimatter is proved to be zero, with antimatter
proved to require an asymmetry. Each is a checked statement about finite readings.

\begin{quote}
\emph{A note on the cosmological label.} One identification here reaches for the largest of subjects and
must be held at arm's length accordingly. That a symmetric baseline yields pure matter, and antimatter
requires asymmetry, has the shape of the baryon-asymmetry problem --- the unexplained dominance of matter
in the universe. But the construction proves a finite fact about its own baselines, not a fact about the
cosmos. It does not derive the matter excess, does not predict its magnitude, does not supply the
symmetry-breaking the world's asymmetry would need. What it offers is a reframing: if matter and
antimatter are baseline-relative, then a matter-dominated reading is the signature of a tilted baseline,
and the question shifts from \emph{why more matter} to \emph{why a tilted baseline}. That is a genuine
structural insight and a finite theorem; the identification of it with the cosmological asymmetry is the
modeling bridge, and like every physical name in this book it is offered as a shape and not a derivation.
\end{quote}

What is assumed, then, is the standing grant and this one cosmological shape, held as a reframing rather
than an explanation. With matter and antimatter shown baseline-relative, the second axiom's relativity
has spread from velocity to charge: both are selections, read against references an observer brings.
There remains one more thing to read against a reference, and it is the most reflexive of all. The next
chapter turns the construction's gauge into a vector of bits and measures distance in that space ---
setting up the final chapter, where the reference brought to the construction is the reader's own.
