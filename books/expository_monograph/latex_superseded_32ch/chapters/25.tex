\chapter{The Orientation EKG}
\label{chap:orientation-ekg}

The construction is built, completed, and self-describing; what it has not yet done is submit to a
reading from outside. The remaining part takes that step, asking what an observer who did not build the
construction --- who sees only its outputs, not its scaffolding --- could measure of it. The first such
reading is the simplest and sets the stance for the rest. It reads the construction's matter and
antimatter content as a trace, the way an electrocardiogram reads a heartbeat: a signal that swings
between plus one and minus one, whose opposite swings cancel to a baseline, whose net deflection is what
the reader actually records, and whose polarity --- which way counts as up --- is a convention the
outside reader fixes rather than a fact the construction dictates. Around that trace the chapter builds a
reciprocal accounting, the bookkeeping any partial reading must keep: what it resolves and what it leaves
unresolved together make the whole.

\section{The trace and its baseline}
\label{se:the-trace-and-its-baseline}

Each orientation gives a reading, and the readings are the ones the electron chapter found: an
orientation one way reads minus one, the other way reads plus one. The construction proves the two cancel
--- minus one and plus one sum to zero --- and proves it with no axiom, the cancellation falling out of
the readings themselves rather than being imposed. This is the trace's baseline. A balanced pair of
opposite orientations nets to nothing, the flat line a steady heart draws between beats, and the
construction establishes that this flatness is not assumed but earned: where matter and antimatter are
present in equal measure, the orientation trace reads zero, axiom-free. The tange here is the reading
itself --- each orientation selected, by the decidable test of its sign, to a definite plus or minus
one --- and the baseline is what those tanges sum to when they balance.

That the baseline is a sum of cancelling tanges is worth holding onto, because it tells the outside reader
what kind of zero they are looking at. There are two ways a trace can read zero: because nothing is there,
or because equal and opposite things are there and cancel. The orientation trace's baseline is the second
kind --- a zero full of matched matter and antimatter, not an empty one --- and the construction's
axiom-free cancellation is precisely the proof that the two read the same. To an outside observer the
distinction is invisible in the trace itself; a flat line is a flat line. But the reciprocal accounting to
come will recover it, because the matched pairs that cancelled in the trace are not gone from the whole
--- they are resolved into the baseline, counted and then summed away, present in the accounting even when
absent from the deflection. The outside reading thus has a memory the bare trace does not: what cancelled
is still on the books.

It is worth seeing why axiom-freeness matters for a baseline. A baseline imposed by fiat --- declared to
be zero because a balanced state ought to read zero --- would tell the outside reader nothing; it would
be a convention dressed as a measurement. The construction's baseline is the opposite: it computes that
the opposite readings cancel, so that a flat trace is a genuine finding about a balanced configuration,
not a definition. When the outside reader sees the trace at zero, they are seeing a real equality of
matter and antimatter, certified by the construction's own arithmetic, and when they see it deflect, they
are seeing a real imbalance. The baseline is information because it was proved, not posited.

A worked trace makes the reading concrete. Imagine the outside reader watching a sequence of
configurations pass --- a stretch with matter and antimatter in balance, then a stretch where one sign
runs ahead. Over the balanced stretch the trace sits at zero: every plus-one orientation is matched by a
minus-one, and the construction's cancellation drives their sum to the baseline, beat after beat reading
flat. Then the imbalance arrives, and the trace lifts off zero by exactly the net count of the dominant
sign --- one unit of deflection for each unmatched orientation. The reader, watching only this trace,
learns precisely one thing about each stretch: its net orientation, the signed surplus of one sign over
the other. They do not learn how many of each were present, only the difference, because the cancellation
has already collapsed the matched pairs to nothing before the trace is read. This is what an
electrocardiogram does too --- it reports the net electrical swing of the heart, not the firing of each
cell --- and it is the first lesson of reading from outside: an outside trace reports a net, and the
matched detail is gone into the baseline.

\section{Dominance and the convention anchor}
\label{se:dominance-and-the-convention-anchor}

A reading that only ever cancelled would be a flat line forever; the trace carries information because
configurations need not be balanced. The construction tallies the orientations present and proves that
when one sign outnumbers the other, the tally has a unique dominant orientation --- a definite net sign,
the trace's deflection from baseline. The dominance is a funge: the orientations are pooled by sign into
two counts, and the larger count names the dominant side. That the dominant orientation is unique is a
small theorem with real content, because it says the net sign is well-defined --- there is no ambiguity
about which way the trace deflects once the tally is taken.

But which dominant sign counts as matter, and which as antimatter --- which way is up on the trace --- is
where the outside stance first asserts itself, and the construction is scrupulous here. It introduces a
convention anchor, a choice that fixes which orientation is read as positive, and it proves the anchor
fixes a definite reading. What it pointedly does \emph{not} do is claim that its anchor is the world's. No
theorem here asserts that matter, rather than antimatter, is the dominant sign of the universe, or that
the construction's sign convention matches the one physics chose; the construction builds an anchor and
notes that picking it is a convention, the outside reader's free choice of polarity, not a fact derived
from within. This is the chapter's load-bearing fence. The orientation trace is real, the cancellation is
real, the dominance is real; but the labeling of one pole as matter is a convention laid on from outside,
and the construction marks it as exactly that. The experiment on chirality supplies the physical
counterpart with the same care: it finds a genuine, irremovable asymmetry between the two orientations ---
a real handedness in the ledger --- while leaving the choice of which hand to call right where it belongs,
with the conventions of the observer.

There is a symmetry here the construction is careful to honor, and it is the reason the convention cannot
be promoted to a theorem. Nothing internal to the construction distinguishes the two orientations except
that they are opposite; the readings plus one and minus one are mirror images, and every theorem about one
has its mirror about the other. A construction symmetric under the swap of its two signs cannot, on pain
of contradiction, prove that one of them is the matter the world is made of --- to do so would be to break
a symmetry it has not broken. The asymmetry between matter and antimatter that the world exhibits,
whatever its origin, is not in this construction's finite content, and the construction does not pretend
otherwise. It offers a trace symmetric in its two poles and an anchor that picks one, and tells the reader
plainly that the picking is theirs. Honesty about the convention is forced by the symmetry: a construction
that respects its own mirror has nothing to say about which side of the mirror is real.

\section{Reciprocal accounting}
\label{se:reciprocal-accounting}

An outside reader never sees everything; a reading resolves part of a configuration and leaves the rest
unresolved, and an honest account must track both. The construction proves the accounting that makes this
exact: the resolved part and the unresolved part sum to the whole. Whatever a reading manages to pin
down, plus whatever it leaves open, is precisely the total --- no more, accounting for everything, and no
less, hiding nothing. This is reciprocal in the strict sense that the two parts determine each other
against the fixed whole: to resolve more is to leave less unresolved, by exactly the amount resolved, so
the reader who knows the whole and the resolved knows the unresolved too.

It is worth being clear about why this accounting is reciprocal and not merely additive, because the word
carries the chapter's point. Additive bookkeeping would say the resolved and the unresolved happen to sum
to the whole, as two independent quantities that add up. Reciprocal bookkeeping says more: that the two
are locked against a fixed total, so that one is determined by the other, the resolved and the unresolved
two readings of a single partition rather than two separate amounts. This is why an outside reader who
resolves more thereby learns the unresolved exactly, with no further measurement --- the partition has
only one degree of freedom, and fixing the resolved fixes its complement. The whole is the conserved
quantity; the resolved and unresolved are how a given reading splits it. That conservation is what makes a
partial reading a reading at all rather than a fragment: it is always a reading of the whole, taken at a
particular resolution, with the remainder accounted for by the same conservation that fixes the part.

This is the seventeenth chapter's funge and tange seen from the outside. There, counting the funges was
the measurement and the tanges turned the crank; here, the resolved part is what the reading funges into
its count, and the unresolved part is the tange residue the reading could not turn into a reading. The
construction traces the boundary between them and calls it the truth-parity surface: the surface along
which the resolved truth and the unresolved residue meet, the parity between what a reading captures and
what escapes it. Every outside reading lives on this surface, dividing the whole into a counted part and a
residual part whose sizes are reciprocal. The orientation trace is one such reading --- it resolves the
net sign and leaves the internal detail unresolved --- and the reciprocal accounting is what guarantees
that the trace, partial as it is, still adds up against the whole it came from. A reading need not be
complete to be honest; it needs only to account for its own incompleteness, and the reciprocal sum is how
it does.

The reciprocal accounting is more than bookkeeping; it is what lets a partial reading be trusted. An
outside reader, seeing only the resolved part, might worry that the unresolved part hides something that
would overturn the reading --- that what escaped the measurement is not residue but a contradiction
waiting to surface. The reciprocal sum forecloses that worry by pinning the unresolved part exactly: it is
the whole minus the resolved, a definite quantity the reader can name even without resolving it further.
The unresolved is not a fog of unknown size but a measured remainder, and a measured remainder cannot hide
an arbitrary surprise; it can hold only as much as it is large. This is the outside counterpart of the
running integrator's certificate from the eighteenth chapter. There, the construction vouched for its
reading by running it; here, it vouches for a partial reading by accounting for what the reading missed,
so that the missing part is bounded and named rather than open. A reading you can trust is not one that
resolves everything but one whose unresolved remainder is itself under control.

\section{What is demonstrated, and what is assumed}
\label{se:demonstrated-assumed-ch25}

The split here is unusually instructive, because the chapter's one assumed thing is a convention rather
than a grant.

What is demonstrated is the trace, the dominance, and the accounting. The two orientation readings are
proved to be plus and minus one and to cancel, axiom-free, so the baseline is earned. The tally is proved
to have a unique dominant sign, so the deflection is well-defined. And the resolved and unresolved parts
are proved to sum to the whole, the reciprocal accounting exact and the truth-parity surface the boundary
between them. Each is a checked statement about finite readings.

\begin{quote}
\emph{A note on the convention.} The one thing this chapter takes without proving it is not a grant of
the usual kind --- not an oracle, not a completion --- but a convention: the choice of which orientation
to call matter. The construction is emphatic that it does not derive this. It proves that orientations
cancel, that a tally has a dominant sign, that an anchor fixes a definite reading; it does not prove that
the world's matter is its positive pole, and it claims no theorem tying its sign convention to the charge
convention of physics. The polarity of the trace is the outside reader's to set. This is a different
species of assumption from the standing grant: the grant is something the construction needs and cannot
build; the convention is something no construction could supply, because it is a labeling choice that
lives with the observer by its nature. Marking it as a convention rather than smuggling it in as a result
is the whole discipline of reading from outside.
\end{quote}

What is assumed, then, is the standing grant as before, plus this one convention now named as a
convention. With the outside stance established --- a real trace, an earned baseline, a conventional
polarity, and an accounting that closes against the whole --- the construction is ready for the harder
outside readings to come. The next chapter takes the deepest of them: read from outside, the interior
invariant that was the electron becomes a boundary obstruction, and the field equations that hold there
turn out to force a balance the outside reader can name.
