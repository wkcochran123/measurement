\chapter{Stationarity and the First Variation}
\label{chap:stationarity-first-variation}

The first part of the book built an instrument. This part uses it, and the change of purpose shows
even in the character of the work: where the earlier chapters invented custom machinery rung by rung,
the construction now settles into ordinary mathematics --- plain structures, integers, theorems proved
outright --- and the book settles with it. The question Part II opens with is the oldest one in
physics dressed in finite clothes. Of all the paths a measurement could take from where it starts to
where it ends, which one does it actually take? The answer the construction builds toward is the
variational one: the path that makes an action stationary. This chapter lays the foundation by saying,
finitely and exactly, what stationary means, what the stronger condition of flatness means, and how
the first variation --- the thing that vanishes at a stationary path --- is computed without ever
taking a derivative.

\section{The action, stationary and flat}
\label{se:the-action-stationary-and-flat}

The setting is deliberately spare. A path runs from a source to a target, and it is allowed one
intermediate waypoint --- the construction calls it a slip, a place the path may be made to pass
through on its way. An action assigns a cost to each segment of a path, and from it two totals follow.
The \emph{direct} cost is the cost of the single segment straight from source to target. The
\emph{composed} cost is the cost of the two segments that route through the slip: source to slip, then
slip to target. A path \emph{admits} its slip when these two are equal --- when routing through the
waypoint costs exactly what going straight would, so the slip lies, in effect, on the way.

It helps to see what this spare setup is a finite stand-in for. A continuous path is an unbroken curve
with infinitely many points along it, each free to move, and varying it means perturbing the whole
curve at once. The construction cannot hold infinitely many free points, so it pins the two endpoints
and exposes a single interior degree of freedom, the slip, standing in for the whole continuum of
intermediate positions. Asking how the action responds as the slip moves is the finite shadow of
asking how a functional responds as an entire path is perturbed --- one waypoint where the classical
theory has a function, one difference where it has a derivative. Everything the chapter proves about
the slip is, read back, a statement about the curve it abbreviates, and the abbreviation is exactly the
kind the book has been making since it replaced the real line with a sample.

Now hold the source and target fixed and vary the slip, watching the composed cost move. Two
conditions on the path can be read off, and the whole chapter turns on keeping them distinct. A path
is \emph{stationary} when its slip gives the lowest composed cost of any --- when no alternate
placement of the waypoint routes more cheaply. A path is \emph{flat} when the composed cost does not
change as the slip moves at all --- when every alternate placement costs exactly the same, so the
action cannot tell one slip from another. Stationary says \emph{no alternate is cheaper}; flat says
\emph{no alternate is even different}. The two are not the same condition, and the difference is the
seed of everything to come: a stationary path sits at the bottom of a valley, while a flat path sits
on a level floor the action cannot feel its way across.

The difference is worth one concrete contrast, because conflating the two is easy and costly. Imagine
an action whose composed cost, plotted against the position of the slip, traces a bowl: there is a
single lowest point, and the cost rises in every direction away from it. The slip at the bottom is
stationary --- no alternate is cheaper --- but it is emphatically not flat, because moving it even
slightly does change the cost, upward. Now imagine instead an action whose cost holds the same level
value across a whole stretch of slip positions: anywhere on that stretch is flat, the cost genuinely
unchanged by moving the slip within it, and every such point is therefore stationary as well, tied for
cheapest. Flatness is the rarer and stronger thing. A bowl has one stationary point and no flat ones; a
level floor is flat everywhere it is level, and stationary there into the bargain. When the
construction insists later on the closed-flat condition rather than mere stationarity, it is asking for
the level floor, not the bottom of the bowl --- a slip the action cannot detect, not merely one it
cannot cheapen.

Flatness is the stronger of the two, and the construction proves it. If every alternate slip yields
exactly the same cost, then in particular the current slip's cost is no greater than any other's, so a
flat path is automatically stationary --- the theorem is almost a tautology once the definitions are
set side by side, and that is the point: the strong, clean condition implies the weak, useful one.
There is a stronger condition still, which the climactic result of this part will rest on, that pairs
flatness with admissibility: a path that both admits its slip and is flat in it. The construction calls
this closed-flat, and it is the form in which stationarity will be cashed in when the single invariant
arrives. At the level of this chapter, the move to remember is the selection it licenses: among all the
paths the construction can write, it tanges out the stationary ones by exactly the trait of being flat
here --- it keeps the paths the action cannot detect a slip in, and sets the rest aside.

The admissibility half of the closed-flat condition is easy to overlook and worth a sentence. A path
admits its slip when routing through the waypoint costs exactly what going straight would --- when the
detour is no detour, the composed cost equal to the direct. Flatness alone says the action cannot tell
the slip's position; admissibility adds that the slip is not buying that indifference at the price of a
longer path. A path can be flat without admitting --- every alternate slip equally bad --- and the
construction wants more than that. Closed-flat asks for both at once: a slip the action cannot detect,
on a path that costs no more than the straight one. That pairing is what makes the later result usable,
because it certifies not merely that a path is stationary but that it is stationary \emph{and} as cheap
as the direct route, which is the only kind of stationary path a measurement has any reason to take.

The cleanest picture of all this is the one the harmonic oscillator draws. Read as a quadratic action,
its stationary path sits at the bottom of a parabola, and the bottom of a parabola has a flat
tangent: small displacements to either side cost nothing to first order, which is exactly flatness, and
the path is a minimum, which is exactly stationarity. The experiment under the oscillator's name makes
the same point without geometry, as a conjugate pair trading recorded distinction against predicted
change; the quantity that governs the trade is a quadratic form, and the stationary point of a
quadratic form is where its first-order part goes silent. That silence is the first variation
vanishing, and it is what the next section computes.

\section{The first variation}
\label{se:the-first-variation}

The first variation is the change in the action caused by a small move of the slip --- the composed
cost at an alternate placement minus the composed cost at the current one. In ordinary calculus this
change is captured by a derivative, the limit of that difference as the move shrinks to nothing. The
construction cannot take that limit exactly, and it does not pretend to. It works with the difference
itself, exactly, as a composed quantity it can compute outright, and flatness is then the flat
statement that this difference is identically zero --- not small, not vanishing to first order, but
equal to zero for every alternate slip whatsoever. There is no first-order term left over and no
remainder hiding behind it; the variation is simply gone. A path is stationary precisely when its
first variation vanishes, and flatness is the clean, fully checkable form that vanishing takes when it
holds in every direction at once.

It is worth being clear about why the construction works with a difference rather than a derivative,
because the choice is principled, not a workaround. A derivative is a limit, and a limit is a claim
about infinitely many ever-smaller steps that no finite device ever finishes taking; to assert one is
to assume a completed infinity of exactly the kind the early chapters refused. A difference, by
contrast, is a single finite subtraction the construction can perform and check. By stating flatness as
the vanishing of an honest difference for every alternate slip, the construction gets the full force of
a first-order condition --- the action does not change --- without ever invoking the limit that would
have made it an approximation. The price is that flatness, so defined, is a strong condition: it asks
for no change at all, in any direction, not merely no change to leading order. The reward is that when
it holds, it holds exactly, and the stationary path it certifies is certified by a computation rather
than promised by an analysis.

This is where the contravariant engine of the second chapter returns, now doing variational work. The
slips that the action cannot tell apart --- all the alternate placements that leave the composed cost
unchanged --- share a single characteristic, that the action is blind to them, and the construction
funges them together into one object: the kernel of the action, the equivalence class of directions
along which nothing happens. To build a number by a cut was to decide, against an entire domain, a
below and an above; to find a stationary path is the same move turned on the action --- decide, against
the whole space of slips, which ones the action can feel and which it cannot, and quotient out the
ones it cannot. The flat directions funged into the kernel are the contravariant complement to the few
directions in which the action actually varies, and reading an action is reading what survives once its
kernel has been divided away.

A concrete case makes the vanishing visible. Let the cost of a segment be its length, and let a path
run from a source to a target through a slip. If the slip is placed anywhere on the straight line
between source and target, the two segments together have exactly the length of the direct line ---
the path admits its slip --- and, crucially, sliding the waypoint \emph{along} that line changes
nothing: every on-line placement gives the same total length. Along the line the action is flat, its
first variation identically zero, and the path is stationary. Lift the slip off the line and the total
length grows; that is a direction the action can feel, a direction outside the kernel. The straight
path is selected not because some derivative was set to zero by calculus, but because it is the one
whose slip can be moved freely without the action noticing --- flat in the kernel, stationary by the
theorem, and admitting its waypoint exactly. The classical calculus of variations does the same thing
in the limit: the experiment under the name of minimizing variations forms the functional whose value
is an integral over a path, perturbs the path by a vanishing displacement that fixes the endpoints, and
sets the resulting first variation to zero. The construction's finite version replaces the vanishing
displacement with an exact difference and the integral with a finite cost, and arrives at the same
gate --- the gate the next chapter walks through to reach the Euler--Lagrange equation.

One thing the first variation leaves behind is worth flagging now, because the rest of the part is built
on it. When the first variation vanishes, the action's behavior near the stationary path is no longer
governed by how it changes --- it does not, to first order --- but by how that change itself changes:
the second variation, the curvature of the action at its flat point. A bowl and a saddle can both be
stationary, both have a vanishing first variation, and be told apart only by their second variation,
positive in every direction for the bowl and mixed for the saddle. The construction has already met this
object once, as the interpolant of a spline --- the second variation, the weak derivative, the curvature
left behind once the bending is done. That it keeps reappearing is not a coincidence. The second
variation is the single invariant this entire part is named for, and the first variation's work,
finished in this chapter, is to clear it a stage to stand on.

\section{What is demonstrated, and what is assumed}
\label{se:demonstrated-assumed-ch9}

This chapter opens the second part, so the separation of built from assumed also marks the change in
how the construction works: from here, most of what it claims it proves outright.

What is demonstrated is a small, complete, finite theory of stationarity. There is an action over
paths with a waypoint, with a direct and a composed cost; there are the conditions of admitting a
slip, of being stationary in it, and of being flat in it; and there are the theorems that bind them ---
that flatness implies stationarity, and that the closed-flat condition, flatness together with
admissibility, delivers a stationary admissible path. These are not models or analogies. They are
proved statements about finite objects, and the first variation is an exact computed difference, not
an approximated derivative.

\begin{quote}
\emph{A note on the labels.} The mathematics here is clean, but two of its names are bridges. Calling
the finite cost an \emph{action} and its waypoint a \emph{slip} borrows the vocabulary of physics for
objects that are, in themselves, just costs and coordinates; the theorems hold of the costs whatever we
call them. The larger bridge is the variational principle itself --- the claim that the path a
measurement \emph{actually} takes is the stationary one. The construction proves how stationary, flat,
and admissible relate; that nature selects the stationary path is the modeling assumption laid over
those relations, the same assumption physics has run on since Maupertuis, named here rather than
assumed silently.
\end{quote}

What is assumed, then, is that principle and the physical reading of the costs --- and, for this
chapter, nothing heavier. That last clause is worth marking, because it will not hold for long. The
next chapter takes the first variation to its destination, the Euler--Lagrange equation, and there the
construction will meet the one assumption it cannot discharge by computing --- the single granted step
that the whole constructive discipline of Part I was built to make visible. For now the ground is
solid: a stationary path is a flat one, flatness is a first variation that is exactly zero, and the
flat directions are a kernel the action divides away.
