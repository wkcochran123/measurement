\chapter{The Number Tower up to the Reals}
\label{chap:the-number-tower}

The previous chapter produced a number from a Fact and the ability to tell two carriers apart. That
is the bottom rung. This chapter climbs. From counting it builds ratios, from ratios sequences, from
sequences limits, and at the top it reaches for the continuum --- and there it stops, because the
continuum is the one thing a finite construction is never allowed to finish. What arrives at the top
is not the real line but a \emph{sample} of it: a finite symbol that stands in for a number it can
only ever approach. The chapter is the story of that climb, of the single ordering idea that repeats
at every rung, and of the honest gap that opens at the summit between what is built and what is named.

Two threads run the length of it, so it helps to name them in advance. The first is a braid: each new
kind of number is produced in the same three-part move, a process that does some work, a property that
certifies the work was admissible, and the new kind of number that results --- and then the new kind
of number becomes the raw material for the next turn of the braid. The second is a single comparison
rule, present in every order from the counting numbers to the samples, that already contains the
distinction between two ways of measuring. We meet both at the first rung.

\section{The braid that climbs}
\label{se:the-braid-that-climbs}

Counting is not as simple as it looks, and the development is careful to say which counting it means.
There are two ways to count a collection. One is to iterate: to walk along it, laying down one more
unit at each step, producing an enumeration. The other is to decompose: to break the collection into
like pieces and ask how many pieces there are, producing a cardinality. The two coincide on simple
finite heaps and diverge the moment the pieces themselves start to demand description, and the first
rung of the tower --- the counting numbers --- is built deliberately on iteration, with decomposition
held back for later. A counting number is assembled from the Facts and base numbers of the previous
chapter, and counting proper is the act of stepping a carrier forward and recording that one more
event has occurred.

This is the first full turn of the braid, and the shape is worth fixing because it recurs unchanged
all the way up. There is a \emph{process} --- here a counting process, which holds a carrier, a
running count, and a step that advances it. There is a \emph{property} --- here admissibility, which
certifies that a proposed comparison ran to completion without turning up a counterexample, so that
the order ``remembered which way was forward'' even as what is being counted changes. And there is the
resulting \emph{kind of number}, the counting numbers themselves. Every rung above this one is the
same: a process that does the work, a property that certifies it, a new kind of number that results
and then feeds the next rung. The tower is not a list of definitions; it is one move, performed over
and over, each time on the output of the last.

The order on the counting numbers introduces the second thread, and it is the more important one. To
compare two counting numbers you look at their leading Facts and at the magnitudes beneath them, and
the rule splits on whether the two Facts decided the same way. If they agree, the comparison is passed
to the magnitudes \emph{in the same direction} --- the larger magnitude is the larger number. If they
disagree, the comparison is passed to the magnitudes \emph{reversed} --- the larger magnitude is now
the smaller number. The development calls these two branches covariant and contravariant, and the
words are exact. A same-sign comparison runs covariantly, with the magnitude; an opposite-sign
comparison runs contravariantly, against it. This is not a quirk of the counting numbers. The same
two-branch rule --- agree and run with, disagree and run against --- appears in the order at every rung
of the tower, and by the time we reach the top it will have grown into a claim about measurement
itself: that there are exactly two ways to learn a quantity, one that reads it off passively through a
carrier and one that pushes against it to find where it gives way.

What kind of fact is a count, though? Here the development leans on Aristotle, and the experiment filed
under his name draws a line the rest of the chapter respects. Aristotle separated a thing's substance
from its predicates: what a thing \emph{is} comes before how it is classified, ordered, or named, and
no change in description brings a new thing into being. Transposed into the setting of records, the
events in a ledger are the substance and the enumeration laid over them is the predicate. The factual
content lives in the events: which distinctions actually occurred. The enumeration --- the particular
way they are indexed, the order they are listed in, the alphabet their symbols are drawn from --- is
descriptive structure, and reindexing it, renaming its symbols, or rescaling its display changes the
manner of expression without adding or removing a single recorded distinction. The counting numbers,
on this reading, are not themselves facts about the world; they are an admissible way of expressing
facts that reside, underneath, in the bare record of what was distinguished from what. Holding that
line --- counts express, events record --- is what keeps the tower honest as it grows, because every
rung above adds expressive power, and expressive power is exactly the thing one can mistake for
content.

\section{Beyond binary}
\label{se:beyond-binary}

The base number speaks in two symbols, true and false. The world it is asked to describe does not, and
the next rungs of the tower are the development teaching its numbers to carry finer distinctions
without smuggling in any structure it has not built. A ratio is the first step: a number expressed as
a relation between two others, which lets the construction name magnitudes that no single string of
binary digits lands on exactly. A sequence is the next: an indexed run of numbers, the raw form in
which an unfolding quantity gets recorded one entry at a time. With each step the construction adds a
property to the braid --- countability, that the entries can be put in correspondence with the
counting numbers and so enumerated; then encoding, that distinctions can be packed into a carrier and
pulled back out by refinement --- and at no point does it help itself to a completed infinite object.
A sequence is always, at any moment, a finite record with a rule for extending it.

The experiment that carries this rung is Fessenden and Shannon's, and it is the cleanest possible
statement of what ``beyond binary'' is allowed to mean. Fessenden's early radio showed that voice ---
continuous, analog, nothing like a bit --- could be carried by partitioning a physical response into a
finite set of admissible distinctions, enough to reconstruct speech at the far end. What travelled was
never the waveform itself; it was a structured modulation that an instrument could discretize and
decode. Shannon then stripped the idea to its bones and named the thing that did the work: a channel,
a refinement structure that supports several symbolic distinctions at once, independently of whatever
physical form happens to realize them. That is precisely the license the tower needs. Going beyond two
symbols does not require a continuum hiding inside the carrier; it requires only that a finite set of
distinctions can be embedded and recovered by refinement, with distinct decompositions allowed to
coexist as long as they stay disjoint. The development's numbers become multi-valued in exactly
Shannon's sense --- a channel's worth of admissible distinctions --- and not a hair more. The carrier
gains capacity; it does not gain a hidden infinity.

It is worth pausing on a small surprise the development records here: the same pile of Facts now stands
for three kinds of number at once. Read one way it is a binary string, read another a counting number,
read a third a ratio, and each reading comes with its own ordering. One construction, three numbers,
three orders, all sharing the covariant--contravariant comparison of the previous section. This triple
reading is not a trick; it is the reason the climb can continue without the tower toppling. Because a
single finite object is already carrying several arithmetic identities, the construction can move
between counting, proportion, and sequence without ever having to build a bridge from one completed
number system to another. It just reads the same record under a finer description.

\section{The continuum you can only approach}
\label{se:the-continuum-you-can-only-approach}

Now the climb reaches for its limit, and the careful language of the development becomes the whole
point. A limit, in ordinary mathematics, is a completed thing: the number a sequence converges to,
sitting at the end of an infinite process as though the process had finished. The construction cannot
say that, because it never finishes anything. So it says less, and means more. A limiting process is
modelled as a crowd of terms agreeing to stop wandering apart --- a Cauchy condition stated without
the ontology of a destination. The process holds a current ratio, its best finite value so far, and a
separate record of where the terms are tending, and it inches forward, as the development puts it, by
whole in the sequence and by part in the value: one more entry recorded, the approximation refined one
notch finer. At no stage is there a completed real number in hand. There is a finite record and a rule
for tightening it.

A concrete look at the representation shows why the gap at the top is unavoidable rather than a failure
of nerve. In the development's binary numerals the number one is the all-true string, every digit
deciding the same way; the smallest magnitude the construction can represent at a given depth is the
all-false string, every digit deciding the other. Read as whole numbers there is no zero among them ---
one is the least of the whole numbers and nothing lies beneath it. Read as parts, the all-false string
is the smallest representable fraction, and the construction simply \emph{defines} that to be zero,
exploiting the very ambiguity between reading a numeral as a whole and reading it as a part. The same
ambiguity is what turns the familiar identity that a string of nines equals the next whole number from
a paradox into a plain fact about representation: two different finite descriptions can name the same
limit, and the construction treats agreement in the limit, not identity of description, as the thing
that counts. The reals it climbs toward are exactly the numbers at which description runs out and only
the limit remains.

What, then, is the number at the top? The development gives it a deliberately modest name and a
deliberately strange construction. It calls the object a residue: what is left over once a process has
almost explained itself, the difference between the story the model tells and the measurement actually
taken. And the number that carries a residue --- the construction's stand-in for a single real number
--- is a \emph{sample}: an admissible symbol for one normal number, built as a pair, a limit in time
and a limit in value, ordered by the same covariant--contravariant rule now applied across both
coordinates at once. A sample does not complete the continuum and does not pretend to. In the
development's own blunt gloss, a sample proves nothing; it gives the next stage something small enough
to work with. The real number arrives as a finite token standing in for an object that the
construction has shown how to approach as closely as one likes and has pointedly declined to claim it
has reached.

It is worth saying exactly what act produces such a number, because it is the act on which the rest of
this book turns. The construction has two ways of handling a characteristic it can decide. It can
\emph{tange}: single out the one member that bears the characteristic, individuating it from everything
else by the decision that holds of it alone. And it can \emph{funge}: pool together everything that
shares a characteristic, collecting like with like into one bagged object, indifferent to whatever else
distinguishes its members. Tange selects; funge pools. A real number is built from both at once, as a
cut. To specify a real is to decide, for every rational, whether it falls below the number being named:
tange the rationals where that decision returns true, funge all the rest together as the ones where it
returns false, and the dividing line between the two heaps simply \emph{is} the real. Nothing else is;
the number is that decision, made everywhere along the domain of rationals.

This is construction running in the opposite direction from the one that built the counting numbers.
Those were assembled covariantly, by accumulation --- start from a floor and lay down one more unit,
and one more, raising the number from below. A cut is the contravariant move: it fixes a number not by
accumulating it but by deciding it against an entire domain laid out in advance, carving the rationals
into a below and an above. So the construction has its own covariant and contravariant --- accumulate
versus cut --- and they are the building counterpart of the same/different cadence that runs through
every comparison in the tower; the two covariances, of building and of comparing, are faces of one
rule. The decisive point is that tange and funge are things the construction actually \emph{does},
because every characteristic it uses is decidable. Selecting the member that bears a property and
pooling the ones that share it are not powers it must assume it has; they are operations it carries
out, one decision at a time. That is exactly why the continuum can be \emph{approached} by finite
deciding rather than seized whole: a cut across all the rationals is an unbounded amount of deciding,
so the construction never completes one, only ever tightening a finite stretch of it. That is the
reason, underneath the Fourier--Nyquist density law met just below, that a finite record can come
arbitrarily close to a real and never arrive.

Two experiments hold this summit between them, and they pull in opposite and complementary directions.
The first is Archimedes and Proust's. Archimedes measured by embedding finite acts of comparison
inside an idealized continuous geometry --- continua that are never themselves recorded, but that
furnish a smooth framework for reasoning about what the balance reports. The instrument commits few
facts; the mathematics carries the burden of continuity. Proust stands at the opposite pole: his law
of definite proportions grounds chemical fact in whole numbers, refusing any appeal to intermediate
fractional composition, so that mixtures which look continuous are licensed as facts only at integer
ratios. Put side by side, the two mark the exact division the tower has been building toward.
Continuity is an expressive scaffold --- legitimate, useful, never recorded --- and facthood is
discrete. The development keeps the scaffold and records only the integers, which is why its real
numbers can be samples without apology: the continuum was always the framework one reasons in, never
the thing the ledger holds.

The second experiment is Fourier and Nyquist's, and it supplies the law that makes a sample
trustworthy rather than wishful. Fourier showed that a complicated quantity can be decomposed into
orthogonal components; Nyquist found the condition under which that decomposition can be inverted ---
under which the original can actually be recovered from a record of samples. The lesson is that
decomposition is cheap and recovery is not: no component may be reconstructed unless the record commits
its distinctions densely enough to support the inversion. This is the precise sense in which the
tower's summit is honest. The sample at the top is allowed to stand in for a real number exactly to
the degree that the refinement beneath it is dense enough to pin that number down; refine too coarsely
and the sample names nothing recoverable. Recovery depends not on the continuity of the underlying
process --- which is never recorded --- but on whether the finite refinements committed to the ledger
are sufficient. The continuum is approached, and the rate of approach is itself subject to a law.

\section{What is demonstrated, and what is assumed}
\label{se:demonstrated-assumed-ch2}

As in the previous chapter, the experimental library's own discipline supplies the template for the
close: each experiment is a finite model that states how much it is entitled to claim and is candid
about what it does not. The tower invites a particularly tempting overclaim --- it really does look
like it has built the real numbers --- so the separation matters more here than anywhere yet.

What is demonstrated is a graded sequence of kinds of number, each produced by the same process /
property / type braid and each carrying a total, computable order. The orders all obey one comparison
rule, covariant on agreement and contravariant on disagreement, and that rule is a genuine theorem of
the construction, not a stipulation laid on from outside. The limiting machinery is real: there is a
finite Cauchy-style process, a well-defined notion of residue, and a sample object --- a real specified by a decidable cut,
tange below and funge above --- that is a perfectly concrete finite symbol with a perfectly concrete
order. Everything in the tower can be computed,
compared, and checked, and none of it requires a completed infinity at any step.

\begin{quote}
\emph{A note on the labels.} The word \emph{real number} is the heaviest label in this chapter, and it
is a modeling choice, not a theorem. What the construction builds is a sample: a finite token, a pair
of approaching limits, with a residue attached. To call that token a real number is to assert that the
finite thing in hand stands faithfully for the continuous thing it approaches --- and that assertion
is licensed by the Fourier--Nyquist law only to the extent the refinement is dense enough to recover
what is named. The continuum itself is never constructed; it is the expressive scaffold of
Archimedes, reasoned within and never recorded. Likewise, reading the covariant branch as passive
measurement and the contravariant branch as active measurement is an interpretation laid over a single
comparison rule. The arithmetic is earned; the physical and Platonic names are bridges, and they carry
the weight of their analogies and no more.
\end{quote}

What is assumed is therefore the same kind of bridge met in the first chapter, now bearing more load.
We assume that a finite sample, refined densely enough, may be treated as the real number it
approaches --- that the gap the construction leaves open between approximation and limit is one a
working scientist is entitled to step across, having been told exactly how wide it is. And we assume
that the two branches of every order are worth reading as the two faces of measurement, the passive and
the active, rather than as a bookkeeping convenience. These are the assumptions that turn a tower of
finite symbols into a usable number system. Note what is conspicuously \emph{not} among them: any
power to choose. The selection and pooling the construction leans on --- tange and funge --- are
operations it performs by deciding, never capacities it must be granted, so the continuum is approached
without assuming a single choice the construction could not itself carry out. With the numbers in hand, up to and including a disciplined
stand-in for the reals, the construction is ready to put them to work --- and the first work it takes
up is the one quantity it has so far only gestured at, the one the counting process kept making us wait
for: time.
