\chapter{The Signal}
\label{chap:the-signal}

% EPISODE COVERAGE: Episodes 3 and 4 -- TuringProcess, REPRESENTABLE,
% ChaitinsNumberSequence, PHYSICAL, OBSERVED, GaugeProcess, MEASURABLE,
% Jar, GUNGAN
% KEY CONCEPT: The measurement boundary. Not everything can be decided.

A speedometer is often treated as a friendly instrument. It gives one number,
prints it on a dial, and lets the driver pretend that speed is a simple
property of the car. But the instrument is doing something stranger. It is not
touching speed. It is listening for pulses, refusing some disturbances,
counting others, smoothing the result through a clock, and then deciding when
the needle is allowed to move. The number on the dial is the visible end of a
private trial.

Chapter 2 used the speedometer to think about the number ladder: whole pulses,
ratios against time, progressions of readings, and settling values. Chapter 3
used thresholds to ask what may enter the ledger. Chapter 4 returns to the
same dashboard, but now the question has changed. The wheel sends a stream of
events into the sensor. Some events are clean. Some arrive during dead time.
Some are too faint to distinguish from electrical noise. Some contradict the
previous trend. Some would require the instrument to decide more than its
mechanism can decide. The speedometer must not merely count. It must decide
what counts as a signal.

That decision is not passive. The sensor contains a little state machine. It
waits, detects a crossing, records a pulse, advances its internal state, and
waits again. The instrument can be described as a program: read the present
condition, update the register, compare the elapsed time, move the reported
needle. The program is not printed on the dial, but it is there. A purely
mechanical speedometer has its program in gears and springs. An electronic one
has it in firmware. Either way, the instrument has a rule for what the next
state should be.

Once the speedometer is understood as a state machine, the comfortable picture
of measurement begins to crack. A state machine may fail to produce an answer.
It may wait too long for a pulse. It may receive pulses faster than it can
process them. It may enter a diagnostic state. It may be unable to decide
whether the next flicker is a real tooth on the wheel or a spike induced by a
nearby wire. The dial hides this because a consumer instrument is designed to
hide hesitation. The device in this book does the opposite. It makes the
hesitation formal.

The halting boundary is already present in the car. Imagine asking the
speedometer a question it cannot answer from its own stream: is the vehicle
following a road that eventually returns to its starting point? The pulses
alone do not contain that answer. They contain motion, not the future of the
route. Even a simpler question can strain the instrument: has the vehicle
reached a final speed? The sensor can watch the needle settle, but it can
never prove that no later acceleration will occur. It can report that further
comparison has gone quiet inside the chosen window. It cannot own the whole
future.

Noise makes this boundary visible. A speedometer without noise is a textbook
fiction. Real sensors chatter. Magnets age. Teeth wear. Wires pick up
interference. Road vibration shakes the pickup. The pulse train is not a
perfect sentence written by the wheel. It is a signal arriving through a body.
The instrument must therefore use noise structurally. It must know the
difference between a pulse, a glitch, a missing pulse, and a pulse whose timing
is plausible only if the previous reading was wrong. Signal and noise are not
two separate substances. They are two roles a mark can play inside a formal
process.

This is what Chapter 4 calls a signal. A signal is not merely an event with a
large amplitude. It is an event that can be represented, processed, and carried
forward without asking the instrument to decide what it cannot decide. The
signal is admitted through computation. If the computation returns, the
instrument has something it can use. If the computation does not return, the
instrument has discovered a boundary in its own ability to report.

The gauge enters when the instrument must choose a reference. A wheel sensor
does not measure absolute motion in the universe. It measures rotation relative
to a pickup, counts pulses relative to a clock, and reports speed relative to a
calibration. Change the tire radius and the same pulse frequency means a
different road speed. Change the clock and the same count produces a different
rate. Change the filtering window and the same raw signal produces a different
needle response. A gauge is this disciplined choice of reference: not a lie,
but a way of making a reading possible.

The quantum jar enters because some readings cannot be forced into a single
class before measurement. A marginal pulse may be neither safely accepted nor
safely rejected until the processing context decides what it becomes. An
instrument can hold such an unresolved state if the formal system gives it a
place to live. The point is not that a speedometer is secretly a quantum
experiment. The point is that unresolved alternatives are not always ignorance
to be swept away. Sometimes they are the honest state of the record before the
measurement process closes.

The chapter therefore follows the signal inward. A closed study becomes a
process that can run. Running meets a halting boundary. The boundary sounds
like noise from inside the instrument. A gauge chooses the reference that makes
a present reading possible. The jar holds unresolved states until a process has
earned the right to resolve them.

The speedometer keeps that sequence close to the hand. The dial looks simple
because the instrument has already run the harder test: what in this stream can
be used as signal?

The old cable-driven speedometer makes the lesson tactile. A rotating cable
spins a magnet inside a cup. The magnet does not touch the cup, but it drags
the cup through eddy currents. A spring resists the drag. The needle settles
where magnetic pull and spring tension balance. Even before electronics, the
reading was not direct contact with speed. It was a contest among rotation,
coupling, resistance, and settling. The dial showed the equilibrium of a
process.

An electronic speedometer makes the same contest explicit in logic. A sensor
produces a pulse train. A counter counts pulses in a time window. A filter
rejects implausible changes. A display routine decides how quickly to move the
visible number. Some systems also compare wheel speeds against one another for
traction control or anti-lock braking. The speed reading is now part of a
larger computational ecosystem. It is not only information for the driver. It
is a signal other systems may act on.

That is why Chapter 4 matters for the rest of the book. Once a reading can be
used as input to another process, every hidden uncertainty in the reading can
propagate. A noisy pulse can become a false acceleration. A bad calibration can
become a wrong braking decision. A delayed computation can become a late
response. The ledger must therefore learn not only how to produce a number, but
how to certify the computational path by which that number becomes a signal for
the next process.

The chapter is about this certification. It asks when a closed record can be
run, when running returns an answer, when failure to return becomes part of the
record, when noise is a feature of the channel, when a local reference makes an
event measurable, and when an unresolved state must be carried honestly. The
speedometer's needle is a small public answer to all of those private
questions.

The order matters, but it need not be mystical. First build a record. Then run
it. Then learn where running fails. Then filter the noisy boundary, report
relative to a reference, and resolve only what the process has earned the right
to resolve.

\section{The Turing Machine Arrives}
\label{se:turing-machine-arrives}

Chapter 3 ended with closure. A \lean{Study} could begin as a hypothesis,
receive data, and return as a closed account. That was already computational in
the everyday sense: an open record went into a process, and another record came
out. But it was not yet a theory of computation. It did not yet ask what it
means for the ledger itself to be run as a program.

That is the turn Chapter 4 takes. The number ladder gave the ledger forms. The
permission chain gave the ledger rules. Closure gave the ledger a completed
study. A Turing machine asks a harder question: can the completed study be
treated as an instruction, and can an instruction be carried forward as a state
of the same formal instrument?

The classical story begins with a tape, a head, a finite alphabet, a finite
state, and a table of rules. The head reads a symbol, writes a symbol, moves
left or right, and changes state. This abstraction is powerful because it does
not care whether the machine is made of gears, relays, vacuum tubes, silicon,
or pencil marks. If the process can be written as a finite rule table acting
on discrete symbols, it belongs to the same computational universe.

The device cannot accept that story in its ordinary form. It has spent three
chapters refusing to accept bare symbols. A mark had to be carried by a
\lean{CarrierProcess}. A difference had to become \lean{DISTINGUISHABLE}. A
count had to be admitted, bounded, encoded, reduced to residue, sampled,
observed, repeated, and closed. The tape alphabet of the ordinary Turing
machine looks innocent only because ordinary mathematics lets the symbols
arrive without receipts. This project does not.

So the Turing machine does not arrive as a primitive machine over a primitive
alphabet. It arrives as a wrapper around the whole stack already built. The
machine is allowed to compute only because the ledger has already earned the
forms it computes with.

The excerpt shows only the new fields. The inherited parameter block and
constraint stack are the one established in the previous chapters.

\begin{leancode}
computational\_process :\\
\quad ComputationalProcess Value Carrier\\
program : Study\\
state : Computation\\
compute? : Computation -> Option Study\\
turing\_step? : Computation -> Computation
\end{leancode}

The first field is the bridge back to Chapter 3. A \lean{TuringProcess} does
not replace \lean{ComputationalProcess}; it uses one. The closed study remains
the substrate. Computation is not a new magic above the ledger. It is what
happens when the closure machinery is placed inside a state machine and asked
to advance.

The next two fields name the two sides of that advance. A \lean{program} is a
\lean{Study}. This matters. The program is not merely a string of syntax. It is
a record that has already passed through the ledger's discipline. It is a
hypothesis or a chain of data, not a naked inscription. The \lean{state} is a
\lean{Computation}: the current formal condition of the machine while the
program is being used.

This is the first major reversal in the book. Earlier chapters asked how an
instrument can turn the world into a record. Here the record begins to act on
itself. A closed study can become a program. A program can sit inside a
computation. A computation can ask the same closure machinery to produce the
next study. The ledger has not escaped measurement. It has folded measurement
into the operation of a machine.

The field \lean{compute?} marks the point where output becomes uncertain. Its
result type is \lean{Option Study}. The machine may return a study, or it may
return nothing. This is not yet the halting problem in full force. That comes
in the next section. But the shape of the problem has already appeared. A
computation is not guaranteed to yield an observed study merely because a
program was supplied. The possibility of no output must be part of the type.

In the Lean file, \lean{compute?} distinguishes a program state from a compute
state. A program state can hand back the study it carries. A compute state
must pass its current study through the closure operation of the underlying
\lean{ComputationalProcess}. The interpretation of a symbol and the update of
machine state are therefore different acts. Turing's abstraction depends on
that separation. Reading a mark is not the same as changing the machine because
of the mark.

\lean{turing\_step?} makes the same separation explicit from the other side. A
program state is already a program state, so it remains itself. A compute state
is advanced by closing its current study and installing the result as the next
piece of machine state. The step function does not know the future. It knows
how to take one legal step from the present configuration.

\begin{sidebar}{Alan Turing and the Universal Machine}
\label{sb:turing-universal-machine}
Turing's 1936 construction showed how a finite description of a machine could
itself be placed on a tape and read by another machine. The universal machine
does not need a different physics for every program. It needs a way to encode a
program as data and a rule for interpreting that data. Chapter 4 translates
that insight into the ledger's language: a closed study can become the program
read by a formal process.
\end{sidebar}

The arrival of \lean{TuringProcess} is therefore not just the arrival of a
famous model. It is a stress test for the whole construction. If the earlier
number forms were ornamental, they would disappear here. If the permission
chain were only metaphor, the Turing machine would ignore it. Instead the
machine is parameterized over the same carrier and inherits the same promises.
The computational state machine is not allowed to pretend that symbols explain
themselves.

This also changes the reader's expectation of what a program is. A program is
not a command shouted at an obedient machine. It is a proposed continuation of
a record. The machine may be deterministic, but the record still has to be
legible to the machine that is asked to run it. A malformed program is not only
bad syntax; it is a failed representation. A program whose state cannot be
closed has not yet become an admissible computation. The familiar image of a
machine following instructions therefore has to be read through the ledger: the
instruction is itself a measured artifact, and execution is the attempt to
carry that artifact forward without breaking the promises that made it
representable.

This is why the next class is \lean{REPRESENTABLE}. It names a promise more
delicate than computation. A process may be able to step without being able to
represent what its step means. A compiler may manipulate syntax without any
guarantee that the syntax carries a stable value for the instrument. The
ledger needs a way to say: for this carrier, facts and studies can be made to
speak through computations.

\begin{leancode}
calculation\_process : TuringProcess Value Carrier\\
representable? : \(\forall\) (\_ : Fact) (s : Study),\\
\quad \(\exists\) (\_ : Computation),\\
\quad calculation\_process.computational\_process.closure s = s
\end{leancode}

The class is doing two things at once. First, it installs a
\lean{TuringProcess} as the calculation process. Second, it asserts a
representability condition over facts and studies. For every fact and every
study, there exists a computation witnessing that the study can sit fixed under
the closure operation. The displayed equality is strange on purpose. It says
that representation is not merely the production of a different object. It is
the ability of a study to survive closure as itself.

That is the ledger's version of the Church-Turing thesis. Not the historical
claim in its broad philosophical form, and not a proof that every effective
procedure has been captured. It is a local typeclass promise: whatever this
instrument can treat as an effective calculation must pass through the
\lean{TuringProcess} installed for this carrier. Computability has become a
contract inside the measurement tower.

The contract matters because representation is where mathematics often hides
its most expensive assumption. To say that a number can be computed is already
to say that the number can be represented. To say that a state can be updated
is already to say that the state can be read. Ordinary notation blurs this
because it writes the symbol, the represented value, and the reader's
understanding of the symbol in the same mark. The device separates them. The
mark must be distinguished. It must be admitted. It must be encoded. It must
settle. Only then may it become something a Turing process can treat as part
of a calculation.

That separation keeps the chapter honest. A Turing machine is often introduced
as if it were pure syntax. Here it is introduced as a measured syntax. Its
symbols are not heavenly tokens. They are the residue of previous
comparisons, carried into a state machine that may or may not return an
answer. This is why the section is called the arrival of the Turing machine,
not the foundation of the Turing machine. The foundation has already been
laid.

The next section asks what happens when this machine meets the boundary it
cannot cross. Once studies can be represented as computations, the ledger is
allowed to ask whether a computation finishes. That question is not merely a
technical inconvenience. It is the first place where the device must admit
that representation has a horizon.

\section{The Halting Boundary}
\label{se:halting-boundary}

A Turing process gives the ledger a state machine. It does not give the ledger
omniscience. The moment the record can be run as a program, the ledger inherits
the oldest wound in computation: some programs finish, some do not, and no
general procedure can decide which case holds for every program. This is not a
failure of patience. It is a boundary in the form of the question.

A speedometer makes the boundary less abstract. Suppose the instrument is
asked to report the current speed. That is a local question. It watches pulse
frequency over a window, compares the count with the clock, and moves the
needle. Now suppose it is asked whether the car will ever again pass exactly
through this speed. The same pulse stream is not enough. The instrument can
continue watching, and if the speed reappears it can say yes. But if the speed
never reappears, no finite waiting time lets the speedometer certify that the
answer is no for all future time. The question has crossed from measurement
into nontermination.

The code names this boundary with \lean{ChaitinsNumberSequence}. The excerpt
shows the two shapes the ledger can hold.

\begin{leancode}
| halting :\\
\quad Fact -> Computation -> ChaitinsNumberSequence\\
| nonhalting :\\
\quad Fact -> Computation -> Computation ->\\
\quad ChaitinsNumberSequence
\end{leancode}

The constructors are not symmetrical decoration. The halting case carries a
fact and a computation. The nonhalting case carries a fact, a computation, and
another computation. The extra computation is the continuing trace: the process
has not closed, so the record must carry not only the program under study but
the next unresolved computational state. Nonhalting is not represented as
nothing. It is represented as an ongoing burden.

The order on this sequence is deliberately limited. Halting computations can
be compared by their computations. A halting state sits below a nonhalting
state because a finished record is easier to use than an unfinished one. A
nonhalting state cannot be placed below a halting one. Two nonhalting states
can be compared only by the computation they have reached so far. The ledger
can order partial evidence. It cannot turn partial evidence into a completed
verdict by wanting one.

This is where Chaitin enters. Chaitin's halting probability, often called
Omega, is the probability that a randomly chosen program halts. It is a real
number in the mathematical sense, but its bits are algorithmically random. To
know enough bits of Omega is to know answers to halting questions. That is
exactly what no general algorithm can provide. The number exists, but existence
is not the same as computable access.

The instrument lesson is severe. A real number may be nameable without being
measurable by a given process. A program may be written without the ledger
being able to know whether it will return. A signal may be present without
being representable as a closed study. Chapter 4 does not treat these as
philosophical embarrassments. It turns them into the next formal threshold.

\begin{sidebar}{The Halting Problem Without Programs}
\label{sb:halting-without-programs}
Imagine a book that may or may not contain a sentence saying that the book
itself will never mention its own title again. If the sentence appears, the
book has answered one question by creating another. A procedure that tries to
settle every such self-referential case will eventually meet a book whose
answer depends on the procedure's own behavior. The halting problem is the
computational version of this trap: a general decider can be turned against
itself.
\end{sidebar}

The next class is \lean{PHYSICAL}. Its name is intentionally blunt. The point
is not that physics is merely computation. The point is that a physical
measurement process must live with the halting boundary. If an instrument is a
state machine running over measured symbols, then some requested outputs lie
outside what the machine can certify. The physical is not the domain where all
questions become answerable. It is the domain where answerability is bounded
by process.

The excerpt shows the new fields and predicate.

\begin{leancode}
noisy\_process : NoisyProcess Value Carrier\\
threshold : ChaitinsNumberSequence\\
halted? : ChaitinsNumberSequence ->\\
\quad ChaitinsNumberSequence -> Prop
\end{leancode}

\lean{PHYSICAL} uses a noisy process and a Chaitin threshold. That threshold is
the line the instrument cannot cross by computation alone. A speedometer can
report present speed because its process returns a bounded reading. It cannot
settle every future-speed question because the question asks for more than a
bounded process can provide. The threshold is not laziness. It is the formal
shape of the instrument's horizon.

The predicate \lean{halted?} is wonderfully austere. When the second sequence
is a halting case, it returns true. When the second sequence is nonhalting, it
returns false. The predicate does not solve the halting problem. It reports the
status of a sequence that has already been shaped as halting or nonhalting.
The ledger can use a witnessed halt. It cannot manufacture one for arbitrary
programs.

This distinction matters for measurement. Instruments often report with the
authority of a number, but the authority belongs only to the process that
produced the number. If a process halts with a reading, the reading may enter
the ledger. If the process does not halt, the failure is itself part of the
record. A failed or endless computation is not invisible. It tells the ledger
that the requested measurement has exceeded the instrument's current
permissions.

Real instruments acknowledge this with timeouts and watchdogs. A speedometer
does not wait forever for the next pulse before deciding the vehicle has
stopped. It uses a rule: after this much time without a valid crossing, report
zero or enter a fault state. That rule is not a solution to the future. It is a
bounded convention for making the present usable. The instrument cannot prove
that no pulse will ever arrive again. It can only say that, relative to its
current gauge and threshold, waiting longer would no longer serve the reading.

The same distinction appears in software. A program that waits for a sensor may
block forever unless it carries a timeout. The timeout does not decide the
mathematical halting status of the sensor process. It decides what the system
will do when the process has not returned within the admissible window. In the
ledger's language, the timeout is a measurement policy at the halting boundary:
do not confuse failure to return with a negative fact, but do not let the whole
instrument become hostage to an unclosed computation either.

That policy is a kind of humility. A timeout says, "I do not know that this
process will never finish; I know only that my responsibility to the present
reading has expired." The distinction protects the ledger from overclaiming. It
also protects the system from paralysis. Measurement must be humble enough not
to turn nonresponse into cosmic truth and practical enough to move on when the
instrument's window has closed.

The boundary is therefore not outside engineering practice. It is what good
engineering practice has been negotiating all along.

The formal chapter gives that negotiation a name so it can be carried upward.
Without the name, every later timeout would look like an implementation detail
instead of a boundary in the measurable process.

\begin{gphenom}{Chaitin}
\label{ph:chaitin}
\GPhObs Chaitin showed in 1974 that some real numbers are algorithmically
random: they contain no pattern shorter than themselves. No program can
generate them. They must be copied in full or not at all.
\GPhCon Some ledger entries are lawless -- they carry no compressible
structure. No instrument can predict them; no theory can derive them.
They must be witnessed directly.
\GPhSeq The compiler must accept lawless entries. The Chaitin sequence type is
the honest acknowledgment: some programs never halt, and the ledger must record
that fact without being able to explain it.
\end{gphenom}

The word lawless should not be heard as meaningless. A lawless entry can still
be witnessed. A radioactive click may be unpredictable and still real. A
network packet may arrive from noise and still be logged. A program may fail to
halt and still shape the behavior of the machine waiting on it. Lawlessness
means that the record has no compressible explanation available to this
process. It does not mean that nothing happened.

For the speedometer, the lesson is that the dial is not a window onto all
truths about motion. It is the output of a halting process over a pulse stream.
When that process is well posed, the needle moves. When the requested question
is not well posed for the instrument, the needle is not merely blank; the
instrument has discovered the edge of its own formal world.

This is the chapter's first boundary. The signal can be represented only when
the computation that carries it can be brought to account. The next section
asks what happens at that boundary in practice. The answer is not silence. It
is noise.

\section{Noise as Structure}
\label{se:noise-as-structure}

Noise is what a boundary sounds like from inside an instrument. A clean theory
would like to place signal on one side and noise on the other. The sensor does
not get that luxury. It receives a stream of marks, some regular, some
irregular, some too small, some too late, some arriving while the previous mark
is still being processed. The instrument has to decide not only what the signal
is, but what kind of undecidedness surrounds it.

The speedometer is again useful because its noise is ordinary. A magnetic
pickup near a rotating wheel receives pulses from teeth or magnets. But the
wire can pick up electrical interference. The wheel can vibrate. The timing
between pulses can jitter. A tooth may be chipped. The sensor may bounce at the
threshold and produce two crossings where the wheel produced one. The raw
stream is not the signal. The signal is what remains after the instrument has
made a structured decision about the stream.

The Lean file names the first wrapper \lean{NoisyProcess}. It pairs a Turing
process with a Chaitin sequence. That pairing is the whole thesis of the
section. Noise is not external to computation. It is the presence of the
halting boundary inside the representation of the measurement.

\begin{leancode}
turing\_process : TuringProcess Value Carrier\\
program : ChaitinsNumberSequence\\
next\_recursive\_step? :\\
\quad Computation -> Option Computation\\
next\_representation? :\\
\quad ChaitinsNumberSequence -> ChaitinsNumberSequence
\end{leancode}

The process has a Turing machine and a program drawn from the Chaitin sequence.
It can ask for the next recursive step, but the answer is optional. A source
program may not yet be computing; an executing computation may produce another
computation. The process can also ask for the next representation of the
Chaitin sequence. If the sequence is already halting, it remains halting. If it
is nonhalting, the process advances the computation when it can and otherwise
carries the unresolved state.

This is exactly how a noisy speedometer behaves. A clean pulse advances the
state. A missing or ambiguous pulse does not let the instrument pretend that a
clean state has arrived. It either carries the previous state, marks a
diagnostic condition, or waits for the next admissible crossing. Noise is a
state-transition problem, not a decorative error term.

This is also where aliasing enters. If the sampling window is badly chosen,
distinct motions can look the same to the instrument. A wheel rotating quickly
may be undersampled until the pulse train appears slower than it is. A noisy
oscillation near threshold may be counted as a series of real crossings. The
problem is not that the world became ambiguous in some vague philosophical
sense. The problem is that the instrument's representational grain and the
incoming pattern have formed a misleading relation. Noise is often the name we
give to that relation before we have understood it.

The chapter then introduces \lean{Metavariable}. A metavariable is a
placeholder with a type. In the prose of measurement, it is the smallest
representable distinction at the current level of the tower: the instrument's
local grain. Do not dress that up as a physical constant. It is
representational granularity, and that is already enough.

\begin{leancode}
| base : Fact -> \(\alpha\) -> Metavariable \(\alpha\)\\
| step : Fact -> Metavariable \(\alpha\) ->\\
\quad Metavariable \(\alpha\)
\end{leancode}

A base metavariable carries a fact and a value of the underlying type. A step
metavariable carries a fact and another metavariable. The shape is recursive
because precision is recursive. A reading can be refined only by adding a
further witnessed distinction. The instrument does not hold infinite
resolution. It holds a current representable level and a way to step deeper
when the process can justify the step.

\begin{sidebar}{Shannon's Lesson}
\label{sb:shannon-noise}
Shannon's theory of communication did not treat noise as a moral failure of
the channel. Noise is part of the channel's capacity. A message is not defined
apart from the uncertainty through which it must be recovered. Chapter 4 uses
the same discipline: a signal is not the absence of noise, but a structure that
can survive the noise allowed by the instrument.
\end{sidebar}

The speedometer's granularity appears as resolution. At low speeds, a short
measurement window may contain too few pulses to distinguish one speed from
another. At high speeds, pulses may arrive faster than the electronics can
separate. The reported number is always a compromise between the grain of the
sensor, the clock window, and the filtering process. A smoother reading may be
less responsive. A more responsive reading may be noisier. The instrument does
not escape this trade. It formalizes it.

Filtering is therefore a kind of memory. A display that averages several pulse
windows is asking the past to stabilize the present. That can be wise: a needle
that jumps at every jitter is useless. But memory also has a cost. The
instrument may continue to report yesterday's trend after today's signal has
changed. This is why the formal process must carry both the state and the rule
for advancing the state. A reading without its filter is an orphaned number.
The ledger wants the family history.

Once memory is admitted, noise cannot be treated as a single bad sample. It is
a relation between present disturbance and remembered pattern. A lone strange
pulse may be rejected because the recent history makes it implausible. A run of
strange pulses may force the instrument to revise the trend. The same mark can
be noise in one context and signal in another. Structure, not volume, decides.

That is the chapter's noise doctrine in one sentence.

The code then moves toward observation through \lean{SlipProcess}. The metaphor
is static friction. A block at rest does not move under every small force. It
absorbs disturbance until the threshold is crossed; then it slips. Measurement
works the same way. A perturbation below resolution does not become a new
entry. A perturbation at the threshold reveals the surface on which the old
symbol was standing.

\begin{leancode}
physical\_process : PhysicalProcess Value Carrier\\
projection : Sophism\\
stress : ChaitinsNumberSequence\\
threshold : Type\\
happened : Sophism -> Sophism
\end{leancode}

\lean{SlipProcess} carries a physical process, a projection, a stress, and a
threshold. The stress is measured in the language of the Chaitin boundary: the
pressure of a computation whose status may not be settled. The function
\lean{happened} turns one sophistic representation into another. In physical
language, the instrument has been pushed through a threshold. In compiler
language, the type of the record has been lifted through another
representational level.

\lean{OBSERVED} packages that slip as an observation. It adds the slip process,
an observation type, and a predicate \lean{possible} over sophistic states. The
predicate is not equality. It asks whether one state can be carried into
another under the observed relation. Origins can move toward dimensions.
Dimensions do not move back into origins. Two dimensions compare only when the
facts differ and the Chaitin stress is ordered. The observation is the
structured record of a slip.

That is why noise belongs in the middle of the chapter rather than at the edge.
Without noise, the signal would look like a clean token handed to the gauge.
With noise, the signal becomes what the instrument can still carry after
crossing its own threshold of confusion. A speedometer reading is not the
absence of jitter. It is the result of a process that knows how much jitter it
can absorb before the needle must move.

\begin{gphenom}{Schrodinger-Young}
\label{ph:schrodinger-young}
\GPhObs Young's double-slit experiment (1801) produces an interference pattern
even when particles are sent one at a time. Schrodinger's formalism (1926)
models the alternatives by amplitudes that can interfere before a detector
forces a recorded outcome.
\GPhCon A single particle's ledger entry is not resolved until measurement.
\lean{Jar.superposition} is not temporary ignorance -- it is a first-class
ledger state that the compiler holds without resolving.
\GPhSeq In the device, unresolved measurement is carried as an explicit
obligation. \lean{GUNGAN} must resolve a \lean{Jar} value before the compiler
can use it as ordinary data. Superposition is not a defect in the ledger. It is
a valid entry that has not yet been closed.
\end{gphenom}

The phenomenon box points forward to quantum measurement, but the present
lesson is already enough: unresolved structure is not nothing. Interference,
jitter, and nonhalting traces may all be real entries whose resolution depends
on the measurement process. The ledger must be able to hold them without
pretending that they have already collapsed into ordinary data.

Noise is therefore the structural presence of the boundary. The signal is what
passes through that boundary with enough form to be carried forward. The next
question is where the boundary is drawn. That is the work of the gauge.

\section{The Gauge}
\label{se:the-gauge}

A gauge is a disciplined way to be local. It chooses the reference structure in
which a reading can be made. This is easy to misunderstand. A gauge is not a
trick for changing reality. It is a way of choosing coordinates, clocks,
scales, and phases so that the instrument can ask a well-posed question. The
same physical situation may admit several gauges. A good theory says which
readings remain invariant when the gauge changes.

The speedometer has gauges everywhere. It counts wheel rotations, not road
truth. It assumes a tire radius. It assumes a clock. It assumes the pickup is
mounted at a fixed relation to the teeth it reads. Change the tire and the
calibration changes. Change the clock and the rate changes. Change the
filtering window and the needle changes its behavior. The car has not become a
different car, but the reported number depends on the reference structure the
instrument is using.

Episode 4 begins with time as area: a present tick and a small interval,
\lean{t} and \lean{dt}. The order is deliberately not a simple well-ordering.
Relativity has already made that impossible. There is no universal clock that
all instruments must obey. A gauge must therefore carry a local present, not a
cosmic Now. The speedometer's clock is mounted in the vehicle's instrument
system. It reports relative to that system. It does not certify the temporal
order of the universe.

The section's main process is \lean{GaugeProcess}. The excerpt shows the
fields that matter for the prose.

\begin{leancode}
sensing\_process : SensingProcess Value Carrier\\
clock : Phenomenon\\
event? : Phenomenon -> Prop
\end{leancode}

The gauge holds a sensing process, a clock, and an event predicate. This is
the minimum structure needed to ask: which events are admissible right now?
The sensing process supplies contact with the phenomenon. The clock supplies
the local reference. The predicate asks whether a candidate phenomenon counts
as an event under that reference.

This is how a speedometer turns rotation into speed. The sensing process is
the pickup. The clock is the time base. The event predicate decides whether a
crossing of the sensor threshold is a pulse to be counted. If the crossing
arrives too soon after the previous one, it may be bounce. If it arrives too
late, it may indicate a missing tooth or a stopped wheel. If it arrives with
the wrong shape, it may be noise. The gauge is the local rule by which the
instrument treats one mark as an event and another mark as background.

A second vehicle makes the locality clearer. Two cars may drive side by side
with the same wheel pulse frequency and report different road speeds because
their tires have different rolling radii. Neither speedometer is lying simply
because the pulse count matches while the reported speed differs. Each is
using a different calibration. The pulse frequency is not the road speed; it is
an event stream under a gauge. Road speed is what that stream becomes when the
calibration is applied.

Wheel slip makes the gauge still more delicate. On ice, a driven wheel may
spin faster than the car moves. The speedometer tied to that wheel may report a
speed the vehicle is not actually carrying across the road. Anti-lock and
traction systems compare multiple wheel gauges against one another because no
single local reference can be trusted absolutely under all conditions. The
reading is real as a wheel reading. It is not automatically real as road
motion. The gauge tells the ledger what kind of event has been measured.

\begin{sidebar}{Gauge}
\label{sb:gauge}
In physics, a gauge is a choice of reference structure that does not change the
observable content when handled correctly. Two gauges may describe the same
field in different local terms. The discipline is to know which quantities are
artifacts of the chosen reference and which survive as measurable relations.
\end{sidebar}

The code also introduces \lean{PRESENT}. The name is almost too human. The
present is not a metaphysical instant. It is the local clock condition under
which an instrument can treat a phenomenon as available for measurement. A
speedometer cannot count a pulse before it arrives. It cannot keep a pulse
forever without smearing the reading. It has a present window: a span in which
marks are eligible to become events.

That window matters because the signal has already passed through computation,
halting, and noise. By the time a mark reaches the gauge, it is not a raw fact.
It is a candidate event shaped by previous permissions. The gauge asks whether
the candidate is admissible under the current reference. This is why a gauge
belongs after noise. Before noise, the instrument might pretend that reference
choices are merely cosmetic. After noise, reference choices are operational.
They determine which distinctions survive.

\begin{gphenom}{Descartes}
\label{ph:descartes}
\GPhObs Descartes' coordinate grid (1637) imposes a discrete address on
continuous space. Every point becomes a pair of numbers. Continuity
is replaced by a countable index.
\GPhCon Quantization is the act of assigning a finite address to an otherwise
undifferentiated position. The instrument's resolution determines the
grid spacing; below that spacing, no distinction is admissible.
\GPhSeq The \lean{Metavariable} type is the compiler's Cartesian grid:
it assigns a finite index to the smallest representable distinction at
each level of the tower. The continuum does not precede the grid;
the grid is what makes the continuum measurable.
\end{gphenom}

Descartes' grid is a useful ancestor of the gauge. A coordinate grid does not
create the plane, but it creates a way to address points. It turns geometric
presence into an ordered pair. That address is powerful and dangerous. It lets
the instrument compute, but it can seduce the reader into thinking the grid is
the thing itself. Chapter 4 refuses that seduction. The grid is a gauge: a
chosen reference that makes measurement possible.

The speedometer's grid is temporal. Each pulse gets an address in the clock's
window. Without that address, the pulse cannot become a rate. A heap of wheel
events is not speed. Speed is wheel events under a time gauge, filtered by a
sensing process, admitted by an event predicate, and reported only after the
instrument has decided that the local reference is good enough.

\lean{MEASURABLE} then wraps the gauge in a class promise. A phenomenon is
measurable when the ledger can carry the gauge process that makes its events
available. This is not the same as saying that the phenomenon exists. Existence
is too broad. Measurability is local and procedural. It says that, for this
carrier and this reference structure, the phenomenon can be brought into the
ledger without breaking the formal promises already installed.

The lesson is not relativism. The lesson is accountability. If a reading
depends on tire radius, name the calibration. If it depends on a clock, name
the clock. If it depends on filtering, name the window. A gauge does not weaken
measurement by admitting its reference. It strengthens measurement by refusing
to let the reference masquerade as the world.

This is why the gauge is the natural predecessor of measurability. A value
becomes measurable only after the local conditions of its measurement have been
made explicit enough to be carried by the ledger. The world may contain many
relations that no current gauge can expose. The gauge does not deny them. It
names the relation this instrument can expose now. That modesty is what keeps
later physics from turning into wishful coordinate choice.

The same modesty lets gauges be compared. If two speed sensors disagree, the
system can ask whether the disagreement comes from tire radius, wheel slip,
clock drift, sensor failure, or a real difference in motion. That question is
possible only because each reading carries its gauge. Without the gauge, the
two numbers merely conflict. With the gauge, the conflict becomes diagnostic.
The ledger can ask which reference changed.

This diagnostic use is why the book cares about gauge before it reaches
physics proper. Later chapters will need choices of coordinate, derivative,
and integration domain. Chapter 4 teaches the smaller dashboard version first.
A reference frame is not an afterthought attached to a finished quantity. It is
part of the procedure that lets the quantity become visible at all.

Once that is learned, the later mathematics can change gauges without losing
the reader. The reader has already seen why a reference can be local and still
honest.
Locality becomes a discipline rather than an excuse.
That discipline is the gauge.

The next section pushes this accountability into the hardest case: a state
that the ledger can hold but not yet resolve. The gauge says where a reading is
made. The quantum jar says what happens when the reading has not yet chosen
which ordinary value it will become.

\section{The Quantum Jar}
\label{se:quantum-jar}

The quantum jar is deliberately disarming. After Turing machines, Chaitin
sequences, noisy processes, metavariables, slips, and gauges, the code names an
inductive with three little constructors. Color. Bang. Superposition. The
humor is part of the pedagogy. The hardest measurement problem in the chapter
does not enter as a cathedral of notation. It enters as a jar the compiler is
not allowed to open too early.

\begin{leancode}
| color : Jar\\
| bang : Jar\\
| superposition : Jar
\end{leancode}

The first two constructors are ordinary outcomes. The third is the point.
Superposition is not represented as missing data, and it is not represented as
a probability distribution outside the type. It is a first-class value of
\lean{Jar}. The ledger can hold it. The compiler can see that it is a legal
case. But the rest of the process may not be allowed to use it as if it had
already become color or bang.

The speedometer metaphor has to be handled gently here, because a car wheel is
not a quantum system. Still, the instrument has unresolved states. A marginal
pulse near threshold may be held in a buffer until later context decides
whether it belongs to the count. A diagnostic register may mark a crossing as
ambiguous. A filter may carry two possible interpretations of a jittering
signal until the next pulse selects the consistent path. These are not quantum
superpositions in the physical sense. They are a familiar engineering shadow of
the same formal problem: the record sometimes has to hold an unresolved value
without pretending it is resolved.

Quantum measurement makes that formal problem unavoidable. A particle in a
superposed state is not merely hiding a value from the observer. The state
itself carries structural information that may produce interference. Treating
superposition as ordinary ignorance loses the phenomenon. The double-slit
experiment is the canonical warning: one particle at a time still builds an
interference pattern when the path is not measured. The unresolved state does
work.

Schrodinger's cat is famous because it turns that formal discomfort into a
household image, but the jar here is cleaner than the cat. It does not ask the
reader to picture a macroscopic animal as both alive and dead. It asks the
reader to notice what the type must be able to hold before a measurement
resolves. Color and bang are ordinary outcomes. Superposition is the state that
cannot honestly be replaced by either ordinary outcome yet. The jar is a small
formal container for that refusal.

A good instrument designer already understands the need for such containers.
Digital circuits use metastability windows. A latch may receive an input too
near the clock edge and spend a short time neither cleanly high nor cleanly
low. Engineers do not celebrate this as magic. They design synchronizers so the
rest of the circuit does not spend the metastable state as if it were ordinary
data. The unresolved state is real enough to be dangerous. It must be held,
waited on, or resolved by a process designed for that purpose.

The device therefore gives unresolvedness a constructor. This is a major
ethical move in the formalism. If the ledger lacks a place for unresolved
states, it will force them into ordinary outcomes too soon. It will pretend
that every candidate event has already crossed the threshold into color or
bang. The result would be a false simplicity: a clean record created by
destroying the very structure measurement was supposed to respect.

The next process is \lean{MeesaProcess}. Its name is playful, but its role is
serious. It holds a gauge process and a jar concept. The gauge supplies the
local reference. The jar supplies the unresolved or resolved measurement state.
Together they say that measurement is not only a question of what reference is
used, but of what kind of value is permitted to sit under that reference before
resolution.

\begin{leancode}
gauge\_process : GaugeProcess Value Carrier\\
concept : Jar
\end{leancode}

This is a compact structure with a large consequence. It says the gauge does
not automatically produce an ordinary value. It may produce a concept that is
still \lean{superposition}. The measurement process has not failed. It has
arrived at a state whose honest representation is unresolved.

For the speedometer, the analogous state is the buffered ambiguity that should
not yet move the needle. A pulse near the debounce threshold, a wheel-speed
disagreement during a skid, or a diagnostic condition during sensor dropout may
be carried in the instrument's internal state without being displayed as a
clean speed. The driver sees either a stable number or a warning light, but the
instrument may pass through an intermediate region in which the honest record
is neither. The point is not to make the car quantum. The point is to teach the
reader to respect unresolved formal states instead of treating them as defects
in exposition.

\lean{GUNGAN} is the translation layer. It carries a \lean{MeesaProcess} and a
resolution rule. The rule is the moment the jar is forced into a usable
measurement value. A superposed jar is not usable by the later ledger until
this class has done its work. In prose terms, the compiler will not let the
book spend a superposition as if it were already color or bang.

\begin{leancode}
meesa\_process : MeesaProcess Value Carrier\\
resolve : Jar -> Jar
\end{leancode}

The key word is not collapse but permission. The typeclass does not explain
the metaphysics of quantum measurement. It installs the formal promise that an
unresolved measurement state has been passed through an explicit resolution
process before it is used as an ordinary reading. That is exactly the kind of
promise this book has been building from the beginning. Do not count a mark
until it is distinguished. Do not admit a mark until it clears the threshold.
Do not treat a settling path as a value until residue has done its work. Do not
use a jar until the translation layer has resolved it.

This restraint keeps the chapter from doing fake physics. The book is not
claiming that a typeclass solves the measurement problem. It is claiming that a
formal measurement device should refuse to erase the problem. If the record
contains an unresolved state, the record should say so. If a later process
needs an ordinary value, the later process must name the resolution step it is
using. That is a much smaller claim than a theory of collapse, and it is
stronger because it is enforceable inside the tower.

The ordinary language of "getting a result" hides this enforcement. A lab
instrument may display a number, but behind the number there may be saturated
detectors, ambiguous fits, discarded transients, calibration warnings, or
states held until a trigger condition resolves. Good experimental practice
does not pretend these never occurred. It records them as part of the path to
the result. The jar is the same moral pressure in type form: if the state is
not yet a result, do not call it one.

There is also a useful asymmetry. A resolved jar may be carried forward as an
ordinary outcome, but an unresolved jar cannot be used by code that requires an
ordinary outcome. That restriction is the point. The tower has learned to make
illegal moves untypeable. It does not rely on the reader's virtue to remember
that superposition is special. It makes the special state explicit enough that
later promises can demand its resolution.

That is laboratory care in formal dress. Label the sample before it is
processed. Mark the ambiguous trace before it is interpreted. Preserve the raw
state until the resolution step has been named. The jar is a tiny laboratory
protocol for unresolvedness.

It also preserves surprise. If every unresolved state were flattened
immediately, the ledger would never notice interference, metastability, or
ambiguous diagnostic traces. The jar keeps the record open just long enough for
the right process to act.
That pause is part of the measurement, not a delay before measurement begins.
The pause has a type.
Good.

This closes the chapter's arc. A signal entered as a computational question.
The Turing process gave it a machine. The halting boundary limited what the
machine could certify. Noise gave that limit a structure inside measurement.
The gauge supplied the local reference under which events can be admitted. The
jar supplied a place for unresolved measurement states. \lean{GUNGAN} supplied
the explicit act of resolution.

The speedometer, humble as it is, now looks different. Its needle is not a
mere number. It is the end of a signal path that includes computation,
undecidable horizons, noise filtering, gauge choice, and state resolution. A
consumer dial hides those layers because the driver needs a usable reading.
The formal device exposes them because the book needs to know what a reading
costs.

\section*{Coda}
\markboth{CODA}{CODA}

A radio tuner offers another way to feel the chapter. Turn the knob across the
band and the speaker gives hiss, fragments, whistles, and voices that almost
settle into words. The station is not found by wishing away the noise. It is
found by bringing the receiver into relation with a carrier, a bandwidth, a
filter, and a threshold. The signal was never simply the loudest thing in the
air. It was the thing the receiver could lock onto and carry forward.

At the edge of a station, the receiver hesitates. A syllable appears, dissolves
into static, and appears again. The listener may know there is a broadcast
nearby, but the radio cannot yet use it as speech. The tuning circuit has to
settle. The demodulator has to extract the waveform. The amplifier has to
raise it without raising the noise beyond use. A station is not a miracle in
the air. It is a successful relation between transmission and apparatus.

Chapter 4 has treated measurement the same way. The Turing process is the
receiver's internal circuit: a finite machinery for carrying a pattern from
state to state. The program is the tuned procedure by which the incoming
structure is interpreted. A closed study becomes something like a carrier wave
inside the instrument, a pattern that can be read by the next stage rather
than merely stored.

The halting boundary is the station that never resolves. The receiver can keep
listening. It can sometimes find a clean tone. But no act of listening proves
that every possible faint pattern will eventually become a station. Some
questions are not answered by more gain. More gain may only amplify the hiss.
The boundary is not a weakness of the listener. It is built into the relation
between procedure and possible signal.

Noise is the hiss that teaches the receiver its own shape. Without noise, the
radio would not need selectivity. With noise, the radio's filters become part
of the meaning of reception. Static is not the opposite of signal in any simple
sense. It is the field through which signal must become detectable. A receiver
that cannot characterize noise cannot honestly say what it has received.

The gauge is the tuning dial. Shift the dial and the same electromagnetic room
becomes a different set of possible stations. The broadcast world did not
change because the knob moved, but the receiver's relation to it changed. This
is not deception. It is locality. A gauge says: from here, with this reference,
these are the events that can be admitted.

The quantum jar is the almost-station before lock. The receiver may hold a
state that is neither usable speech nor mere absence. There is energy in the
band. There is structure in the hiss. There may be a carrier. But until the
receiver locks, the state should not be spent as a message. A good receiver
does not pretend that every ambiguous tone has already become a word. It holds
the ambiguity until the resolving process does its work.

This metaphor is not an explanation of quantum mechanics, any more than the
speedometer is an explanation of Turing machines. It is a pattern for the
reader's hand. The hand knows what it means to tune. It knows the difference
between noise, almost-signal, lock, and message. Chapter 4 asks the mind to
give formal measurement the same patience.

The signal is not what arrives first. It is what survives the route through
the receiver. It must be represented, processed, bounded by halting, carried
through noise, read in a gauge, and resolved when resolution is required. Only
then can the ledger use it.

The receiver does not conquer the air. It learns how to listen without lying
about what listening can do. That is the chapter's discipline. A signal is a
successful listening relation, and every successful listening relation carries
the memory of the noise, reference, and unresolved states it had to pass
through.

Think of the little red tuning light on an old receiver. It does not say that
the world has become music. It says that the internal circuit has found enough
agreement between oscillator, carrier, and filter to treat the incoming pattern
as a station. Before the light, there may still be fragments of speech. After
the light, there may still be static. The light is not metaphysical certainty.
It is an admissibility receipt.

The speedometer's needle is such a light. The radio's lock indicator is such a
light. A compiler's completed elaboration is such a light. Each says: this
process, under these references, has returned enough structure to be used. None
of them says: all possible questions have been answered. The distinction is
the difference between measurement and fantasy.

The coda's radio also makes clear why the chapter had to come after
permissions. A receiver with no entry threshold would treat every fluctuation
as a station. A receiver with no growth threshold would overload. A receiver
with no direction threshold would confuse tuning with detuning. A receiver
with no settlement threshold would never stop adjusting long enough to play a
sound. Chapter 3 built those permissions. Chapter 4 lets them operate inside a
signal path.

And the chapter had to come before the tower that follows. A tower of further
abstraction can be built only if the lower signal path has learned how to admit
failure, noise, gauge, and unresolvedness without panic. Otherwise every later
abstraction would smuggle in an impossible receiver: one that hears all
stations at once, never mistakes static for speech, and never waits on a
program that fails to return.

The real receiver is humbler and more interesting. It listens in time. It
chooses a band. It filters. It locks. It sometimes loses lock. It carries
ambiguity honestly until a process can resolve it. The signal is the pattern
that survives this discipline, not the pattern imagined before discipline
begins.

That is the sound Chapter 4 wants the reader to hear under every later piece
of code. The formal tower is not built in silence. It is built inside hiss,
drift, tuning, and lock. The ledger's promise is not that the hiss disappears.
The promise is that when a signal is finally used, the path by which it became
usable has not been forgotten.

There is one more radio lesson. A broadcast can be strong and still useless if
the receiver is tuned to the wrong band. Another can be weak and still usable
if the receiver knows how to lock. Strength alone is not signal. Signal is fit:
fit between transmitter and receiver, carrier and oscillator, modulation and
filter, message and decoding process. Chapter 4 turns that fit into a formal
requirement.

That is why the chapter's metaphors keep returning to instruments instead of
floating into pure logic. Pure logic can state the halting problem. It can
state representation. It can define types. Measurement has to live with
contacts, windows, gauges, noise floors, and ambiguous states. The formal
device is interesting because it tries to make those conditions visible inside
the same tower that carries the mathematics.

When the reader meets later claims about abstraction, science, physics, and
closure, the radio should still be humming in the background. Ask what station
is being tuned. Ask what counts as lock. Ask what noise has been filtered and
what noise has been mistaken for message. Ask which unresolved states have
been honestly held and which have been prematurely spent. These questions are
not suspicions from outside the book. They are the habits Chapter 4 installs.

The coda therefore leaves the reader not with a final answer but with a hand on
the dial. A signal is not a gift. It is an achievement of tuned relation. The
ledger can use it only because the receiver has done the work of computation,
boundary, noise, gauge, and resolution. The rest of the book builds upward from
that achievement, and every higher floor should still feel the faint vibration
of the tuner finding its station.

When that vibration is felt, the chapter has done its job. The reader no
longer sees signal as a simple arrival. Signal has become a disciplined event:
something found, held, tested, tuned, and finally trusted only within the
limits of the apparatus that made trust possible.

That trust is not naive. It is engineered trust, the kind a receiver earns by
knowing both how to lock onto a station and how to admit when the station has
not been found. The next chapter can build a tower only because this receiver
has learned the difference.
Listening has become a formal skill.
The ledger will need that skill for every higher abstraction that follows.
It will keep listening.
With receipts.
