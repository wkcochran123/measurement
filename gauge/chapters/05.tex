\chapter{The Tower}
\label{chap:the-tower}

% EPISODE COVERAGE: Episodes 5, 6, and 7 -- SOURCE/EXECUTED/Abstraction,
% VALUE/MAGNITUDE/SCALED/LOAD/FINITE_ELEPHANT/Spline, Episode 7 totality proof
% KEY CONCEPT: The totality proof. The tower exists not just as definitions
% but as a concrete verified instance.

A speedometer is not one instrument. It is a tower pretending to be one
instrument. At the bottom is contact with rotation: a wheel, a cable, a pickup,
a magnet, a toothed ring. Above that is a pulse vocabulary: this crossing
counts, that flutter does not, this window is still open, that window has
closed. Above that is arithmetic: pulses per unit time, circumference times
rotation, smoothing across windows, conversion to miles or kilometers. Above
that is display: the needle, the digital number, the warning state. The driver
sees only the top floor.

Chapter 5 asks how such a tower can stand. Chapter 4 made the signal honest by
forcing it through computation, halting, noise, gauge, and unresolved state.
That was the receiver learning to listen. Chapter 5 asks what happens after a
signal can be listened to. The instrument must now build an internal world in
which instructions can be encoded, abstractions can be ordered without falling
into self-reference, arithmetic can be assembled from small formal parts,
smooth behavior can be constructed from finite records, and the whole stack can
be shown to exist by concrete instances.

The speedometer's tower is a useful guide because none of its floors is
optional. A wheel pulse with no instruction set is just a click. An instruction
set with no abstraction is a pile of operations that cannot be reused. An
abstraction with no arithmetic cannot become speed. Arithmetic with no
interpolation cannot produce a stable needle. A stable needle with no verified
path back to the sensor is only theater. The tower matters because each floor
inherits the obligations below it and adds exactly the structure the next floor
needs.

An electronic speedometer makes this especially plain. A firmware routine
begins with a few primitive instructions: initialize the counter, clear a bit,
set a bit, branch, load, compare, return. Engineers build drivers, filters, and
display logic from that vocabulary. Eventually the dashboard can show a number
that feels immediate. But immediacy is the illusion of a completed tower. The
number is the visible output of a hierarchy of encodings and verified
transitions.

The chapter is called "The Tower" because it stops treating that hierarchy as
background. It asks the reader to look at the levels themselves. At the bottom
is an instruction vocabulary small enough to be trusted. Above it is
abstraction, the ability of a solved problem to behave like a primitive in the
next problem. Above that is arithmetic assembled from sums, products, bases,
and loads. Above that is the spline, where finite observations become a smooth
interpolant without pretending the continuum was given for free. At the top is
the totality proof: the compiler confirms that the floors are inhabited, not
merely described.

This chapter also changes the emotional texture of the book. Earlier chapters
often asked whether a mark may enter. This chapter asks whether the entered
mark can be built upward without cheating. The question is architectural. A
tower is not stable because its top is beautiful. It is stable because weight
is carried down through every floor. A speedometer reading is stable only if
the needle's motion can be traced back through display logic, arithmetic,
instruction, signal, threshold, and wheel.

That trace is the chapter's central discipline. Each abstraction must carry a
receipt from the floor below. Each operation must be limited enough that the
compiler can accept it. Each new mathematical convenience must be earned by a
structure that can actually be used. The result is not a grand theory imposed
from above. It is a tower climbed from below, one verified floor at a time.

The tower is therefore a dependency order, not an ornamented outline of topics.
Encoding must precede execution because no machine executes an instruction it
cannot read. Execution must precede abstraction because an abstraction that has
never run is only a name. Arithmetic must precede the spline because smooth
interpolation needs sums, products, bases, and loading. The totality proof must
come last because only the completed stack can be checked as a stack.

An analog speedometer hides the same order in metal. The cable turns, the
magnet drags the cup, the spring resists, the needle settles, and the dial
marks give the settled position a public value. If any lower relation fails,
the upper reading may still move, but it no longer means what it claims to
mean.

Chapter 5 slows down to label those stairs. Later chapters will use high-level
words quickly. This chapter teaches the reader to ask what supports them: what
was encoded, what was executed, what abstraction is being spent as primitive,
which arithmetic was assembled, which spline made finite records smooth, and
which instances prove the floor is not empty.

It also teaches the reader not to despise small primitives. The tower begins
with almost childish gestures: boot, zero, one, compile, execute, add, multiply,
load. These are not small because the ambition is small. They are small because
trust has to be small before it can be large. A dashboard number that looks
effortless is made from many little permissions kept in order. Chapter 5 asks
the reader to develop affection for that order. The grandeur of the tower is
not that it escapes primitive acts. The grandeur is that primitive acts can
become a structure tall enough to hold arithmetic, smoothness, and proof.

That is the mood of this chapter: patient construction under pressure. The
speedometer does not win trust by sounding philosophical. It wins trust by
booting, counting, scaling, smoothing, and surviving inspection. The formal
tower wants the same kind of trust. It will not ask the reader to believe in a
continuum before showing how finite pieces are allowed to support one.

The reader should feel that patience as relief, not delay. The chapter gives
each floor enough time to become legible before the next floor leans on it. A
fast dashboard is useful only because slow construction happened somewhere
else first.

\section{Encoding Instructions}
\label{se:encoding-instructions}

The first floor above signal is instruction. A signal may be received, but an
instrument cannot use it until the signal is encoded as something the machine
knows how to do. The speedometer's firmware does not receive "speed" as an
idea. It receives transitions. It has to turn those transitions into primitive
operations: start, clear, set, count, compare, branch. The chapter begins at
this humble level because a tower that cannot explain its instruction
vocabulary is already leaning.

Episode 5 introduces \lean{Equivalation}. The word is odd because ordinary
equivalence is too still. The device needs equivalence under procedure:
something has the same value after the machine has done the work that makes
the sameness usable. In measurement language, this is the difference between
saying two readings look alike and saying the instrument has a procedure by
which one can be carried as the other. A speedometer uses this all the time.
One pulse from one tooth is equivalent to another pulse from another tooth only
under the procedure that treats both as wheel crossings in the current gauge.

The constructors of \lean{Equivalation} are physical, zero-like, and one-like.
That is not yet a full arithmetic. It is a way to say that a measurement state
may be grounded in a jar-like physical condition, may behave like absence, or
may behave like a next distinguished presence. The tower has learned not to
import zero and one as metaphysical givens. It must teach the machine how zero
and one arise as procedural roles.

The next process is digital in the older sense of the word. Digital means
fingered before it means electronic. A digit is a counted gesture, a way for a
hand to carry a discrete state. \lean{DigitalProcess} gives the tower a way to
tick an equivalation from one role into another. The tick does not reveal a
hidden Platonic bit. It performs a transition. In a speedometer, the equivalent
transition is the counter advancing when a pulse is admitted. The bit changes
because the process says it changes.

The instruction vocabulary then narrows to \lean{Encoding}. The type has three
primitive opcodes: boot, zero, and one. Boot initializes. Zero and one carry
the two elementary symbolic states. This seems absurdly small until one
remembers that every actual processor is built from a finite instruction set.
No CPU understands all possible actions as primitives. It understands a small
vocabulary and derives complexity by composition.

\begin{leancode}
| boot : Encoding\\
| zero : Encoding\\
| one : Encoding
\end{leancode}

The point of displaying this tiny excerpt is not to impress. It is to slow the
reader down. A tower of computation begins with a finite alphabet of actions.
The instrument is not allowed to assume that arbitrary procedures are already
available. It starts with initialization and a binary distinction. Everything
else must be built by sequencing, interpretation, and execution.

\lean{SOURCE} asks where such an instruction sequence claims to come from. The
name is deliberately broader than source code. A source is the origin story the
machine is allowed to use. In a speedometer, source may be firmware, a lookup
table, a calibration constant, or the pulse stream itself. A source is not
trusted because it exists. It is trusted because it can be carried through the
ledger's existing promises.

\lean{EXECUTED} adds the question that matters to a machine: what happens when
the encoding is run? It wraps the instruction vocabulary with an execution
process, an output question, and the relation between encoded state and
reported result. The transition from encoding to execution is the transition
from a possible instruction to an acted instruction. A pulse counter sitting in
memory is not a speedometer. The instruction must run in time.

\begin{sidebar}{Instruction Sets}
\label{sb:instruction-sets}
An instruction set is the finite vocabulary a processor can perform directly.
Programs feel rich because instructions are composed, not because the machine
begins with infinitely many primitives. Chapter 5 uses \lean{Encoding} in this
spirit: boot, zero, and one are not the whole tower, but the first alphabet
from which the tower can speak.
\end{sidebar}

The speedometer metaphor helps resist a common mistake. A driver may think the
instrument "knows" speed. The firmware knows procedures. It can boot, read,
count, clear, set, compare, and display. Speed emerges only after those
instructions have been ordered by the tower. If the instruction set is wrong,
the displayed number can be wrong even if the sensor is perfect. If the source
is wrong, the execution can be wrong even if the instruction vocabulary is
sound. The tower must therefore care about source and execution before it cares
about elegant arithmetic.

This section is the foundation of the chapter because it teaches the tower to
move from signal to procedure. A signal that cannot be encoded remains a
received event. A signal that can be encoded can become part of a program. A
program that can be executed can become a higher-level instrument. The tower
has begun to climb.

The speedometer's boot sequence makes the point concrete. When the instrument
powers on, it does not begin by reporting speed. It clears registers, checks
the display, loads calibration constants, initializes timers, and waits for a
valid pulse stream. Boot is not decorative. Boot is the act by which the
instrument creates the conditions under which later zero and one states can
mean anything. A counter that has not been initialized can still contain bits,
but they are not yet a measurement.

Zero is equally procedural. The instrument may report zero because no pulses
have arrived in the current admissible window, because the vehicle is
stationary, because the sensor has failed and the display has been forced to a
safe state, or because the firmware has just cleared the register. These are
not the same physical situation. The tower cares about the procedure by which
zero is produced. A zero that follows a valid timeout is different from a zero
that follows a power-on reset.

One is procedural too. A single pulse is not one in the abstract. It is one
admitted crossing under the current source, gauge, and execution rule. The
pickup may see a voltage transition; the firmware must decide whether that
transition is the one it is allowed to count. Once counted, the pulse becomes a
unit in the instruction vocabulary. Before that, it is only a candidate mark.

This is why the chapter uses a minimal instruction set rather than a familiar
large one. A large instruction set would hide the problem under convenience.
Boot, zero, and one are almost embarrassingly small, and that smallness is
their virtue. The reader can see the transition from physical mark to symbolic
operation. The tower starts where the compiler can still account for every
gesture.

Execution then tests whether the gesture can live in time. A stored encoding
is like a service manual on the passenger seat. It may contain the right
procedure, but it is not yet measuring. Execution is the procedure acting under
conditions. It reads a state, produces another state, and leaves behind an
output relation. The same instruction can behave differently if the source,
clock, or gauge changes. That is why execution must be formal, not assumed.

The section's deeper claim is that instruction sets are themselves measuring
instruments. They measure what a machine can do directly. Every later program
is an experiment conducted inside that vocabulary. If the vocabulary is too
weak, the experiment cannot be expressed. If the vocabulary is too loose, the
experiment cannot be trusted. Encoding is the first contract between signal and
machine.

By the time the section ends, the reader should feel a small but crucial
shift. The tower is no longer asking only what a reading is. It is asking what
an instruction is. A reading that can instruct the next process has crossed a
new threshold. It has become executable material.

Executable material is still material. It can be malformed, stale, mismatched,
or loaded under the wrong source. A firmware patch can change how the same
pulse stream is interpreted. A diagnostic mode can execute a different path
from the driving mode. The tower must therefore remember that execution is
always execution of some encoding from some source. That memory is the first
defense against treating software behavior as invisible magic.

This is why debugging begins at the instruction level. When the displayed speed
is wrong, a careful engineer asks whether the sensor is producing marks, whether
the marks are admitted, whether the counter is initialized, whether the
calibration source is current, and whether the display routine is executing the
same instructions the specification describes. The formal tower keeps those
questions alive. Encoding is not a prelude to the real mathematics. It is the
floor on which every later mathematical action must stand.

\section{Abstraction and the Berry Paradox}
\label{se:abstraction-berry}

An abstraction is where a solved problem learns to impersonate a primitive.
Once the speedometer's firmware has a trusted routine for counting pulses over
a window, later code does not need to reprove pulse counting every time it
asks for speed. It calls the routine. The routine behaves like a new primitive
for the next layer. This is useful, dangerous, and unavoidable.

The danger is self-reference. An abstraction can refer to what came before it,
but it can also be tempted to define itself by the very act of definition. The
Berry paradox is the classic warning: "the smallest positive integer not
definable in under eleven words" appears to define such an integer in under
eleven words. The phrase trips over its own power. It uses the system's
definition machinery to point at the boundary of definability from inside that
same machinery.

Chapter 5 cannot ignore this. The tower has just learned how to encode and
execute instructions. It now wants abstractions so later floors do not have to
carry every lower detail explicitly. But if abstraction is allowed to float
freely, it can become the same trap: a process that claims more definitional
power than it has earned.

\lean{Abstraction} has three roles: satire, compile, and execute. Satire is
the base case, the deliberately awkward reminder that not every formal object
is already a useful machine action. Compile and execute are the two serious
movements. Compile turns an encoding into a form the machine can carry.
Execute runs the carried form. A speedometer's calibration table is compiled
into firmware; the firmware is executed when pulses arrive. Confusing those
acts is how instruments lie to themselves.

\begin{leancode}
| satire : Fact -> Abstraction\\
| compile : Fact -> Encoding ->\\
\quad Abstraction -> Abstraction\\
| execute : Fact -> Encoding ->\\
\quad Abstraction -> Abstraction
\end{leancode}

The order on abstractions is careful because the tower cannot allow every
abstraction to compare with every other abstraction. Compile may sit below
execute because compilation prepares execution. Satire is not simply smaller
than execution. It is a different kind of formal gesture. The code's ordering
is not a mathematical flourish. It is a guardrail against pretending that every
named abstraction belongs on one clean ladder.

The strict order matters even more. A nonterminating or self-referential
ordering would ruin the tower. To say that one abstraction is less than another
must itself terminate as a usable comparison. The tower is not allowed to
define an order whose own use reopens the Berry problem. Abstraction must be
bounded enough to be executed by the compiler that carries it.

\lean{VALUE} wraps abstraction as carried context: an abstraction and a
less-than predicate. A value is not a naked object. It is an object inside the
process that knows how to compare and carry it forward.

In the speedometer, a value is never naked. "Sixty" on the dial is not just
the integer 60. It is 60 under a unit, a calibration, a timing window, a
filter, a source, and an executed display routine. The value can be compared
with 55 or 65 because the instrument carries the abstraction that makes such
comparison meaningful. Without that abstraction, the number is a symbol with
no operational home.

\begin{gphenom}{Galileo-Abel}
\label{ph:galileo-abel}
\GPhObs Galileo's free-fall law separates the law of acceleration from the mass
of the falling body. Abel's impossibility theorem separates polynomial roots
from the hope of one general radical formula for all quintics. In both cases, a
familiar presentation is not the same as the structure it tries to describe.
\GPhCon An instrument may change presentation without changing the law, and a
presentation may fail without destroying the object. The invariant is not the
surface form. It is the dependency relation that survives the allowed change.
\GPhSeq \lean{VALUE} preserves the order on \lean{Abstraction}: compile remains
below execute. The carried value is useful because the dependency order
survives the presentation in which the instrument meets it.
\end{gphenom}

The phenomenon box names the good side of abstraction. An invariant can survive
changes in representation. Galileo's falling bodies and Abel's transformations
both teach that law is not the same thing as presentation. Chapter 5 wants the
same strength for the tower. If compile is structurally before execute, that
relation should not depend on the incidental instrument doing the work.

But invariance is earned only when self-reference is controlled. The Berry
paradox shows what happens when naming becomes too powerful. The
\lean{Abstraction} layer makes the reference structure explicit and bounded. It does not
let the tower say "the abstraction that defeats this abstraction" and then
walk away smiling. It asks which constructor is being used, which encoding is
being carried, and which comparison is allowed to terminate.

This section therefore gives the tower its second floor. The first floor was
instruction. The second is reusable procedure. The price of reuse is
discipline. An abstraction may impersonate a primitive only after the compiler
can see how it was compiled, how it may be executed, and how it avoids eating
its own definition.

The speedometer's unit conversion is a gentle example. Once the firmware knows
how to convert wheel pulses into miles per hour, later routines can treat that
conversion as a primitive. A cruise controller may ask whether speed is below a
target. A warning routine may ask whether speed exceeds a limit. Those later
routines need not rebuild the conversion each time. They rely on an
abstraction. But if the conversion routine secretly depends on the warning
routine, or if its definition changes depending on who asks, the instrument has
created a Berry-like knot inside its own dashboard.

Good abstraction therefore has provenance. It remembers the encoding it
compiled from, the conditions under which it may execute, and the comparison
rule by which it can sit below or above another abstraction. This is why the
constructors carry facts and encodings. The abstraction is not an empty label.
It is a record of how a lower process became a reusable higher process.

The satire constructor keeps the chapter from becoming too solemn about its
own abstractions. Some formal gestures look like abstractions but do not yet
function as reusable machine parts. Satire is the tower's way of preserving a
place for that awkwardness. Not every named thing has earned execution. Not
every clever phrase has become a floor. This is a surprisingly practical
warning in formal work, where a seductive definition can feel like progress
before it has been connected to anything.

The Berry paradox also explains why less-than matters more than it first
appears. Ordering abstractions is a way to control dependency. Compile before
execute is not merely a slogan; it is a termination discipline. If execution
could be prior to its own compilation, or if a definition could be ordered by a
comparison that requires the definition itself, the tower would circulate
without climbing. The strict order forces a direction.

This direction is what lets later floors spend abstraction safely. Arithmetic
will soon use operations as if they were available units. The spline will use
polynomial structure as if it were a tool. The totality proof will use
instances as if each floor can be summoned by name. All of that depends on the
Berry problem being contained here. The tower must know the difference between
a reusable primitive and a self-referential trap.

The speedometer's calibration routine again gives a practical version. A
calibration can be loaded and executed. Later speed estimates can depend on it.
But the calibration cannot be defined as "the calibration that makes this
speedometer correct in all cases where its correctness is questioned." That
sentence sounds clever and measures nothing. A usable calibration has finite
source, bounded procedure, and known application. It can be wrong, but it is
not self-devouring.

Abstraction is therefore not the enemy of measurement. It is the way
measurement becomes usable at scale. The danger is unreceipted abstraction,
abstraction without source, execution, or terminating order. Chapter 5 allows
abstraction only after it has learned to carry its receipts.

The practical payoff is maintenance. A mechanic can replace a speed sensor
because the rest of the instrument treats the sensor through an abstraction:
produce admissible pulses under this gauge. The replacement part does not need
to be metaphysically identical to the old part. It must satisfy the abstraction
the tower uses. Good abstraction makes substitution possible without erasing
accountability. Bad abstraction makes substitution look possible until the
system fails.

The Berry paradox is the nightmare version of bad substitution. A phrase
pretends to substitute for a bounded description while smuggling in the whole
act of description. Chapter 5's abstraction layer refuses that trick. It lets a
solved process stand in for a primitive only when the standing-in has a visible
source and a direction of execution. The result is not less abstraction. It is
abstraction strong enough to be maintained.

\section{Arithmetic Assembled}
\label{se:arithmetic-assembled}

The tower can now encode instructions and control abstraction. It still does
not have ordinary arithmetic as a gift. Chapter 5 insists that arithmetic be
assembled. This is one of the book's most important refusals. Arithmetic is
not a bag of familiar operators imported because the reader expects them. It
is a set of behaviors the ledger earns by constructing the processes that make
them usable.

The speedometer again keeps the refusal honest. A dashboard can display speed
only after it has assembled addition, multiplication, scaling, basis choice,
loading, and smoothing in a concrete procedure. Pulses must be summed. Counts
must be multiplied by calibration factors. Time windows must scale the result.
Encoded sensor data must be loaded into the process and decoded back into a
reported value. The arithmetic is not floating above the instrument. It is the
instrument's internal labor.

Episode 6 begins with \lean{Sum}: zero and add. Addition is the first
assembled operation because the instrument must accumulate. A speedometer
counts wheel events in a window. A fuel injector counts pulses. A Geiger
counter counts clicks. Sum is not glamorous, but without it there is no
running ledger of events. \lean{MAGNITUDE} then asks whether one sum can
dominate another. Counting becomes comparison of size.

The next form is \lean{Product}. It has origin, one, and multiplication.
Product is where scaling enters. A speedometer multiplies wheel rotations by a
circumference. A display may multiply by a unit conversion. A controller may
multiply by a gain. Multiplication is not just repeated addition in this
context. It is the formal act of making one accumulated quantity act as a
scale on another.

\lean{SCALED} asks about orthogonality. Two products may scale along
independent directions. A speedometer's pulse count and its clock count are
not the same dimension; they meet in a ratio. A vector space needs basis
structure because not every direction is interchangeable with every other
direction. Orthogonality is the promise that two scaling roles can be kept
apart while still belonging to the same assembled arithmetic.

\lean{Basis} then gives the tower a three-part vector-space skeleton:
null-space, origin, and basis. This is not yet a full linear algebra course.
It is the minimum formal shape needed to say that some changes do nothing, some
point marks the start, and some set of directions spans the space in which the
instrument will operate. For the speedometer, the null-space may be
disturbance that does not move the reported value. The origin is zero speed.
The basis is the calibration structure by which pulses and time become a
speed dimension.

\lean{LOAD} adds decoding. This is crucial. A model may accept encoded data
without being able to recover the record it needs. A speedometer that logs raw
sensor ticks but cannot decode them back into calibrated speed has stored
activity, not measurement. Loading is not merely placing data in memory. It is
placing data where the process can invert enough of the encoding to answer the
question being asked.

\begin{gphenom}{Dirichlet-Bancroft}
\label{ph:dirichlet-bancroft}
\GPhObs Dirichlet's function is 1 on rationals and 0 on irrationals. It is
defined everywhere but Riemann-integrable nowhere. A model can be complete --
defined for every input -- while being useless for measurement.
\GPhCon Model completeness is not ledger sufficiency. A model that cannot
invert its encoding cannot recover the original record, even if it is defined
for every possible input.
\GPhSeq \lean{LOAD}'s \lean{decoded?} predicate marks this boundary. A model
accepted by the compiler is not necessarily a model that can answer questions.
Completeness is cheap; sufficiency is earned.
\end{gphenom}

The phenomenon box is a warning against a common mathematical comfort. A
function can be total and still useless for the measurement at hand. Dirichlet's
function is defined everywhere, but it does not give the kind of integrable
structure an instrument can use for ordinary accumulation. Likewise, a data
model can accept every input and still fail to produce a recoverable record.
The tower cares about sufficiency, not mere total domain.

\lean{FINITE\_ELEPHANT} appears as the digital-to-analog layer. The name is
comic, but the role is serious. A finite process may produce smooth behavior
only by passing through a structure that knows how discrete values become
oscillation, approximation, or display. A speedometer's digital count becomes a
smooth needle only through such a layer. The smoothness is not imported. It is
generated from finite instructions.

\lean{BASICProcess} adds minimal flow control: GOSUB and GOTO. This is not
nostalgia. The point is that once arithmetic is assembled, the instrument needs
control flow. It must branch, return, and repeat. A speedometer must reset a
window, continue a filter, jump to a fault routine, or return to display. Even
the humblest arithmetic needs a little program around it.

By the end of this section, arithmetic has become a working floor of the
tower. Addition, magnitude, product, scale, basis, load, finite smoothing, and
flow control have all appeared as explicit structures. The reader is not being
asked to forget ordinary arithmetic. The reader is being asked to notice what
ordinary arithmetic costs when an instrument must actually carry it.

The cost appears whenever a reading is used outside the narrow context in which
it was first produced. A pulse count by itself can be added. To become speed,
it must be divided by time or multiplied by a reciprocal time scale. To become
distance, it must be accumulated against circumference. To become acceleration,
it must be compared against another speed across another interval. Each new
quantity is arithmetically familiar, but each requires a different load path
through the instrument.

This is why basis matters. The instrument must know which dimensions it is
assembling. Pulse count, clock ticks, wheel circumference, and display units do
not belong to one undifferentiated heap. They are coordinates in a working
space. The null-space matters because some disturbances should vanish from the
reported value. The origin matters because zero speed is not the same as a
missing reading. The basis matters because the instrument must know how to
combine independent directions without confusing them.

The finite elephant is funny because the task is large and the carrier is
small. A finite digital process is asked to produce behavior that appears
smooth, analog, and continuous. That is exactly what dashboards do. A digital
controller updates a display often enough that the driver experiences motion
rather than discrete jumps. The elephant is finite because every update is
discrete. It is large because the displayed phenomenon pretends to be smooth.

Flow control completes the floor because arithmetic is never merely an
expression in a living instrument. It is a path through cases. If the pulse
window is empty, do one thing. If the count is implausible, do another. If the
sensor has failed, branch to a diagnostic routine. If the value is valid, load
the display update. BASIC's old GOTO and GOSUB are crude names for a permanent
truth: computation needs routes as well as formulas.

The assembled arithmetic section is therefore less about inventing addition
and multiplication than about domesticating them. The tower wants these
operations to live inside measurement rather than above it. Sum is
accumulation with receipts. Product is scaling with receipts. Basis is
direction with receipts. Load is decoding with receipts. Flow control is
procedure with receipts. The repeated phrase is the point.

Once arithmetic has receipts, it can be trusted by later smooth constructions.
Without receipts, a smooth curve may be built from numbers whose origins have
already been forgotten. With receipts, the curve remains tethered to the
instrument. The speedometer can draw a needle path because the arithmetic below
the path has not dissolved into anonymous calculation.

There is also a lesson about units. Arithmetic that ignores units can produce
formally valid nonsense. Adding pulse counts to milliseconds is not a speed
calculation. Multiplying by a circumference in the wrong unit can produce a
beautifully precise wrong answer. The tower's assembled arithmetic must
therefore remember basis and load, not just operator shape. The speedometer
works because the arithmetic is dimensional before it is numerical.

This is why "assembled" is the right word. The pieces have to fit. Addition,
product, scaling, basis, loading, and flow control are not independent
decorations. They are parts of one mechanism. The section teaches the reader
to inspect the mechanism before trusting the number it emits.

The assembled mechanism is also reusable. Once the speedometer has an honest
way to add pulses, scale by circumference, compare against time, and load the
result into a display routine, later systems can use speed as an input. Cruise
control, braking assistance, navigation, and fault diagnostics all inherit the
assembled arithmetic. They do not inherit a naked number. They inherit a number
with a machine history, which is the only kind this tower trusts.

\section{The Spline}
\label{se:the-spline}

A speedometer needle should not twitch with every raw pulse. It should move
smoothly enough to be readable and responsively enough to be useful. That
smoothness is not the same as the raw data. It is an interpolated behavior
constructed from finite observations under constraints. The chapter names this
floor \lean{Spline}.

A spline is a curve built from pieces. It passes through or near selected
points called knots while satisfying smoothness conditions between them. The
important lesson is that the continuum is constructed, not assumed. A finite
instrument does not receive a continuous speed field from heaven. It receives
discrete observations and builds a smooth report under rules.

\lean{Spline} has three constructors: observation, knot, and interpolant. An
observation is the raw admitted fact. A knot is a constraint that holds the
curve at a meaningful location. An interpolant is the smooth structure that
threads the observed and constrained record. The smooth curve is not a
primitive object. It is the result of a finite process that has learned how to
respect its data.

\begin{leancode}
| observation : Prop -> Spline\\
| knot : Prop -> Polynomial -> Spline -> Spline\\
| interpolant :\\
\quad Fact -> Polynomial -> Polynomial ->\\
\quad Spline -> Spline -> Spline
\end{leancode}

The speedometer's display filter is a small spline-like story. The instrument
has observed pulse counts at times. It has constraints: the needle cannot jump
infinitely fast, the display should not flicker, the reported speed should
follow sustained acceleration. The interpolated display is the curve the driver
sees. It is not every raw pulse. It is a constructed reading that honors the
knots the instrument regards as admissible.

The chapter also invokes polynomials and Galerkin projection. That method
projects a continuous problem onto a finite basis. Instead of solving in an
infinite space directly, one chooses basis functions and asks for the best
finite approximation inside their span. This is finite element thinking:
approximate the continuous by assembling local, computable pieces.

The tower needs this because measurement sits between finite records and
continuous claims. Physics speaks of smooth motion, fields, curvature, and
flow. Instruments produce finite entries. The spline is the bridge. It says:
given observations, knots, and polynomial structure, the ledger may construct a
smooth-enough object without pretending smoothness was present before the
construction.

This matters for the book's relationship to calculus. A derivative of a raw
pulse train is mostly nonsense. A derivative of an interpolated curve may be
usable if the interpolation process is part of the record. A speedometer can
estimate acceleration only after speed has been smoothed into a path whose
change makes sense. The spline is therefore not decoration. It is what lets
later differential language have a measured subject.

The word "unique" should also be handled with care. In ordinary spline theory,
constraints can determine a unique admissible interpolant under chosen
conditions such as degree, knots, and smoothness. The tower's point is not that
every set of observations magically yields one obvious curve. The point is that
when the constraints are named, the interpolant is accountable to them. A
different knot choice or smoothness condition is a different gauge of the
continuous report.

For the speedometer, this is the difference between a racing display and a
family sedan display. The racing display may prefer responsiveness; the sedan
may prefer smoothness. The same pulses can produce different visible curves
because the interpolation constraints differ. Neither curve is the wheel
itself. Each is a constructed report for a purpose.

The spline also protects the reader from another false comfort: that finite
data automatically imply a continuum. They do not. Infinitely many curves can
pass through finitely many points. The instrument earns its curve by adding
constraints, basis, and admissibility. The curve is a measured artifact. It is
not a metaphysical disclosure.

This section gives the tower its smooth floor. Arithmetic can now become a
path. Encoded values can be loaded, scaled, assembled, and interpolated. The
needle can move as if it were continuous because the tower has built the
conditions under which that motion is an honest report.

The spline also helps the reader understand why approximation is not a
confession of failure. Every instrument approximates. The question is whether
the approximation is accountable. A speedometer display that updates ten times
per second is not lying because it does not show infinitely many values between
updates. It is honest if the interpolation rule, update window, and smoothing
constraints are appropriate to the measurement task. Approximation becomes
deception only when the rule is hidden or when the smooth report claims more
than the finite record can support.

Knots are the moral center of the spline. A knot says: this finite constraint
matters. The curve may be smooth between knots, but it must answer to them. In
experimental practice, knots are calibration points, observed samples, boundary
conditions, or conservation constraints. For the speedometer, knots might be
verified pulse counts at known wheel speeds, zero at rest, or a maximum
allowed acceleration of the display needle. The interpolant is free only within
the discipline those knots impose.

The Galerkin idea reinforces that discipline. Rather than pretending to solve
a continuous problem in its full infinite richness, one projects the problem
onto a finite basis. That projection is not a surrender. It is the way finite
machines do honest work with continuous phenomena. The basis is chosen, the
residual is controlled, and the approximation is judged relative to the chosen
space. A finite element mesh is not the object. It is the instrument's
accountable grip on the object.

The chapter's spline is therefore the first place where the tower begins to
look like engineering mathematics in the usual sense. Earlier floors built
receipts, instructions, values, sums, and products. The spline uses them to
make a shape. The shape can be seen, plotted, differentiated, and used by
later physics. But the reader should now know better than to treat the shape as
free. It is the child of finite records and named constraints.

There is a political lesson hiding in the mathematics as well. Smooth curves
are persuasive. They look more authoritative than the scattered points from
which they came. A chart with a smooth line can make sparse or noisy data feel
inevitable. The tower resists that seduction by making the spline's
construction visible. The curve is admissible only because its observations,
knots, polynomial basis, and interpolation rule have been named.

This is why the spline belongs before the totality proof. The tower must show
not only that it can build discrete arithmetic but that it can responsibly
construct the smooth objects later chapters will need. Totality without a
smooth floor would leave the later physics unsupported. Smoothness without
totality would leave the curve theatrical. Together they make a path the
compiler can climb and the reader can inspect.

The speedometer needle has become the chapter's simplest spline. It is a
smooth report assembled from finite rotations. It is useful because it hides
raw jitter, and honest only if the hiding has rules. The tower's spline keeps
those rules in the record.

A spline also gives the reader a way to understand prediction. Once a smooth
curve has been constructed, the instrument may estimate a value between
observed points. That estimate is not an observation. It is an interpolated
claim under the spline's constraints. A speedometer needle between pulses is
doing exactly this: it reports a continuous-looking value between discrete
sensor events. The report is useful because the interpolation has been made
part of the instrument.

Prediction beyond the knots is more dangerous. Extrapolation asks the spline
to speak outside the region where observations constrain it. A dashboard may
briefly carry a trend forward when pulses are delayed, but the longer it does
so, the more it owes the reader a warning. The tower's spline discipline
therefore teaches a difference between interpolation and prophecy. Smoothness
inside the record is one thing; confidence beyond the record is another.

This distinction will matter later, when the book talks about science and
physics. A theory often behaves like a spline at a higher scale. It honors
known observations, imposes smoothness and symmetry conditions, and then speaks
between or beyond the measured points. Chapter 5 wants the reader to feel the
difference between those acts. Interpolation inside a disciplined record is a
measured convenience. Extrapolation beyond it is a wager that must remain
visible as a wager.

The speedometer is humble enough to make the lesson merciful. When it smooths
between pulses, the driver benefits. When it keeps reporting a confident speed
after the sensor has gone silent, the driver is endangered. The same formal
curve can be helpful or dishonest depending on where its constraints end. The
spline floor teaches the tower to know that boundary before later theory asks
for more.

\section{The Totality Proof}
\label{se:totality-proof}

A tower made only of definitions might still be imaginary. One can describe
beautiful floors that no one can inhabit. Lean demands more. To declare an
instance is to provide a concrete inhabitant of a class. The compiler does not
merely admire the blueprint. It asks whether each promised floor can actually
be built on the floor below.

Episode 7 provides the cascade. Seventeen chained instances carry the tower
from the early promises through the later machinery. Each instance is local.
It says: assuming the lower floors, here is how this floor is inhabited. The
global result is the tower, but the proof proceeds by local construction. This
is why the chapter can claim totality. The tower is not only a collection of
names. It has been instantiated.

The speedometer's manufacturing line offers a concrete analogy. A design file
may say that the instrument has a sensor, a counter, a filter, a display, and a
calibration routine. But the manufactured unit must pass tests. Does the sensor
produce pulses? Does the counter advance? Does the filter settle? Does the
display respond? Does the calibration fit the tire radius? Each test is local,
but passing all of them makes the instrument real.

The totality proof is that test bench for the formal tower. The compiler checks
that each class can be inhabited under the accumulated assumptions. A missing
instance is a missing floor. A mismatched dependency is a beam that does not
land on its support. The proof is not glamorous, and that is why it matters.
Real towers stand by unglamorous load paths.

The heartbeat counter belongs here. Lean's elaborator works under resource
limits. When the tower becomes large enough, those limits have to be raised.
That is not merely an inconvenience. It is evidence that the compiler is doing
work. The tower is not a poem written in Lean syntax. It is a set of
dependencies the elaborator must actually traverse.

\begin{gphenom}{Markov-Conway}
\label{ph:markov-Conway}
\GPhObs Conway's Game of Life produces arbitrarily complex structures --
gliders, oscillators, universal computers -- from three local rules applied to
a binary grid. No global architect is required.
\GPhCon Global structure emerges from local admissibility conditions. The tower
does not need a designer hovering above it; it needs each floor to be locally
consistent with the floor below.
\GPhSeq Each typeclass instance in \episode{7} is a local rule. The instance
cascade is the Game of Life running on the tower: global structure, the
verified existence of the entire hierarchy, emerges from local consistency
checks.
\end{gphenom}

The Conway comparison is apt because no single cell in Life knows the glider.
Each cell follows a local rule. The glider is the pattern that emerges when
those rules are applied coherently. Likewise, no single instance in Episode 7
is the whole tower. Each instance confirms one local promise. The tower is the
pattern produced by the cascade.

This is also why totality differs from completeness. Completeness might mean
that every syntactic piece has been named. Totality means the construction has
inhabitants all the way up. A speedometer manual may list every subsystem. The
test bench asks whether a real unit exists whose subsystems work together. The
compiler is the test bench.

There is a philosophical temptation to treat formalization as a decorative
appendix to prose. This chapter rejects that. The prose can explain why the
tower matters, but the instance cascade is what makes the tower more than
explanation. Lean does not care whether the metaphor is attractive. It checks
whether the promised structure can be elaborated.

The result is modest and enormous. Modest, because the tower is local to this
construction and inherits its rough edges. Enormous, because a verified
inhabitant changes the status of the manuscript. The book is no longer only
saying "imagine a hierarchy like this." It is saying "here is a hierarchy the
compiler has been made to accept."

By the end of Chapter 5, the tower stands. It has an instruction floor, an
abstraction floor, an arithmetic floor, a spline floor, and a totality proof
that the floors are not merely decorative. The next chapter can ask what the
compiler finds expensive because there is now a real object whose expense can
be felt.

The totality proof also changes the burden of disagreement. Before the proof,
one might object that the tower is only a sequence of suggestive names. After
the proof, the better objection has to be more precise. Which instance fails
to mean what the prose says? Which dependency is too weak? Which abstraction is
too expensive? Which floor should be rearranged? The compiler has not made the
book unquestionable. It has made the questions sharper.

This is the real gift of formalization. It does not end interpretation. It
focuses interpretation. A reader can still disagree with the modeling choices,
but the choices are no longer fog. They have names, dependencies, and
inhabitants. The tower may be eccentric, but it is not merely atmospheric. It
has joints one can inspect.

The speedometer analogy ends here with a passed bench test. The instrument is
not perfect. It may be calibrated for the wrong tire. It may smooth too much or
too little. It may fail under conditions the designer did not anticipate. But
it is no longer a sketch. It powers on, receives pulses, executes procedures,
assembles arithmetic, moves a needle, and can be tested as a unit. That is what
the instance cascade gives the formal tower.

The raised heartbeat is part of the story because it registers strain. A proof
that costs nothing might still be correct, but a proof that makes the compiler
work reminds the reader that dependency has computational weight. The tower is
not an outline drawn in air. It is something the elaborator has to carry. The
next chapter will make that weight explicit by measuring the price of
precision.

The totality proof is therefore both ending and invitation. It ends the
construction phase by showing that the floors can stand together. It invites
the cost phase by revealing that standing together is not free. A verified
tower still has load, strain, and maintenance. Chapter 6 will listen for that
strain.

The instance cascade also teaches that verification is cumulative. The last
instance does not float above the first. It depends on the chain of prior
inhabitants. If an early promise changes, the upper floors may have to be
rebuilt. This is familiar to anyone who has changed a sensor specification and
then watched calibration, filtering, display, and diagnostics all need review.
Formal dependency is not bureaucracy. It is how the tower remembers its own
load paths.

There is a second lesson: local success does not mean global triviality. Each
instance may be understandable on its own, yet the cascade can still be hard
for the compiler. Many small obligations become a large elaboration problem.
That is why the raised heartbeat is narratively right. The compiler's strain
is the felt cost of totality. The tower stands, and the compiler has the
sweat to prove it.

The proof also marks a change in what the manuscript can responsibly claim.
Before totality, the prose could gesture toward a possible hierarchy. After
totality, the prose can refer to an inhabited hierarchy. That does not make
every interpretation correct, but it changes the ground. The reader is now
arguing with a built object, not only with an analogy.

Totality also gives the reader confidence in the chapter's pacing. The earlier
sections may feel slow because they refuse shortcuts. The proof reveals why
that patience mattered. Each floor was prepared so that the next floor could
actually be inhabited. The speedometer was not assembled slowly because the
builder loved ceremony. It was assembled slowly because skipping a floor would
leave no place for the test bench to attach its probes.

This is why Chapter 5 is the hinge between construction and critique. Chapter
6 can measure bullshit because Chapter 5 has given it something real enough to
strain. Without totality, cost would be theatrical. With totality, cost becomes
diagnostic.

The chapter ends construction without ending responsibility. A verified tower
can still be awkward, expensive, overly specific, or hard to extend. Totality
does not mean the final design is beautiful. It means the design has crossed
from rhetoric into artifact. Chapter 6 will take that artifact seriously enough
to ask how much precision it can afford.

This is why the proof feels like a handoff rather than a curtain call. The
tower is now sturdy enough to be measured. Its practical measured strength can be tested, its
costs can be counted, and its refinements can be judged against the work they
actually demand from the compiler and the patient reader who must follow the climb.

\section*{Coda}
\markboth{CODA}{CODA}

A tower can also be heard as an organ. The pipes visible to the audience are
not the instrument by themselves. Beneath them are keys, trackers, valves,
windchests, reservoirs, stops, and the bellows that give the whole system
breath. Pressing a key does not create a note directly. It opens a path. Air
travels through a mechanism, chooses a pipe, meets a resonant body, and becomes
sound.

Chapter 5 has built its tower like that organ. Encoding is the keyboard: a
finite vocabulary of actions the player can actually press. Abstraction is the
stop mechanism: one gesture can bring a prepared bank of pipes into play.
Arithmetic is the windchest: pressure, distribution, scaling, and routing.
The spline is the pipe speaking smoothly from a finite opening. The totality
proof is the moment the builder pumps the bellows and discovers that the
instrument really sounds.

The metaphor helps because an organ is hierarchical without being abstract in
the empty sense. Every level has a job. A key with no valve is decoration. A
valve with no wind is motion without sound. Wind with no pipe is pressure
without pitch. A pipe with no tuning is noise with ambition. Only the assembled
instrument can play.

The speedometer taught the chapter through engineering. The organ teaches it
through resonance. A tower is not only a stack of supports; it is a stack of
permissions for vibration. Lower floors make higher sounds possible. Higher
sounds do not erase the lower floors. When a chord fills a room, the listener
does not hear the tracker action, but the tracker action is inside the chord's
possibility.

So it is with the formal tower. A later value may sound like a primitive, but
inside it are instructions, execution, abstraction, sums, products, bases,
loads, splines, and instances. The point of the chapter is not to make the
reader stare forever at the mechanism. It is to let the reader hear the note
without forgetting that the note has a mechanism.

An organ also teaches why totality matters. A drawn stop that opens no pipe is
a broken promise. A beautiful console connected to no wind is a theatrical
object, not an instrument. The instance cascade is the wind test. It says that
the keys are connected, the valves open, the wind arrives, and the pipes
answer. The tower has breath.

The next chapter will ask about cost. An organ makes that question natural.
Some stops require more wind. Some chords strain the reservoir. Some pipes
speak slowly. Some combinations are possible but impractical in a small room.
Likewise, some formal refinements compile easily, some force the elaborator to
work, and some reveal that precision has a price. The tower stands, but a
standing tower is not free.

Keep the organ in mind when the book turns to the bullshit meter. The meter is
not merely mocking complexity. It is listening for strain in the mechanism. A
sound may be possible and still costly. A chord may be correct and still draw
too much wind. A formal layer may be true and still tell the reader that the
instrument is nearing the edge of what it can comfortably carry.

Chapter 5 therefore ends with a working instrument rather than a final
philosophy. The keys can be pressed. The pipes can sound. The tower can carry a
signal upward into arithmetic and smooth report. That is enough. The next
question is how much breath the music costs.

The organ metaphor also gives the reader a way to hold abstraction without
losing materiality. A stop labeled "flute" does not contain a flute in the
orchestral sense. It summons a rank of pipes shaped to speak with a certain
color. The label is an abstraction, but the abstraction is connected to wood,
metal, air, and tuning. The label works because the mechanism beneath it is
real.

So too with \lean{VALUE}, \lean{Spline}, and the other upper floors. Their
names should become playable stops, not disembodied slogans. Pulling the stop
should bring a mechanism into relation with the rest of the instrument. If a
name summons nothing, the tower has decorated itself instead of extending
itself.

An organist also knows that the same key can speak differently depending on
which stops are drawn. That is not contradiction. It is controlled
registration. A formal value likewise speaks differently depending on the
abstraction, basis, load, and spline through which it is carried. The tower
does not seek a single context-free sound. It seeks controlled relations among
contexts.

The audience hears music, not mechanism, but the builder cannot afford that
innocence. The builder must know which pipe is slow to speak, which valve leaks
wind, which stop draws too much pressure, which rank goes sharp in the heat.
The next chapter's bullshit meter is the builder's ear. It hears strain where
the audience hears only volume.

This is why the coda does not end with triumph. The organ sounds, but the
sound opens the next question. Can the instrument sustain the chord? Can the
bellows supply the wind? Can the mechanism be tuned without making the player
fight the keys? A tower that stands still has to be played. A formal tower
that compiles still has to be used.

The chapter's achievement is therefore concrete and provisional. Concrete,
because the tower has instances. Provisional, because every working instrument
invites the question of cost. Chapter 5 has given the book a playable machine.
Chapter 6 will ask where the machine strains, where precision is expensive,
and where refinement should stop.

The organ also reminds the reader that hierarchy can be beautiful without
being mystical. The beauty is in coupling. A key is coupled to a valve, a valve
to wind, wind to a pipe, a pipe to a room, a room to an ear. The tower's beauty
is the same kind: instruction to execution, execution to abstraction,
abstraction to arithmetic, arithmetic to spline, spline to instance,
instance to use.

If one coupling is broken, the music changes. Sometimes the failure is silent.
Sometimes it is a cipher, a note that will not speak. Sometimes it is a cipher
that speaks too loudly because a valve leaks. Formal systems have similar
failures: missing instances, runaway searches, definitions that are too broad,
abstractions that compile but do not clarify. The next chapter's meter will
listen for these failures as changes in cost.

The coda's organ gives another pattern-matching surface for the reader. The
speedometer says tower as instrument. The organ says tower as resonance. The
same formal structure can be seen through both. One emphasizes calibration and
display. The other emphasizes breath and coupling. Together they teach that a
verified hierarchy is not dead scaffolding. It is a mechanism that can carry
motion into sound.

And sound, like measurement, happens in time. A chord has attack, sustain, and
decay. The tower has boot, execution, abstraction, interpolation, and proof.
The reader should not imagine the finished hierarchy as a frozen diagram. It
is a playable sequence. The value of the tower is that it can be entered,
activated, and made to carry a signal upward.

In that sense, the organ returns us to the dashboard. The driver does not see
the cable, counter, firmware, or filter, just as the listener does not see the
windchest. Yet both experiences depend on hidden couplings. The ethical demand
of the chapter is to enjoy the output without becoming innocent about the
mechanism. A good reader can hear the note and still remember the bellows.

The tower is meant to be used. It should support later chapters the way an
organ supports a hymn: by disappearing enough for the melody to move and
remaining structured enough that the melody has somewhere to live. When the
book turns to science and physics, it will draw on the tower's stops without
rebuilding every pipe. This coda asks the reader to remember that the pipes are
there.

The next chapter will put a meter on the instrument. That may sound suspicious
after such a patient construction, but it is actually a compliment. Only a real
instrument can be tuned. Only a sounding organ can reveal where wind is wasted,
where a pipe refuses to speak, or where a chord asks too much of the bellows.
The bullshit meter is not coming to sneer at the tower. It is coming because
the tower is finally substantial enough to strain.

That is a good place to leave the reader: not in triumph, but with an
instrument under the hands. The next sound will not be another foundation being
poured. It will be the tower learning how much pressure its own music requires
from breath, hand, and proof.
