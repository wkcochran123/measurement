\chapter{The Number Ladder}
\label{chap:number-ladder}

% EPISODE COVERAGE: Episode 1 (completion) -- DISTINGUISHABLE and the
% measurement-grounded number ladder through Natural, Rational, Sequence, Limit.
% KEY CONCEPT: numbers as settled residues of comparison.

Chapter 1 ended with carriage: one mark, one value, one event rule. A carrier
can hold a mark forward. It cannot yet count with any richness. It cannot
compare pieces. It cannot say what it means for a record to settle. To see what
is missing, look at the instruments that already know how to live by marks.

A wheel-speed sensor watches a toothed ring pass a pickup. The sensor is not
given speed. It is given a stream of changes in its own state. A tooth passes;
the signal rises. The tooth leaves; the signal falls. The event is not the
tooth by itself, and it is not the sensor by itself. The event is the
comparison between the state a moment ago and the state now. A pulse is the
record that the comparison crossed a boundary.

That pulse is already numerical in the only sense an instrument can trust. It
is a witnessed mark. The instrument can count such marks because each pulse
has cleared the same comparison again: tooth against no tooth, signal against
baseline, crossing against silence. The speedometer does not know speed. It
knows teeth. Speed is what you get when you ask how many teeth passed in how
many reference ticks. The reference clock supplies the second count. Speed is
the comparison of the two counts.

The odometer is the same discipline with a longer memory. It asks whether the
accumulated count is larger than it was. The wheel has turned again; another
tooth has crossed; the ledger of crossings has grown. Distance is not touched
directly. It is inferred from an accumulation of comparisons. In this modest
sense, the odometer is the first integration: it keeps summing witnessed
crossings across time without ever naming distance as something it can grasp
by itself.

GPS makes the same lesson richer without changing it. A satellite broadcasts a
carrier wave. The receiver generates a local copy and compares phase against
phase: arriving oscillation against local reference. Whole cycles give a count.
Fractional phase gives the leftover piece. Successive epochs give a progression.
A position fix appears only when the remaining candidate differences have
settled below what the instrument can use.

The receiver still has not touched distance directly. It has compared
oscillations, accumulated phase, corrected its clock, and tested candidates
until the residue becomes usable. Bad geometry, multipath reflections, or a
drifting oscillator keep the residue alive. Good geometry lets it collapse into
a report.

The two instruments make one claim in common. A number is not a primitive
object handed to the ledger. It is the settled residue of comparison. Counting
teeth compares an event against silence. Resolving a GPS position compares
accumulated phase readings until no useful distinction remains.

This chapter formalizes that act one level at a time. It builds
\lean{Natural}, \lean{Rational}, \lean{Sequence}, and \lean{Limit}: whole
comparisons, directional comparisons, ordered progressions of comparisons, and
settled residues of progressions. The number forms are not imported from
mathematics. They are assembled from comparison.

This chapter stays with the forms the ledger can hold: whole counts, comparable
pieces, ordered progressions, and settling paths. The next chapter installs the
permissions by which those forms may be used: admission, bounded counting,
encoding, residue, sampling, observation, repetition, and closure.

\section{One Rule at a Time}
\label{se:one-rule-at-a-time}

Chapter 1 ended with a carrier process: a mark, a value, and a rule for what
happens next. That is already enough to remember something. It is not yet
enough to tell whether the next thing is new.

This gap is easy to miss because ordinary instruments hide it. A wheel-speed
sensor seems simply to count pulses. But before it can count a pulse, it must
distinguish a pulse from no pulse. It must be able to say that a tooth passing
the pickup is different from the baseline state in which no tooth has passed.
Without that difference, there is only a changing electrical environment and no
reportable event.

The first typeclass above \lean{CarrierProcess} gives the compiler this power.
It is called \lean{DISTINGUISHABLE}.

\begin{leancode}
@[reducible]\\
class DISTINGUISHABLE\\
\quad (Value: Type)\\
\quad (Carrier: CarrierProcess Value)\\
\quad where\\
\quad fact: Fact\\
\quad symbol: Type Value\\
\quad distinct? : Prop := True\\
\quad different? : Type Value -> Prop :=\\
\quad\quad fun s1 =>\\
\quad\quad\quad s1 \(\neq\) symbol\\
\quad dec\_distinct : DecidablePred different?
\end{leancode}

This is the tower's standard signature making its first appearance. From here
on, nearly every major promise in the device will be made relative to the two
parameters just displayed: a value type and a carrier process over that type.
The compiler is not being told that everything, everywhere, can be
distinguished. It is being told something conditional: given this kind of value
and this carrier process over that kind of value, the promised distinction can
be made.

This is the full form. Later class excerpts will not repeat this parameter
block unless the repetition itself teaches something new. They will show the
new constraint, predicate, or field by which the tower advances.

A parameterized typeclass is a promise that depends on what you bring to it.
\lean{DISTINGUISHABLE} does not float above the ledger as a universal decree.
It becomes active only when the compiler is given a particular \lean{Value} and
a particular \lean{Carrier}. Then the compiler holds the instance to its
promise.

The first field is the anchor.

\begin{leancode}
fact: Fact
\end{leancode}

The instance must supply a \lean{Fact}. Distinguishing power does not appear
from nowhere. It is tied back to the accepted mark from Chapter 1. The promise
to tell marks apart is therefore not free-floating vocabulary; it is grounded
in something the compiler already knows how to carry.

The second field names the symbol against which later candidates will be
tested.

\begin{leancode}
symbol: Type Value
\end{leancode}

This is not a particular reading of type \lean{Value}. It is the compiler's
formal representative of the type itself, used here as the symbol for the kind
of reading the carrier can hold. The instrument does not yet need a specific
wheel pulse. It needs the shape of what a pulse-like mark would be.

The third field is deliberately plain.

\begin{leancode}
distinct? : Prop := True
\end{leancode}

The default proposition says that distinction is available. This is not a
measured result yet. It is the minimal stance required for an instrument to
function as an instrument: unless the instance says otherwise, the carrier is
allowed to treat difference as a question worth asking.

The fourth field is the question itself.

\begin{leancode}
different? : Type Value -> Prop :=\\
\quad fun s1 =>\\
\quad\quad s1 \(\neq\) symbol
\end{leancode}

Given a candidate symbol, \lean{different?} asks whether that candidate differs
from the stored symbol. In the default case, difference is ordinary inequality.
The point is not that inequality is profound. The point is that the comparison
has become part of the formal record. The carrier can now ask, for an incoming
mark, whether it differs from what it was already holding.

The final field supplies the receipt for that question.

\begin{leancode}
dec\_distinct : DecidablePred different?
\end{leancode}

Chapter 1 used \lean{Decidable truth} to say that one particular claim could be
settled. Here the same discipline has been lifted from a single claim to a
predicate. \lean{DecidablePred different?} says that, for any candidate mark,
the compiler can decide whether \lean{different?} holds. The instrument does
not merely hope that difference can be recognized. It carries a method for
settling the question each time it is asked.

Return to the wheel. In Chapter 1, the carrier could hold a count and update
that count when an event arrived. Now the instrument has the minimum formal
equipment needed to recognize the event as a difference. A tooth passing the
sensor is not just activity. It is activity distinguishable from the baseline.
The ledger can now ask whether the next mark differs from the current symbol.

That question is still not enough to say which differences may be added to the
record. It only opens the gate. The next section asks what must be true before
a proposed next mark is allowed to enter.

\begin{gphenom}{Euclid}
\label{ph:euclid}
\GPhObs Euclid's first common notion: things equal to the same thing are equal
to each other. A geometric figure moved across a plane remains the same figure.
The triangle does not become a different triangle by being translated.
\GPhCon The ledger retains its prior entries under transformation. An event
recorded at time $t$ is still recorded after time $t+1$. Irreversibility
guarantees that prior entries cannot be un-witnessed.
\GPhSeq The ledger retains its prior entries under transformation because the
first typeclass in the tower enforces it: a proposed continuation must be
distinguishably different from what is already recorded. The past is fixed.
New entries must clear the bar of difference.
\end{gphenom}

Distinction tells the instrument that a mark is different from the baseline.
But difference alone does not accumulate. To track how many times the threshold
is crossed, the device needs a structure that stacks admitted marks without
losing their order. That structure is \lean{Natural}.

\section{The Natural Number}
\label{se:natural-number}

The next three sections form two pairs. \lean{Natural} and \lean{Rational}
count: first whole marks, then comparable pieces. \lean{Sequence} and
\lean{Limit} approach: first ordered progressions, then settling records. For
now, the chapter studies only the number forms and their orderings. The classes
that authorize their use come in the next chapter.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.19\textwidth}p{0.22\textwidth}p{0.25\textwidth}p{0.22\textwidth}@{}}
\lean{Natural} & \lean{Rational} & \lean{Sequence} & \lean{Limit} \\
\hline
whole count & comparable piece & ordered progression & settling path \\
finite difference & rate and slope & accumulation path & calculus limit \\
event index & ratio and rate & trajectory or field evolution &
reported reading
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The first row names the formal role each object plays in this chapter. The
second row names what the calculus chapter will do with the same forms. The
third row names what physics will ask instruments to measure with them. The
first new number is \lean{Natural}.

\begin{leancode}
inductive Natural where\\
\quad | zero : Fact -> Natural\\
\quad | number : Fact -> Number -> Natural -> Natural\\
\\
namespace Natural\\
def le : Natural -> Natural -> Prop\\
\quad | .zero \_, \_ => True\\
\quad | .number \_ \_ \_, .zero \_ => False\\
\quad | .number p1 n1' \_, .number p2 n2' \_ =>\\
\quad\quad p1.truth = p2.truth \(\wedge\) n1' <= n2'\\
\\
def lt : Natural -> Natural -> Prop :=\\
\quad fun n1 n2 => le n1 n2 \(\wedge\) \(\neg\) le n2 n1\\
end Natural\\
\\
instance : LE Natural where le := Natural.le\\
instance : LT Natural where lt := Natural.lt
\end{leancode}

This is not Lean's \lean{Nat}. It is not the empty set dressed up as zero and
successor. It is a measurement-grounded inductive. The zero case carries a
\lean{Fact}. Every later step carries another \lean{Fact}, a \lean{Number} from
Chapter 1, and the previous \lean{Natural}. A step is never naked. It arrives
with a receipt, a witnessed mark, and the count that came before it.

The ordering is one-directional. Zero is below everything. A nonzero natural is
not below zero. When two nonzero naturals are compared, their facts must agree
and the embedded \lean{Number}s must be ordered forward. There is no
contravariant branch here. Natural counting is the first place where the device
says: this way is forward, and only this way is forward.

This is the full inductive pattern. In later number forms, the zero case, the
terminal case, the strict-order definition, and the two order instances follow
this shape. When the only new work is the nonzero comparison clause, only that
clause will be shown.

Return to the wheel-speed sensor. A tooth passing the pickup is no longer just
activity distinguishable from baseline. As a \lean{Natural} step, it is a
whole counted event. The ledger has the shape of a running total.

Whole counts are not enough. Instruments also compare pieces: a rate, a ratio,
a fraction of full scale. To get there, the next number has to split the act of
counting in two.

\section{The Rational Number}
\label{se:rational-number}

The second number form is \lean{Rational}. It is the first number in the tower
with a real fork in its ordering rule.

The constructors and outer order cases follow the \lean{Natural} pattern. The
new work is the nonzero comparison clause.

\begin{leancode}
\quad | .number p1 n1' \_, .number p2 n2' \_ =>\\
\quad\quad (p1 = p2 \(\wedge\) n1' <= n2') \(\vee\)\\
\quad\quad (p1 \(\neq\) p2 \(\wedge\) n2' <= n1')
\end{leancode}

This is not \(\mathbb{Q}\). No field of rational numbers has been imported.
The device has only added a fork. If the facts agree, the \lean{Natural}s are
read forward. If the facts differ, the order reverses, as reciprocal
comparison requires. Same truth counts with the grain. Different truth counts
against it. That is the whole new move.

The physical payoff is ratios and rates. The instrument can now speak not only
of three teeth, but of a reading as a part of a scale or a rate between one
reading and the next. A piece without order is still not a trajectory. That is
the next form.

\section{The Sequence and the Limit}
\label{se:sequence-and-limit}

\lean{Sequence} is an ordered walk through \lean{Rational}s.

Its constructors and outer order cases again follow the established pattern.
The lifted clause shows how the fork now acts on rational payloads.

\begin{leancode}
\quad | .index p1 r1 \_, .index p2 r2 \_ =>\\
\quad\quad (p1.truth = p2.truth \(\wedge\) r1 <= r2) \(\vee\)\\
\quad\quad (p1.truth \(\neq\) p2.truth \(\wedge\) r2 <= r1)
\end{leancode}

This is not a topology object smuggled in under a familiar name. It is a
measurement-grounded progression. Each step carries a \lean{Fact}, a
\lean{Rational}, and the prior \lean{Sequence}. The ordering is the same
covariant and contravariant rule again, now applied one level higher. When the
facts agree, rational values are compared forward. When the facts differ, they
are compared backward.

What is new is memory of direction. A \lean{Rational} can describe a piece. A
\lean{Sequence} records pieces as a progression. The ledger can now ask whether
one record of readings comes before another record of readings, not merely
whether one rational value is bounded by another.

\lean{Limit} repeats the pattern one level higher. It is an ordered walk
through \lean{Sequence}s.

Only the lifted comparison clause is new.

\begin{leancode}
\quad | .index p1 s1 \_, .index p2 s2 \_ =>\\
\quad\quad (p1.truth = p2.truth \(\wedge\) s1 <= s2) \(\vee\)\\
\quad\quad (p1.truth \(\neq\) p2.truth \(\wedge\) s2 <= s1)
\end{leancode}

This is not \(\mathbb{R}\). A \lean{Limit} is not a completed real number. It
is a settling record built from sequences. The ordering repeats the same rule
one level higher: compare sequences forward when the facts agree, and backward
when the facts differ. No new directional machinery appears here. The device
keeps lifting the same comparison discipline onto the thing it just learned how
to compare.

\begin{sidebar}{Cauchy sequences}
\label{sb:cauchy-sequences}
A Cauchy sequence is a sequence whose terms get arbitrarily close to each
other, not to some named target. It is convergence without naming the limit.
The instrument can declare that its readings are settling without first knowing
what they settle to.
\end{sidebar}

This is where real-number behavior becomes visible without importing the real
line. A wheel-speed sensor can now be understood not only as counting pulses
or comparing rates, but as tracking a path of readings that may settle. The
number forms are now in place. They can count events, compare pieces, arrange
readings, and represent settling paths. That is enough for this chapter. The
ledger has learned what it can hold. The next chapter asks what it may admit,
bound, encode, sample, observe, repeat, and close.


\section*{Coda}
\markboth{CODA}{CODA}

A single note is already a comparison. A string vibrates against silence; the
ear hears that something has crossed out of stillness. A second note makes the
comparison audible as an interval. The two vibrations stand in a ratio. One
note is higher, or lower, or the same, and the direction matters. A scale is
not a heap of notes. It is an ordered walk through intervals. Each step is
heard against the one before it. A resolved chord is what happens when the
motion settles. The ear waits for the next demand and hears none.

The chapter's number ladder has that shape. A whole count is the note against
silence. A rational piece is the interval between counts, with a direction the
comparison itself determines. A sequence is the scale, not because it is
musical, but because its steps are remembered as steps. A limit is the
resolution: not the last sound in the universe, but the place where further
motion no longer changes what the ear can use.

A tide gauge offers a quick echo. A wave reaches the mark, or it does not.
High water is compared with low water, one cycle with another, until the
shoreline keeps the residue. The rock is not the ocean. It is what comparison
leaves when it has run long enough to become patient.

These are not analogies pasted onto arithmetic after the fact. They are ways
of feeling the same construction before naming it. A count that knows which
way is forward. A ratio that knows which direction its comparison runs. A
progression that knows which step comes next. A settling record that knows
when comparison has no useful remainder.

The chapter has built four objects, but they are not four unrelated things.
They are one act held at four distances from the mark. Comparison first
becomes count. Count becomes piece. Piece becomes path. Path becomes settling
value. The number line is not a gift the ledger receives from outside. It is
what comparison produces when it is applied to its own output, patiently, one
level at a time.

The next chapter changes the question. It will not ask what these forms are.
It will ask what they are permitted to become.
