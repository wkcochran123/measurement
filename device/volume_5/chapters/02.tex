\chapter{The Argument}
\label{chap:the-argument}

% Closed question: What public sentence does the procession of necessary names license?

A buyer wants to know whether to buy a used car at the offered price, walk
away, or renegotiate. A mechanic and the buyer move together through the
inspection: title, VIN, scan, fluids, tires, brakes, frame, test drive,
comparable prices, seller claims. The final sentence is not the car's
Truth. Under this inspection, the buyer may infer that the car is worth
buying at this price. Or not.

The procession decides which.

A public-water-safety decision, a court ruling about evidence
admissibility, a regulatory decision about drug approval: each carries a
contested institutional context the reader cannot ignore. The reader hears
those examples through a political filter before the names have done
their work. The used-car inspection is local. It involves a buyer, a
seller, a mechanic, and a sum of money. The decision affects the buyer's
family finances, not the city's water supply. The named procession can do
its work without competing with a pre-existing contested apparatus.

Each section also carries an example from another domain. The same
pressure that drives the inspection appears in pharmacies, kitchens,
libraries, courts, theaters, labs, museums, archives, and bakeries. The
domains are different. The obligation is the same.

The thirty-seven sections cluster into six movements. Each movement does
a specific kind of work for the procession.

The smallest claim a record can carry is that something has happened.

\section{Fact}
\label{se:fact}

Before anything can be distinguished, the record needs something it can
treat as the case.

The world is full of events that almost happened, partial reports that
might be claims, gestures that may or may not be assertions, and states
that are merely backgrounds. None of those is yet a fact. A fact is the
moment when an ambient possibility has been registered as having
happened, in some form the system can recognize. The registration may
be minimal. It may carry no information about who, when, where, or why.
It carries the assertion that something is now on the record as having
happened.

The smallness of the demand is its point. The procession cannot start
with something elaborate. The procession cannot demand that the first
move be evidence, measurement, or proof. The first move is the move
that produces something the procession can work on at all. Without
this move, there is nothing to distinguish, nothing to admit, nothing
to count.

\texttt{Fact}

A fact is the smallest public assertion. It is not yet a measurement.
It is not yet a comparison. It is not yet a count. It is the presence
of a claim in a form the system can carry. A fact says that this,
whatever it is, is on the record as having happened. The form of the
fact may be a checkbox, a timestamp, a signature, a note, an entry in
a log, a row in a table, or a single sentence on a page. The form is
not the fact. The fact is what the form registers.

A longer name would invite elaboration.
\texttt{REGISTERED\_INCOMING\_CLAIM} would suggest that the claim has
been classified, vetted, or characterized. None of that is required at
the level of \texttt{Fact}. The name marks the bare minimum: something
happened that the system is willing to treat as the case. The next
names will demand more of the fact. \texttt{Fact} itself demands only
that there be something to demand of.

A fact must be in a form the system can carry. A claim that the system
cannot register is not yet a fact in this procession. A rumor passing
through a hallway, a feeling, a suspicion, an undocumented impression:
these may correspond to events in the world, but they are not yet
facts in the record. The discipline excludes them not because they are
false but because the record has no form for them. The record can be
told about them later, in a different form, and then they may enter
as facts. Until then, they are outside the procession.

The shortness of the name refuses the temptation to demand more than
registration at the first step. The procession does not ask the fact
to be true. It does not ask the fact to be relevant. It does not ask
the fact to be admissible. Those demands belong to later names. The
shortness of \texttt{Fact} is what allows the procession to begin at
all. A more demanding first name would have no way to admit the events
the procession will then work on.

A seller posts a listing. A 2017 sedan, 82,441 miles, one previous
owner, asking eleven nine hundred. The listing sits on a public
website with a timestamp, a photograph, a phone number, and a
description. The buyer sees the listing and notes that a car is for
sale. The listing does not say the car is good. The listing does not
say the photograph matches the car. The listing does not say the
mileage is verified. The listing says that a claim about a car has
entered the public record. That entry is the fact. The buyer can
answer the claim, refuse the claim, or send a mechanic to test the
claim. None of those moves would be possible without the claim having
first been made. The fact is the move that opens the procession.

A buyer who treats the listing as a fact has not yet bought the car.
A buyer who treats the listing as a fact has not yet inspected the
car. A buyer who treats the listing as a fact has not even confirmed
the car exists. The buyer has acknowledged that a public claim has
been made and may now be acted on. The acknowledgement is small. It
is the necessary first move.

A receiving clerk stamps received on a package that has arrived at a
warehouse loading dock. The stamp does not explain the package's
contents. The stamp does not verify that the package matches the bill
of lading. The stamp does not confirm that the package is the package
the shipper said would arrive. The stamp says that a package has
arrived. A fact has entered the office. The package is now traceable
in the warehouse's records. The shipper can be invoiced. The inventory
system can be updated. The receiver can be notified. The stamp is
small. The bookkeeping it enables is large. Without the stamp, none of
the subsequent operations could happen. The stamp is the first fact
in a chain that may eventually produce a payment, a delivery
confirmation, a customer satisfaction survey, and a long-term business
relationship.

The receiving clerk's stamp and the buyer's acknowledgement of the
listing share a shape. Both are minimal. Both register that something
has happened. Both make subsequent operations possible. Neither
contains the substance of what has been claimed. Both are public: a
record exists; the record can be inspected.

A fact that cannot be told apart from another fact cannot do work. Two
listings posted on the same day, for two different cars, must be
distinguishable before either listing can support an inspection. Two
packages stamped received in the same minute, with the same
description, must be distinguishable before either can be invoiced.
Registration is not enough.

\section{\texttt{DISTINGUISHABLE}}
\label{se:distinguishable}

The first problem is not truth. It is difference.

Before a ledger can say what happened, it must be able to tell one
mark from another. Before a ruler can measure, it must have ticks that
can be counted, and the ticks must be distinguishable from one
another. Before a court can hear testimony, it must know which witness
is speaking, and the witnesses must be distinguishable from one
another. Before any further work can happen, the system needs a
witness that the thing in front of it is not the same as everything
else in the environment.

A reader might assume that difference is obvious. Things either are
different or they are not. The world supplies the differences and the
system reads them.

That assumption is wrong. Difference is not a property of the world
the system observes. Difference is a property of the system in contact
with the world. The same two objects may be distinguishable to one
observer and indistinguishable to another, depending on the observer's
carriers of distinction. Two cars with consecutive VINs, parked side
by side on a lot, are distinguishable by VIN, by production-line
position, by the small variations in their paint match, and by the
specific tire-tread patterns each acquired during shipping. They are
indistinguishable to a customer walking past at forty paces. The cars
have not changed. The observer's distinction apparatus has changed.
The first observer can act on the distinction. The second cannot.

Difference is not enough. Two cars on the lot may be very different
from one another, in size, color, condition, and price. If the buyer's
distinction apparatus does not capture those differences in a form
that the buyer can compare against the listing, the differences may as
well not exist for the purpose of the inspection. A buyer who reads
the listing as 2017 sedan and sees a 2017 sedan on the lot has not yet
distinguished the lot's 2017 sedan from any other 2017 sedan that
might have matched the listing. The distinction has to be carried by
something that connects the listing to the specific physical object.

\texttt{DISTINGUISHABLE}

A long word for a long obligation. The class does not say different.
It says capable of being distinguished. The focus is not on the object
alone. The focus is on the object under a process that can tell it
apart. The carrier of the distinction matters as much as the object.
Two objects can be different in fact and still fail to be
distinguishable inside a particular process. The class names the
combination.

A distinction must be reliable enough to repeat. A distinction that
holds today and breaks tomorrow has not satisfied the obligation. The
buyer must be able to return to the lot next week and identify the
same car as the one from the listing. If the only carrier of
distinction was a temporary chalk mark on the bumper that has since
been washed off, the distinction has lapsed.

A distinction must be transferable. The buyer must be able to
communicate the distinction to the mechanic, to the bank, to the
insurance agent, and to the DMV. A distinction that lives only in the
buyer's head is not distinguishing the car publicly. The buyer's
mental image of the car will not satisfy the bank's loan officer. The
bank needs a VIN, a year, a make, a model, and a trim. Each is a
carrier of distinction the bank can read.

A distinction must be inspectable by parties who were not present at
the original distinction. A title clerk at the DMV will eventually
need to verify that the car the buyer is registering is the car the
listing described. The clerk cannot rely on the buyer's testimony
alone. The clerk reads the VIN stamped on the dashboard, the VIN
stamped on the door jamb, the VIN on the title, and matches them. The
matches are the distinguishability the clerk can act on. The buyer's
distinguishing apparatus had to produce a record the clerk's
distinguishing apparatus can read.

The shortness of the name refuses the temptation to treat difference
as self-evident. \texttt{DISTINGUISHABLE} demands that the difference
be carried, transferable, and inspectable. A claim of difference that
lacks any of these has not satisfied the name.

The carrier of distinction in the inspection is multi-layered. The
VIN on the dashboard matches the VIN on the title document. The VIN
on the title document matches the VIN on the prior service records
held by the seller. The VIN on the service records matches the VIN on
the vehicle-history report the buyer purchased. The trim badge on the
car matches the trim description in the listing. The engine code,
visible under the hood and also encoded in the VIN's tenth character,
matches the listing's engine description. The key fobs that the
seller produces match the ignition. The current registered owner on
the title matches the seller's driver's license. None of those
matches is dramatic. Each is a small carrier of distinction. Together
they let the inspection point at one car and not another.

A buyer who fails to verify the VIN match may be inspecting a car
that is not the car the listing was for. The carrier of distinction
has not been established. The inspection that follows will be the
inspection of some car, but not necessarily the car the buyer was
trying to inspect. If any of the matches fails, the buyer does not
yet have a single car under inspection. The buyer has a candidate
cluster: a car on the lot that may or may not be the car the listing
described. The procession cannot continue until the cluster is
resolved to a single car.

A pharmacy distinguishes two patients with the same last name by
date of birth before filling a prescription. The name alone is not a
carrier of distinction. The pharmacy's distinguishing apparatus is
the name plus the date of birth plus the prescription record plus,
often, an additional verification like a phone number or an address.
A pharmacist who skips the combination has invited a specific kind
of failure: the wrong medicine in the right bottle, or the right
bottle going home with the wrong patient. The failure is not
theoretical. Wrong-patient medication errors kill people every year
in the United States.

The pharmacy's distinguishing apparatus is also multi-layered. The
patient's printed name on the prescription. The patient's verbal
confirmation of name when handed the bag at pickup. The date of
birth printed on the bag label, verified verbally. The patient's
photo ID, sometimes requested. The address on file, sometimes
confirmed. Each layer is a separate carrier of distinction. The
layers reinforce one another. If a layer disagrees, the pharmacist
stops the pickup until the conflict is resolved. The pharmacist has
been trained to treat carrier disagreement as a stop condition, not
a mild inconvenience.

A carrier of distinction can be forged. The VIN stamped on the
dashboard can be replaced. The date-of-birth on a stolen ID can be
the original holder's, not the impersonator's. The carriers of
distinction are not self-authenticating; they presuppose an
institutional context that makes forgery costly. The DMV's title
system, the VIN database the manufacturer maintains, the state's
driver's license issuance procedures: each is part of the
infrastructure that makes the carriers of distinction trustworthy.

Not everything distinguishable should be admitted. A distinct mark
is not yet an admissible mark.

\section{\texttt{ADMISSIBLE}}
\label{se:admissible}

Permission is not the same as difference.

Noise can be distinguished. Errors can be distinguished. Accidents
can be distinguished. A scratch on the wrong panel is distinguishable
from the panel itself. Distinguishability is a necessary precondition
for entering the record, but it is not sufficient. The world
produces distinguishable phenomena constantly. Most of them should
not enter any particular system's record. The record needs a gate.

An instrument needs a gate because the instrument's purpose depends
on what enters its record. A scale that admits the weight of the
air, the wind, and the operator's hand has not measured the object
on the scale. A telescope that admits the light of the ceiling lamp,
the photographer's breath, and the camera body's internal reflections
has not photographed the star. A court that admits every rumor,
hearsay, speculation, and personal opinion has not produced a
verdict; it has produced noise. The gate distinguishes the
instrument's record from the world's noise. The gate's discipline
makes the instrument's record inspectable.

A court needs rules of evidence because the verdict depends on the
record. Evidence law is the codification of admission rules: which
testimony may be heard, under what oath, with what authentication,
with what opportunity for cross-examination, with what limitations
on hearsay. The rules are not arbitrary. They are the result of
centuries of accumulated experience with what records produce
reliable verdicts and what records produce unreliable ones. The
rules are written down. The rules are enforceable. The rules can be
appealed when they are misapplied.

A ledger needs a standard for what may enter, and the standard must
be codified before the entries arrive. A ledger that admits entries
on a case-by-case basis, at the bookkeeper's discretion, is not a
ledger; it is a notebook. The codification of the admission standard
makes the ledger inspectable. The bookkeeper who follows the
codification produces a record the auditor can verify. The bookkeeper
who departs from the codification produces a record the auditor must
reconstruct piece by piece. The codification preceded the entries.

\texttt{ADMISSIBLE}

The class names the gate. The mark is not merely visible to the
inspector. It is allowed into the record under the rules of the
inspection.

The rules of admission must be public. The buyer must know, before
the inspection begins, what kinds of evidence the inspection will
treat as admissible. A buyer who learns mid-inspection that a
category of evidence has been excluded has been deceived about the
inspection's scope. The codification has to be available to the
parties who will be bound by it.

The rules of admission must be enforceable. The codification has to
have consequences. An evidence rule that the court refuses to enforce
when violated is not really an evidence rule. An inspection standard
that the inspector waives when the seller objects is not really a
standard. The rule's force is in its enforcement.

The rules of admission must be revisable through a disciplined
process. A rule that cannot be changed is dead. The rules of evidence
in a courtroom have been revised many times, in response to social
changes, technological developments, and empirical studies of what
makes verdicts reliable. The revisions are not arbitrary; they go
through legislative or judicial processes that themselves have
admission rules. The recursion is real, and the class name
presupposes that the recursion has been worked out somewhere.

The shortness of the name refuses the temptation to admit what
should not be admitted, because the speaker wants the result the
admission would produce. The discipline is that the speaker does not
get to decide admission in the moment. The codification preceded the
moment. The speaker is bound to the codification.

A service invoice with shop name, date, VIN, and mileage is admissible
to the maintenance history. The same words spoken by the seller about
a service the cousin remembers performing are not admissible to the
same record. The first mark crosses a gate the buyer trusts; the
second does not. The buyer is not calling the cousin a liar. The
second mark cannot enter the record at the same level as the first.
If the buyer accepts the cousin's word as equivalent to the invoice,
the record loses the discipline that lets later comparisons mean
anything. A car with five admissible service invoices over its
lifetime can be compared against a car with two. A car with five
service invoices and three undocumented recalled events can be
compared against a car with two service invoices and four undocumented
recalled events only if the undocumented events are tracked in a
separate column with a different admission status.

The admission rule for the maintenance history is: a service event
enters the record only if it is documented by a shop with a name, on
a date that can be verified, against the specific VIN, with the
mileage at the time of service recorded. The rule is short. The rule
is publicly stated, in the inspection's setup. The rule is enforced
consistently: the buyer does not waive it for events the seller
insists on, and the buyer does not impose stricter requirements on
events the seller cannot produce. The rule preceded the inspection.
The rule binds the buyer as well as the seller.

The seller may protest. The seller may say that the cousin really
did perform the service, that the cousin is a trusted family
mechanic, that the cousin's word should be worth at least as much
as a shop invoice. The buyer's response is not that the cousin is
lying. The buyer's response is that the inspection record uses a
specific admission rule, that the rule applies to all events equally,
and that the cousin's testimony does not satisfy the rule. The buyer
can still note the cousin's testimony in a separate column called
seller assertions, but that column is not the maintenance history.
The two columns sit under different obligations. The inspection's
later moves will treat them differently.

A tournament accepts scores submitted before the deadline on the
official card. A number texted after the round, even if honest, does
not enter the same record. The tournament is not punishing honesty.
The tournament is preserving the rule that lets the leaderboard be
inspected. Every player at the tournament agreed, at registration,
to submit scores on the official card before the deadline. The
codification preceded the round. The player who submits late has not
followed the rule. The leaderboard cannot admit the late score
without breaking the rule for every player who followed it. The
tournament director's enforcement of the rule is not capricious; it
is the only move that preserves the leaderboard's meaning.

A handshake claim about prior maintenance is not admissible to the
maintenance history. No invoice, no entry in the history column. A
buyer who allows the handshake to count as maintenance history has
weakened the inspection's ability to distinguish well-maintained
cars from poorly-maintained ones. The weakening is not visible at the
moment of the handshake. It surfaces later, when the buyer tries to
compare this car against the comparable set and finds that the
maintenance column is no longer measuring what it was supposed to
measure. The cousin's word may have been honest. The honesty does
not help the inspection if the admission rule has been broken.
Breaking the rule for one event has broken the discipline for all
events.

A screenshot of a forum post saying this model never has transmission
problems is not admissible to the mechanical assessment of this
specific car. The forum post may contain accumulated experience. The
forum post is not a measurement of this transmission. A buyer who
admits the forum post to the mechanical assessment has confused
generalization with measurement. The forum post may enter a separate
column called prior expectations, where it can inform the buyer's
investigation strategy. It does not enter the mechanical assessment
column, where it would compete with the actual measurement of this
transmission's behavior.

A seller's verbal confidence about the car's condition is not
admissible to the inspection's findings. The seller may be confident.
The seller's confidence is not a finding. The findings are what the
inspection's instruments produced under the inspection's procedures.
The seller's confidence may be noted in a separate column called
seller representations, where it can be cross-referenced against the
findings to assess the seller's reliability for future interactions.
It does not enter the findings column.

Once admitted, the mark can be counted.

\section{\texttt{COUNTABLE}}
\label{se:countable}

A mark that has entered the ledger must be able to take a place.

The pressure comes from the structure of records. A record that admits
marks but cannot place them in order is a heap, not a record. A heap
of distinguishable, admissible marks may preserve each mark
individually, but it cannot answer questions about the marks as a
collection. Did this mark come before that one? Is this mark the
third in a sequence or the seventeenth? Has this mark already been
counted, or is this its first count? The answers depend on the marks
having places in an indexed sequence.

Counting precedes measurement, but it prepares for it. The system
cannot ask how many until it can ask which one. A buyer who wants to
compare this car's prior owner count to the comparable set cannot
perform the comparison until each car's owners have been placed in a
sequence with indices. The first owner, the second owner, the third
owner. Each owner has a place. The number of owners is a count over
that sequence. The number cannot exist without the sequence.

\texttt{COUNTABLE}

The class does not yet mean measured. It does not say how many or
how much. It says the mark can be placed into a sequence where next
and previous make sense. The mark has an index. The index lets the
system ask which mark and is this mark the same one I saw earlier.
Without the index, the admitted marks pile up but do not yet permit
comparison or ordering.

The sequence must be public. A private sequence the buyer holds in
mind does not satisfy the obligation; the seller, the bank, and the
DMV will all need to read the sequence too. The sequence must be
stable: a mark's place in the sequence should not change without a
recorded event that moved it. The sequence must be inspectable: a
reviewer should be able to read the sequence and verify that the
marks are in their stated positions.

The shortness of the name refuses the temptation to skip ordering. A
record that wants to jump from admission to measurement without
establishing sequence has not yet done the ordering work.

The car's title carries a history of owners. The first owner, the
second owner, the third owner. Each owner is a separate event in the
title's history. Each owner has a position: the first to take
ownership after the manufacturer, the second after the first sold,
the third after the second sold, and so on. The position is not yet
a quality judgment about the owners. The position is the index that
lets the buyer ask whether the car has been handed off many times or
few. The buyer needs the count to be in a sequence before the count
can mean anything. Two owners is a fact only because there was a
first and a second.

The sequence is public: the title document records the ownership
history with names, dates, and odometer readings at the time of
transfer. The sequence is stable: an owner's position cannot be
changed without a new title transaction recorded by the state. The
sequence is inspectable: a buyer or a lender or an insurer can pull
the title history and verify the sequence.

The title sequence may have gaps. A car that was registered in one
state, then moved to another, then returned to the first, may have
ownership records distributed across multiple state registries. The
complete sequence requires combining the records. The combining is
itself a disciplined operation: a buyer who only checks the current
state's records may miss events that happened during the
out-of-state period. The vehicle-history reporting services exist in
part to handle this combining for buyers who would otherwise miss
it.

Numbered tickets at a bakery do not bake the bread. They make public
order possible. Ticket forty-seven follows ticket forty-six and
precedes ticket forty-eight. The ticket numbers do not measure
anything. They do not weigh the bread, time the customer's wait, or
assess the bread's quality. They establish that there is an order
in which service may proceed. The order is public: every customer
can see their ticket number and the current served number. The
order is stable: a ticket number does not change because the
customer is impatient. The order is inspectable: a customer who
believes the order has been broken can ask the bakery to verify
which ticket was issued at which time.

The bakery's ticketing system has another property worth naming:
the order is forced. Customer forty-six is served before customer
forty-seven. The forcing makes the system fair. Without the forcing,
the bakery's order would be subject to whichever customer was
loudest, most insistent, or most familiar with the staff. The
ticket numbers remove that decision from the moment. The decision
was made by the order of arrival. The bakery only has to follow the
order.

The bibliography of a research paper carries the same shape. The
references are numbered. The numbering does not evaluate the
references; it gives them places that can be cited in the body.
Reference 12 is what the body's parenthetical citation points to.
The reference's place is what makes the citation work. A paper
without numbered references can be read, but its citations cannot
point with the same precision. The numbering is small. It enables
the entire citation apparatus.

A count needs a carried form.

\section{\texttt{ENCODED}}
\label{se:encoded}

Counting has to survive transport.

A count exists somewhere. It may exist in the inspector's head as a
remembered number. It may exist on a clipboard as a tally mark. It
may exist in a spreadsheet as a cell value. It may exist in a
database as a row. It may exist in Lean as an inductive constructor.
Each is a way of carrying the count across a medium. The count is
not the medium. The count is what the medium carries.

The pressure for encoding comes from the structure of records that
need to last. A count in the inspector's head will be forgotten. A
count on a chalkboard will be erased. A count on a sticky note will
be lost. A count on a properly archived document will survive the
inspector, the chalkboard, and the sticky note. The survival depends
on the form. The form is the encoding.

The encoding makes the count shareable. An inspector who has counted
the car's prior owners cannot share the count with the bank by
transferring the count from the inspector's head to the bank's loan
officer's head. The inspector has to encode the count into a form
the bank can read. The form may be a sentence in an inspection
report. The form may be a row in a database the bank has access to.
The form may be a number on a printed certificate. Each is an
encoding. Each makes the count shareable in a way the head-only
count was not.

\texttt{ENCODED}

The class names the entry of the count into a form that can move.
The form may be physical, electronic, or symbolic. The specific
medium does not matter for the class. What matters is that the count
has been pressed into a form that can be transported, copied,
archived, and re-read.

The encoding must preserve the count. An encoding that loses
information has not satisfied the obligation. A count of eight that
becomes three in the encoding has been corrupted by the encoding.
The encoding's reliability is part of the obligation. Encodings have
failure modes that include translation errors, transcription errors,
and representation errors. The obligation is to use encodings whose
failure modes are known and controlled.

The encoding must be readable by the next reader. An encoding that
only the encoder can read is not yet a public encoding. A buyer who
writes the inspection findings in a private shorthand has encoded
the findings, but the encoding is not shareable until the shorthand
is translated into a form the next reader can decode.

The shortness of the name refuses the temptation to skip the
encoding step and rely on speech or memory. The class demands a
durable form. The demand may be satisfied by writing, printing,
photographing, signing, sealing, or any other process that produces
a record outside the encoder's mind.

The car's condition enters several forms during the inspection. The
OBD-II scanner reads trouble codes as numeric tokens like P0420,
which the manufacturer's database translates as catalyst-system
efficiency below threshold, bank 1. The inspection sheet records
checkboxes and brief notes: brakes worn, replace within 5,000 miles.
The tire-tread depths become numeric millimeters: 6 mm front, 4 mm
rear. The compression test produces pressures in PSI: 170, 168, 175,
169. The photos enter a folder with timestamps. The mechanic
dictates findings to a voice-recognition app that produces written
text. The price-comparison spreadsheet records the listing's asking
price alongside the comparable median and the adjusted estimate.
Each form is an encoding. None of them is the car. The folder of
encodings is what the buyer will carry away from the inspection.

The encodings have specific failure modes. The OBD-II code P0420 is
a standard code; it can be looked up. The OBD-II code P00BC is a
less common code; some scanners and lookup tools will not recognize
it, and the inspection report will need to include a description as
well as the code. The tire-tread millimeter readings are encoded
against a known standard, the minimum legal tread and the wear-
indicator depth; a reader who does not know the standard reads the
numbers without context. The inspection sheet's checkboxes are
encoded against the inspection's own categories; a reader from a
different shop may not recognize the categories, and the report will
need to include category descriptions or be ignored.

The encoding's failure modes are part of the encoding. An inspection
that ignores them produces an encoding that may not survive
transport to the next reader. An inspection that accounts for them
produces an encoding that can be read by a range of subsequent
readers.

A music score encodes rhythm and pitch in a form a performance can
survive. The score is not the music. The score is the form the music
has been forced into so that the music can be reproduced by
performers who were not in the room when the score was written. A
score that uses standard notation can be read by classically trained
musicians anywhere. A score that uses idiosyncratic notation may
not survive transport. A composer who invents a new symbol must
either provide a legend, train the performers, or accept that the
notation will be misread.

The score's encoding has known failure modes. Tempo markings are
imprecise: allegro means fast, but how fast depends on the
conductor. Dynamics are relative: piano means soft, but how soft
depends on the instrument and the hall. Articulation marks are
conventional: a slur means legato, but the specific shaping depends
on the performer's training. The score does not eliminate
interpretation. The score encodes enough that the interpretation is
bounded. A performer who plays a written quarter note as a whole
note has departed from the encoding. A performer who plays the
quarter note slightly faster or slower than another performer has
remained within the encoding's tolerance.

A survey marker is a physical object placed in the ground to encode
a geographic coordinate. The marker carries the coordinate forward
in time and space: a future surveyor can find the marker, identify
it from its inscribed code, and use it as a reference for new
measurements. The encoding is durable. It survives weather, land
changes, and the surveyor's death. The encoding's discipline lives
in the institutional framework that maintains it: the survey
records that list which marker is where, the calibration processes
that verify a marker's coordinate has not shifted, the property law
that prohibits unauthorized removal of a marker. The marker alone is
not the encoding. The marker plus the institutional framework is.

An encoding can be lossy by design. The inspection's tire-tread
reading rounds to the nearest millimeter. The actual tread is
somewhere within a half-millimeter band around the recorded value.
The rounding is chosen because the next reader, the buyer comparing
against the safety threshold, cares only about the millimeter-level
distinction. Sub-millimeter precision would be more accurate and
less useful. The lossy encoding is a deliberate choice. The lossiness
is part of the encoding's design. The class does not refuse lossy
encodings; it refuses encodings whose losses are unstated.

Every encoding leaves a structured leftover.

\begin{movement}{From Entry to Residue}

The mark has crossed the gate. It has been distinguished, admitted,
given a place in a sequence, and pressed into forms that can travel.
The procession has produced its first usable artifact: a record entry
that any subsequent reader can identify, can verify entered under
rule, can locate in a sequence, and can decode.

The first movement has not grappled with what the record cannot fully
contain. Every distinction leaves distinctions it did not make. Every
admission rule leaves events that did not pass the rule. Every count
is a count of something; the unit being counted leaves other possible
units uncounted. Every encoding leaves out what the encoding's form
does not carry. These leftovers are not failures. They are the shape
of what the record has done by being specific.

The second movement walks the discipline of the leftover. A leftover
that has been ignored returns later as a surprise; a leftover that
has been disciplined remains in the record as a known limitation.
The class names of the second movement teach the reader to recognize,
hold, compare, and measure the leftover without prematurely closing
it.

The second movement is the longest. The discipline of the leftover
requires the most names.

\end{movement}

\section{\texttt{RESIDUE}}
\label{se:residue}

Not everything disappears into the form that carries it.

The remainder after a division. The unread part of a signal. The
unexplained error after fitting a model. The obligation still open
after a proof step. The leftover that does not match the recipe. The
exception that does not fit the rule. None of these is a mere
failure. Each is a structured leftover the record has produced as a
consequence of doing the work it has done. A discipline that ignores
the leftover loses information that was actually in the record.

Residues are easy to dismiss. A buyer who finishes the inspection
with one item that does not fit can be tempted to ignore the item,
finish the report, and proceed. The temptation is understandable.
The leftover often looks small. It is not always small. A leftover
that does not fit may indicate that the inspection's categories were
incomplete, that a separate process is running alongside the one
being inspected, or that a previously unknown event has happened to
the car. The leftover is informative not because it is the answer
but because it is a structured demand for further investigation.

Residues compose. The first inspection step may leave one leftover.
The second step may leave another. The third step may leave a third.
None of the leftovers may be alarming individually. The pattern of
leftovers across steps may be highly informative. The car that has a
small leftover on every inspection step is a different kind of car
from the one that has no leftovers. The pattern is what makes the
residue more than the sum of its parts.

\texttt{RESIDUE}

The class names what remains and still matters.

A residue must be recorded in a form that preserves its specificity.
A vague note saying something else was off has not preserved the
residue's structure. A specific note saying the steering wheel
returned to center slowly after a left turn from a stop on dry
pavement has preserved enough structure that a mechanic can
investigate. The residue is not a category; it is the specific
phenomenon that did not fit.

A residue must be carried, not resolved prematurely. A buyer who
hears the steering complaint and decides on the spot that the
problem is alignment has resolved the residue without inspection.
The resolution may be right. The resolution may also be wrong. The
discipline is to carry the residue as a structured note until the
relevant investigation has been performed. Premature resolution is
the leftover's most common failure mode.

Residues must be revisited. A residue noted in one inspection step
may turn out to be relevant to a different inspection step. The
brake-feel oddness may be related to the steering oddness through a
common cause: a failing front-end component. The investigation that
combines the residues will not happen unless both residues are
preserved in inspectable form.

The inspection has produced several residues. The scan returns
clean, with no stored codes and no pending codes. The test drive
nonetheless includes a vibration through the steering wheel under
braking at highway speeds. The title is clear, but one service
interval is missing from the maintenance log: the 30,000-mile
service does not appear, and the 60,000-mile service makes reference
to work that should have been done at 30,000. The paint meter reads
consistently across the body except for one door, where the reading
spikes to twice the surrounding panels' values.

Each residue is structured. The brake vibration has specific
conditions: highway speed, brake application, returns to normal when
speed drops. The missing service interval has a specific inferential
structure: the 60,000-mile work presupposes the 30,000-mile work;
the absence of the earlier record means either the work was not done
or the documentation was lost. The paint reading has a specific
spatial structure: one panel, twice the baseline, no obvious
external damage to the panel.

A buyer who throws these away has lost the only information that
was actually new. The scan was clean: the buyer learned that the
car's onboard computer is not currently storing any trouble codes.
That is good information, but it does not distinguish this car from
any other car with a clean scan. The brake vibration, the missing
service interval, and the paint reading are specific to this car.
They are the data points that let the buyer distinguish this car
from the cars in the comparable set. A buyer who ignores them has
effectively bought based on the same information as a buyer who
never inspected.

The buyer's discipline is to record each residue, classify it for
further investigation, and decide whether the further investigation
can happen now (a second test drive with a focus on the brake
vibration), later (a request to the seller for the missing service
record), or as a condition of purchase (a paint-thickness
re-inspection at a body shop). The residues do not all need to be
resolved at the moment of inspection. They need to be carried into
the next phase of the buyer's process.

A bank reconciliation balances except for one outstanding check. The
check is not noise. It is structured residue. Someone wrote a check
that has not yet cleared. The reconciliation cannot close until the
check is accounted for. The bookkeeper does not erase the check
from the records or ignore it. The bookkeeper carries the check as
an outstanding item in the reconciliation, with the check number,
the date issued, the payee, and the amount. The check may clear in
next week's reconciliation. The check may need to be reissued if
the payee reports it lost. The check may be voided if it remains
outstanding too long. Each is a follow-up process. The follow-up is
possible only because the residue has been preserved with enough
specificity to act on.

The bank's discipline has been codified over centuries. The
accounting profession's standards say what to do with outstanding
items, how long to carry them before flagging them for additional
investigation, and what disclosures the reconciliation owes to the
auditors. The codification carries the residue forward without
treating it as either resolved or ignored.

A residue may turn out to be noise. The brake vibration may be a
stone temporarily lodged in the rotor's cooling vanes. The missing
service record may be in a different file the seller forgot about.
The paint reading spike may be a calibration error in the
inspector's meter. The discipline of carrying the residue does not
say the residue is real evidence of a problem. It says the residue
should be carried until it has been resolved by an inspection, not
by an assumption. The resolution that the residue is noise is
itself an inspection finding; it has to be earned, not asserted.

Records must be compared.

\section{\texttt{BINARY}}
\label{se:binary}

Comparison begins with a small question.

Is this less than or equal to that? Does this pass the threshold? Is
this inside the permitted range? Is the check-engine light on or off?
Is the title clean or branded? Is the price above or below the
comparable median? The public interface of many decisions is a
yes-or-no gate. The gate does not capture the underlying detail. It
captures the operationally relevant face of the comparison.

Public decisions need to be actionable. A decision rule that produces
a continuous spectrum of partial verdicts is hard to act on. A
decision rule that produces a yes or a no is easy to act on. The
buyer cannot half-buy the car. The buyer buys the car, walks away,
or asks for a price adjustment. Each action corresponds to a
discrete branch in the decision tree. The branches are reachable
only by binary gates that have been crossed in a specific order.

The underlying state may be continuous, but the comparison interface
is often categorical. A brake pad's thickness is a continuous
quantity. The decision rule based on the brake pad is categorical:
replace before the next inspection, or not. The continuous
measurement, the millimeter reading, feeds into a binary comparison
above or below the replacement threshold. The categorical face is
what the buyer acts on. The underlying continuous quantity is
preserved in the record for later use, but the action is binary.

\texttt{BINARY}

The class names the comparison interface. The class does not say
the underlying state is binary. It says the comparison interface is.

The threshold the binary gate uses must be codified before the
comparison. A gate whose threshold is chosen after the measurement
is not a binary gate; it is a post-hoc justification. A buyer who
decides at the moment of inspection that this car's brake pads are
below the threshold has made a decision; a buyer who decides next
year, looking back at the record, that the threshold should have
been different has changed the rule.

The binary gate's threshold can be wrong. The shop's safety threshold
for brake-pad thickness may not be the right threshold for a buyer
planning to drive in mountainous terrain. The threshold was codified
for a generic use. The specific use may need a different threshold.
The class does not claim the threshold is universally correct. It
claims the threshold is publicly stated and enforced. If the buyer
disagrees with the threshold, the buyer can choose a different
threshold, but the buyer must do so explicitly and apply the new
threshold consistently.

Binary gates compose into trees. The car passes if every binary gate
passes. The car fails if any binary gate fails. The car is
conditional if some gates pass and others produce a finding that
can be resolved with a price adjustment. The composition rule is
itself a codification. The buyer's inspection process has a specific
rule for combining the individual binary gates into an overall
decision.

The shortness of the name refuses the temptation to finesse the
binary face. A car that almost passes the brake-pad gate has not
passed. The gate's discipline is in the gate, not in the inspector's
willingness to make exceptions.

The brake pad thickness is above or below the shop's safety
threshold. The title is clean or branded. The asking price is inside
or outside the band of comparable sales. The check-engine light is
on or off. The pending OBD code is within the manufacturer's
acceptable range or it has progressed to a stored code. Each is a
binary face on a richer underlying state. The inspection report uses
the binary faces because the buyer's next action is binary: buy or
not, renegotiate or not, walk away or not.

The thresholds are codified. The shop's brake-pad threshold is three
millimeters: at or below three, replace. The title brand status is
codified by the state's titling agency: a clean title is one with no
salvage, flood, junk, or rebuilt notation. The comparable price band
is calculated from the comparison set's 25th to 75th percentile:
prices within the band are considered market-typical, prices outside
the band require explanation. The buyer did not invent these
thresholds. The buyer inherited them from the shop's procedures, the
state's title law, and the comparison service's documented
methodology.

A door sensor reports open or closed. The building automation system
can act on the report because the state has been reduced to a face
the automation can compare. The sensor is not the door. The door has
a continuous range of positions between fully open and fully closed.
The sensor's binary face collapses the range into two categories. The
collapse is intentional: the automation needs to know whether to let
the air conditioning turn on, the door is closed and the room can be
cooled efficiently, or to delay the air conditioning, the door is
open and cooling would waste energy. The underlying continuous state
is preserved by the door itself; the binary state is what the system
needs to act on.

A regulatory environmental sensor reports concentration above or
below a permitted limit. The measured concentration is a continuous
quantity, parts per million of some pollutant. The reportable state
is binary, above the limit requiring action, below the limit no
action required. The threshold has been codified by the regulatory
agency. The sensor's binary face triggers the regulatory response: a
violation report, an enforcement action, a fine. The continuous data
are preserved for analysis and trend monitoring, but the regulatory
response is keyed to the binary gate.

Binary gates can be gamed. A measurement that falls just above the
threshold can be re-taken, in slightly different conditions, until
it falls below. A buyer who re-tests the brake pads until the reading
comes in higher than the initial test has gamed the gate. The shop's
discipline is to record all readings, not just the final reading,
and to apply a fixed procedure for which reading counts. The gate's
discipline depends on the procedure being followed.

A comparison that changes every time is not yet an instrument.

\section{\texttt{REPEATABLE}}
\label{se:repeatable}

One good reading is not enough.

If the same conditions do not yield the same comparison, the
instrument has not earned trust. Repeatability is not truth. A
repeatable wrong answer is still wrong. But without repeatability,
no answer has a place to stand. The same conditions producing the
same result is the floor under which a reading cannot be treated as
a reading.

A single reading from a broken instrument may happen to be correct.
The reader has no way to tell, from the single reading alone,
whether the reading came from an instrument that works or an
instrument that happened to produce the right number once. The
reader's confidence in the reading comes from the reading's
reproduction under controlled conditions. Two readings agreeing is
more informative than one reading by itself. Three readings agreeing
is more informative than two. At some level of agreement, the
instrument has demonstrated that the reading is not the product of a
random fluctuation.

The conditions of reproduction must be specifiable. A reading
reproduced under different conditions has not been reproduced; it
has been replaced. The instrument's discipline is to identify the
conditions under which a reading should be the same. Without that
specification, the reader has no way to know whether two readings
should agree. The specification is part of the instrument's
calibration. The calibration is part of the instrument.

Repeatability and accuracy are different. A ruler can be repeatable
and inaccurate: every measurement gives the same wrong answer. A
ruler can be accurate and unrepeatable: some measurements are right
and some are wrong. The class focuses on repeatability, not
accuracy. Accuracy depends on comparison against an external
reference, which the inspection has not yet established.
Repeatability can be tested by the inspection alone. The inspection
can take the same reading twice and compare.

\texttt{REPEATABLE}

The class names the moral discipline of the instrument. The word
moral is not casual. An instrument that produces different readings
under the same conditions is not telling the inspector the truth.
The discipline is to refuse to use such an instrument. The instrument
has not earned the right to be trusted. A buyer who proceeds anyway
has accepted untrustworthy data into the inspection record.

The conditions of repetition must be controllable. A reading whose
conditions cannot be reproduced is not testable for repeatability. A
test drive in light rain may not be reproducible if the next
available test drive is in dry conditions. The buyer must either find
conditions that can be reproduced or accept that the reading will
not be tested for repeatability and adjust the inspection's
confidence accordingly.

The repetition must be independent of the first reading. A second
reading taken because the inspector remembers the first reading and
unconsciously adjusts the instrument is not an independent test. An
inspector who looks at the first brake-pad reading and then
re-measures, knowing the first reading, may unconsciously place the
calipers in a way that produces a similar number. The independence
is what makes the comparison meaningful. The shop's procedure may
include blinding, the second reading taken by a different inspector
who has not seen the first, or randomization, the readings taken in
scrambled order across the car so the inspector cannot remember
which is which.

Repetition does not require infinite trials. Two agreeing readings
are not as informative as three; three are not as informative as
ten; ten are not as informative as a hundred. At some level, the
marginal informativeness of additional readings drops below the
marginal cost. The inspection's procedure specifies how many
readings to take for each measurement type. The number was chosen
to balance reliability against cost.

The shortness of the name refuses the single-reading inspection. A
car evaluated by one battery test, one brake-pressure check, one
OBD scan, and one test drive has not been inspected to the standard
\texttt{REPEATABLE} demands. A buyer who accepts a single-reading
inspection has accepted an inspection whose findings have not been
tested for repeatability.

The mechanic reads the battery's voltage under load twice. The first
reading is 11.8 volts under starter cranking, which is at the low
end of the acceptable range. The second reading, after the battery
has rested for two minutes, is 12.2 volts at rest. The two readings
together produce a battery-health picture: the battery has
acceptable resting voltage but marginal performance under load. A
single reading would not have told the inspector this. The mechanic
also checks tire tread at multiple points around each tire: inside,
center, and outside, near the front of the wheel and near the rear
of the wheel. The multiple readings reveal whether the wear is even,
suggesting normal use, or uneven, suggesting alignment, suspension,
or pressure issues. The mechanic repeats the road-brake test on the
same route, at the same approximate speed, with the same brake-pedal
pressure, to verify that the vibration noted in the first run also
appears in the second run. The repetition reveals that the vibration
is reproducible, which makes it a finding rather than a one-time
anomaly.

If the result wanders from reading to reading without an explanation
the inspection accepts, the inspection cannot use the reading. The
wandering is the instrument failing to be repeatable on this car
under these conditions. The mechanic's discipline is to either find
the cause of the wandering, a different battery state of charge, a
different tire pressure, a different brake-pedal pressure, or to
report the wandering as a finding. Wandering with no explanation is
itself a finding.

A tailor measures the same inseam twice before cutting cloth. The
second reading is not ceremony. It protects the cut. The cloth costs
money. The tailor's reputation costs more. The second reading exists
because the first one might have moved. The tailor's tape might have
slipped. The customer might have shifted weight. The tailor might
have read the number from the wrong side of the tape. The second
reading catches these errors before the cloth is cut. After the
cloth is cut, the error is permanent. Before the cloth is cut, the
error costs only the seconds it takes to measure again.

The tailor's discipline has been codified by generations of custom
tailoring. Apprentices are taught to measure twice. The discipline
is institutional, not personal. A new apprentice who measures once
and cuts the cloth has departed from the trade's discipline. The
master tailor would correct the apprentice.

A scientific replication carries the same shape. A finding from one
experiment is not yet a finding the field will treat as established.
The finding has to be replicated by a second laboratory, ideally
with different personnel, similar equipment, and the same procedure.
The replication establishes that the finding is not the product of
one laboratory's specific quirks. Failed replication is not a
peripheral issue; it has produced the replication crisis in several
fields. The fields' response to the crisis is itself a codification
update: better preregistration, larger sample sizes, more
transparent reporting. The codification responds to the failures.

Some readings cannot be repeated. A destructive test, such as
cutting open the catalytic converter to inspect for melted
substrate, cannot be repeated on the same converter. The
inspection's discipline is to either accept non-repeatable tests as
a different category of evidence or to substitute a non-destructive
proxy. Non-destructive proxies, temperature differential across the
converter, oxygen-sensor readings, exhaust composition, are
themselves repeatable. The proxy's discipline carries the
repeatability obligation that the destructive test could not.

A repeatable comparison can become a number.

\section{\texttt{NUMERIC}}
\label{se:numeric}

A number is a carried comparison, not magic.

A number does not arrive without an instrument. A number does not
arrive without a unit. A number does not arrive without a comparison
that has happened repeatably under the unit. The public habit of
treating numbers as self-evident has obscured the apparatus
underneath. A buyer who hears that the tire tread is four millimeters
has actually received a compressed report on a repeatable measurement
against a standardized ruler. The compression is what makes the
number portable. The apparatus is what makes the number reliable.

Numbers travel without their context. A spreadsheet of inspection
findings contains numbers. The spreadsheet does not contain the
procedures that produced the numbers. The instruments, the
conditions, the repeatability counts, the codified thresholds: all
are presupposed by the numbers. A reader who treats the numbers as
self-contained has lost access to what the numbers depend on. A
buyer who asks only what the numbers say has not yet asked what the
numbers were produced by.

Numbers compose, and the composition has to be consistent. Adding
the tire-tread millimeter to the brake-pad millimeter produces a
number whose unit is millimeter but whose meaning is incoherent. The
composition rules constrain what operations are admissible on which
numbers. Numbers that look the same can be incommensurable.

\texttt{NUMERIC}

The class names the moment when the repeated comparison enters the
record as a value the record can use. The emphasis is on can use. A
repeated comparison that has not entered the record in a numeric
form is information the inspector holds; once it has entered
numerically, it is information the record holds.

The number must carry its unit. A number without a unit is not yet a
number in the procession's sense. Tire tread 4 means nothing; tire
tread 4 mm has meaning under the codified unit. The unit's absence
is a defect.

The number must carry its precision. A tread depth of 4 mm and a
tread depth of 4.2 mm are different findings. The first rounds to
the nearest millimeter; the second carries a tenth-millimeter
precision. The number's precision is part of the number.

The number must carry its uncertainty. A tread depth of 4 mm has
implicit uncertainty: the ruler's resolution, the placement
variability. A more careful report would say 4 mm plus or minus 0.5
mm. The uncertainty is part of the number's meaning. A buyer
comparing 4 mm to a 3 mm threshold needs to know whether the 4 mm
reading might actually be 3.5 mm.

The shortness of the name refuses the bare-number report. A report
that lists numbers without units, without precisions, and without
uncertainties has not produced \texttt{NUMERIC} entries. It has
produced number-like artifacts that the next reader cannot use
safely.

The inspection report lists specific numeric findings. Front brake
pads are four millimeters thick, plus or minus half a millimeter,
measured at three points on each pad and averaged. Battery health is
71 percent of nominal, measured under the shop's standardized load
test at the manufacturer's specified ambient temperature. Engine
compression is 178 PSI for cylinder one, 175 for cylinder two, 176
for cylinder three, and 174 for cylinder four, measured with the
same gauge, at operating temperature, with the throttle wide open.
The asking price is 11,900 US dollars, the comparable median is
10,800 US dollars, and the 80th percentile of the comparable set is
11,400 US dollars. Each number is a carried comparison.

The numbers in this list are useful only because they are
\texttt{NUMERIC} entries, not bare numbers. The four-millimeter
brake-pad reading is useful because the buyer knows it was measured
against the shop's three-millimeter replacement threshold; the buyer
knows the measurement precision; the buyer knows the measurement
protocol. The 71 percent battery-health reading is useful because
the buyer knows the test conditions; the buyer knows the threshold
below which the shop recommends replacement; the buyer knows the
manufacturer's nominal voltage. Each number carries the metadata
that makes it actionable.

A buyer who reads the report and acts only on the bare numbers,
replace the brake pads because four millimeters sounds low, has
missed the metadata. A buyer who acts only on the inspector's
verbal summary, the brakes are OK, has missed the numbers. The
class preserves both: the numbers and the metadata that makes the
numbers actionable.

A speedometer's displayed value is not speed itself. It is a numeric
output produced by a calibrated comparison of pulse count to time
interval. The pulses come from a sensor on the transmission output
shaft or on a wheel. The time interval comes from the car's clock.
The conversion from pulses-per-time to miles-per-hour requires a
calibration that includes the transmission ratio, the differential
ratio, and the tire's rolling diameter. The driver who reads the
speedometer's display does not see any of this apparatus. The driver
sees the value. The value is honest only because the comparison
happened and the calibration was correct.

A speedometer with an incorrect calibration, after a tire change
that altered the rolling diameter, for example, will display a value
that looks like a speed but is not one. The driver acting on the
displayed value will be wrong about how fast the car is moving. The
numeric display has not produced a true \texttt{NUMERIC} entry; it
has produced a number-shaped error.

A fever thermometer reads 101.4 degrees Fahrenheit. The number is
the patient's body temperature only if the thermometer has been
calibrated to medical standards, placed correctly, and held long
enough to equilibrate. The thermometer's reading is otherwise just
a number on a display. The medical record's entry of the value
presupposes the calibration, the placement, and the equilibration.
The pediatrician who reads the recorded value acts on the
presumption that those preconditions were met. If the parent
recorded the value from a poorly-calibrated thermometer held
briefly under the armpit, the recorded value is not actually a
body-temperature reading.

Numeric findings can be too precise. An inspector who records the
tire tread as 4.2837 millimeters has reported a precision the
measurement instrument cannot support. The number is not honest.
The instrument's true precision was the nearest tenth of a
millimeter, not the nearest ten-thousandth. The over-precise number
invites the reader to make distinctions the instrument cannot
warrant.

A number must stand for something unresolved or real.

\section{\texttt{REPRESENTABLE}}
\label{se:representable}

The world contains things not yet closed.

A lab result is pending. A contract leaves a blank. A compiler
carries a metavariable. A detector leaves a gap between events. A
diagnostic test returns a finding that could be any of several
specific causes. A weather forecast assigns probabilities to outcomes
without committing to one. These are not failures of the record.
They are admissible unresolved states. The record must be able to
carry them without prematurely resolving them.

Records are tempted to close. A spreadsheet wants every cell filled.
A form wants every blank completed. An inspection wants every
finding to be a yes or no. The unresolved entry is uncomfortable.
The temptation is to push the unresolved into a resolved-looking
shape: pick the most likely cause and write it in, even though the
actual finding was a set of possible causes. The push falsifies the
record.

Unresolved states have structure. They are not equivalent to blanks.
A pending lab result is different from a result that was never
requested. A possible-cause set of three specific candidates is
different from a vague we don't know. The structure of the
unresolved state is what the record must preserve.

\texttt{REPRESENTABLE}

The class says the unresolved thing can still be carried. The class
names the obligation that the unresolved have a place in the record
that captures its specific shape.

The representation must be specific enough to be acted on. A
representation that says something is unresolved without saying what
the candidates are has not preserved enough structure. A buyer who
is told the steering vibration could be one of several things has
been told less than a buyer who is told the steering vibration is
consistent with warped rotors, worn bushings, tire imbalance, or
alignment drift. The second list is actionable. The first is not.

The representation must hold open the candidates without ranking
them prematurely. A list that says the cause is probably warped
rotors but might be bushings has assigned a ranking the inspection
did not support. The ranking is a separate inference that should be
earned, not asserted. The obligation is to hold the candidates
equally until further testing narrows the set.

The representation must point at the tests that would distinguish
the candidates. A list of possible causes with no path to resolution
is a sterile list. The brake-vibration candidate set links to
specific tests: rotor-runout measurement, which would distinguish
warped rotors; suspension inspection, which would distinguish worn
bushings; tire-balance check, which would distinguish imbalance;
alignment-rack measurement, which would distinguish alignment drift.
The candidates and the tests are tied together.

The shortness of the name refuses the temptation to resolve the
unresolved by speculation. The class preserves the open shape until
disciplined investigation closes it.

The buyer does not yet know whether the vibration under braking is
warped rotors, worn bushings, tire imbalance, or frame damage from
a prior accident. The mechanic records each possibility as a
candidate, with the test that would confirm or deny it. The rotors
will be checked with a dial indicator at the next inspection
station. The bushings will be visually inspected and tested for play
with a pry bar. The tires can be re-balanced and the test drive
repeated. The frame can be checked at a body shop with a frame-
measurement fixture if the other tests do not isolate the cause.
The unresolved state is represented in the inspection record as a
specific candidate set with specific resolution paths.

The buyer does not have to pick one candidate to act on. The buyer
can request that the seller cover the cost of the candidate set's
investigation as a condition of purchase. The buyer can walk away
from the deal until the seller has done the investigation. The
buyer can buy the car at a discount that accounts for the
worst-case repair, since frame damage requires the most expensive
remediation. Each is an action the buyer can take with the
representation intact. None of them requires premature resolution.

An architect marks future stair on a renovation plan. The stair is
not yet built. The plan does not yet contain the stair's
dimensions, materials, or finish. But the plan has a place where
the stair will go. The place is a representable, not a stair. The
contractor reading the plan knows that the stair will be added
later, that the framing must accommodate it, and that the floor
and ceiling openings need to be left for it. The plan's discipline
is to mark the representable in a form that the contractor can
build around without knowing the eventual stair's specifics.

The architect's discipline has been codified by the profession.
Construction documents have specific conventions for marking
unresolved elements. A future-stair callout uses a particular
graphic notation. A future-window callout uses another. A by
others note indicates an element that someone else will design.
Each notation is the codified form of representability for the
construction discipline. The contractor who reads the plan knows
what each notation requires and what each notation forbids.

A journal article reports a finding with a confidence interval. The
finding is not resolved as a single value; it is represented as a
range with an associated probability. The interval is not a sign of
ignorance; it is the disciplined preservation of the finding's
uncertainty. A reader who collapses the interval to its midpoint
has discarded the representation. A reader who treats the interval
as opaque has missed the representation's structure.

Representations can be padded. An inspection that lists ten possible
causes when only three are operationally plausible has padded the
representation. The padding may look thorough but is actually a
refusal to commit to a reasonable candidate set. The discipline is
to keep the candidate set small enough to investigate and large
enough to cover the actual possibilities.

Representation cannot float free as mere notation.

\section{\texttt{PHYSICAL}}
\label{se:physical}

Symbols need contact with an instrument.

A representation that never touches the world is decoration. The
notation on the page must be tied to a process that could enter a
measurement record. Otherwise the symbol is doing imitation work
without doing inspection work. A check-mark in an inspection box
that was never actually checked is the symbol pretending to be the
inspection.

Representations are tempting to manufacture. A clean inspection
report is easier to produce than an honest inspection report. A
column of checkmarks looks the same whether each check was earned
by an actual measurement or written in to make the form complete.
The discipline is to require that each representation in the record
correspond to a specific physical process that produced it.

Physical contact is what makes records falsifiable. A reading that
came from an instrument can be re-taken, re-calibrated, or compared
against a different instrument. A reading that was made up cannot
be replayed. The ability to re-test depends on the original reading
having been produced by a physical process that can be reproduced.
Without physical anchoring, the inspection's discipline collapses.

\texttt{PHYSICAL}

The class says the representation is anchored to a process. The
class carries the obligation to maintain that anchoring.

The process must be specifiable. A reading produced by some method
the inspector cannot describe has not been anchored; it has only
been asserted. The description must include the instrument, the
procedure, the conditions, and the result. The description is what
allows the process to be reproduced by another inspector.

The process must be reproducible by parties other than the original
inspector. An inspector who has invented an ad-hoc test that no
other inspector can replicate has not produced an admissible
reading. The reproducibility constraint prevents private
methodologies from entering the record as if they were public ones.

The process must be appropriate for the representation. A
representation of catalytic-converter efficiency that was produced
by visual inspection has not been anchored to a physical process
appropriate for the representation. Catalytic efficiency requires
emissions measurement; visual inspection cannot produce a value for
it.

The shortness of the name refuses the imagined finding. The class
will not let a representation enter the record unless the
representation came from a process that produced it.

Frame damage cannot remain a suspicion in the inspection record.
The suspicion has to touch lift points the mechanic can examine,
panel gaps the gauge can measure, weld marks the mechanic can see,
paint depth the meter can read, alignment readings the rack can
produce, and repair photos a prior shop may have on file. Each
contact converts the suspicion into a representation tied to a
process. A frame-damage entry in the report that lists none of
those touches is not yet \texttt{PHYSICAL}. The entry is a
suspicion in the inspector's mind that has been written down as if
it were a finding.

The inspection's procedure for frame damage has been codified. The
mechanic visually inspects the unibody for evidence of collision
damage. The mechanic measures panel gaps with a feeler gauge to
detect misalignment. The mechanic looks for weld marks that should
not be on a factory body. The mechanic runs a paint-depth meter
across body panels to detect filler. The mechanic places the car
on the alignment rack and reads suspension geometry. The mechanic
requests the vehicle-history report's photos from any prior
repairs. Each step is a physical contact. The frame-damage entry
in the report cites the steps that produced it.

A buyer who reads the report and sees a frame-damage entry with no
cited steps has a procedural problem. Either the steps were
performed and the citations were lost, or the steps were not
performed and the entry is imagined. Either case requires follow-up
before the buyer acts on the entry.

A weather icon means little unless it is tied to barometric
pressure, radar return, or thermometer readings. The same cloud
icon on a weather app can be honest or theatrical. The honest one
is produced by an automated process that reads instrument data and
selects the icon based on codified rules. The theatrical one is an
editor's choice, made to fit the app's narrative or the
advertiser's preference. The icon looks the same in both cases.
The user has no visual indication of which one is being shown.

The codification of weather forecasting has produced standards for
what icons mean and what data they should be produced from. The
National Weather Service in the United States has explicit
guidelines: an icon for thunderstorms should appear when the
probability of thunderstorms exceeds a threshold, computed from a
specific set of instrument inputs. Apps that follow the guidelines
produce icons that are physical in the procession's sense. Apps
that do not produce icons that are decorative.

A clinical diagnosis recorded in a medical chart is a
representation. The representation must be anchored to physical
processes: lab results, imaging studies, physical examination
findings, patient-reported symptoms with their elicitation method.
A diagnosis written into a chart without anchoring is a guess
recorded as a fact. The chart's discipline has been codified by
medical regulation: each diagnosis must be supported by documented
findings. The auditor who reviews the chart looks for the support.
A diagnosis without support is a finding that the chart did not
produce in compliance with the discipline.

Physical anchoring can be partial. A frame-damage entry may be
supported by a paint-depth reading but lack the alignment-rack
confirmation. The entry is partially anchored. The discipline
requires that the partial anchoring be reported as partial. An
entry that claims to be fully anchored when it is partially
anchored has overstated its support.

Physical records must be brought under shared questions.

\section{\texttt{COMPARABLE}}
\label{se:comparable}

Two records do not compare themselves.

Different instruments produce different units. Different contexts
produce different baselines. Different representations carry
different fields. Comparison requires a shared question that brings
the records under a common discipline. Without the question, the
records sit beside each other without speaking.

Comparison is not natural. A buyer who wants to compare this car's
brake-pad reading to another car's brake-pad reading cannot do so
unless both readings were produced by the same procedure with the
same precision against the same threshold. The procedure, the
precision, and the threshold are the shared question. Without them,
the two millimeter readings are two numbers that happen to share a
unit but do not measure the same thing in a comparable way.

The shared question may not exist yet. A buyer comparing two cars
from different dealers may find that the two dealers used different
inspection procedures. The brake-pad readings are not directly
comparable because the procedures produced different kinds of
measurements. The buyer's options are to bring both cars under a
common procedure, having both re-inspected by the same shop; to
make the procedures comparable through cross-calibration,
translating one dealer's procedure into the other's; or to accept
that the readings cannot be compared.

\texttt{COMPARABLE}

The class names the permission to bring records under the same
comparison. The class is short, but the permission it grants is
non-trivial. A reader who claims records are comparable must have
done the work of identifying the shared question.

The shared question must be public. A buyer who privately decides
that two readings are comparable because the buyer feels they
should be has not satisfied the obligation. The shared question has
to be stated, codified, and reviewable.

The shared question must actually apply to both records. A question
that fits the first record but does not fit the second is not a
shared question; it is a question imposed on the second record by
force. The fit has to be established.

The shared question may require translation. Two records produced
under different conventions may both speak to the shared question
after translation, even if neither originally spoke to it. The
translation is itself a disciplined operation, and the
translation's correctness is part of the obligation.

The shortness of the name refuses the temptation to compare numbers
without checking that they answer the same question.

The dealer asking price, the private-party listing on the same
model, the auction value, and the mechanic's repair estimate become
comparable only under the buyer's question. The question is whether
this car is worth the offered price after likely repairs. The
question makes the four numbers a single comparison.

The dealer asking price reflects the dealer's overhead, warranty,
reconditioning costs, and profit margin. The private-party listing
reflects an individual seller's expectations and negotiating
position. The auction value reflects wholesale conditions at a
specific auction house. The repair estimate reflects the parts and
labor for the inspection's specific findings.

Each number measures something different in isolation. The buyer's
question forces them into a common frame: how much should the
buyer pay, accounting for what each number represents about the
market and the repairs. The translation is done by the buyer's
pricing model, which can include markups for dealer guarantees,
discounts for private-party risk, adjustments for auction-to-retail
spread, and subtractions for the repair estimate. The model is the
shared question's machinery.

The buyer's pricing model has been codified, at least informally,
in the consumer-protection literature and the vehicle-pricing
services. Kelley Blue Book and similar services publish
methodologies that explain how dealer prices, private-party
prices, and auction values are derived from the same underlying
transaction data. The methodology is the shared question's
documentation. A buyer who uses the service's numbers without
reading the methodology is using the comparison without
understanding the comparison's discipline.

Two recipes become comparable only when both are converted to
servings, grams, oven temperature, and time. Until the conversion,
one recipe says cups and the other says deciliters and they sit on
different counters. The conversion is the shared question's
machinery: how much of each ingredient does each recipe call for,
per serving, in a common unit. The conversion preserves the
recipes' information while making them comparable.

A cook who wants to choose between two bread recipes needs both
recipes in the same units. The cook can convert by hand, use a
kitchen scale, or use a software tool. Each conversion method has
accuracy implications: hand conversion may round; the scale may
be calibrated to a specific gram. The conversion's discipline
matters. A cook who converts sloppily produces a comparison that
may favor the wrong recipe.

Shared questions can be wrong questions. The buyer's pricing model
may give too much weight to dealer guarantees, or not enough
weight to private-party risk. The recipes' conversion may
emphasize ingredient weight when the cook actually cares about
ingredient ratio. The shared question's correctness is itself a
question that has to be asked.

Some comparisons cross into observation.

\section{\texttt{OBSERVED}}
\label{se:observed}

Possible is not yet recorded.

The event has entered the ledger. The instrument has admitted it.
Until the crossing, the event is a possibility, a worry, a
candidate. After the crossing, the event is in the record.

The difference between what might be the case and what has been
registered as the case is operationally large. A car may have a
problem; until the inspection has observed the problem, the
problem is not in the record. The buyer cannot act on what is not
in the record. The seller cannot be asked to address what is not
in the record. The repair shop cannot estimate what is not in the
record. The crossing into observation is the prerequisite for
everything that follows.

Observation is not automatic. The world is full of phenomena. Most
phenomena are not observed by any particular inspection. The
inspection's procedure determines which phenomena will be observed
and which will not. The procedure is the gate that controls what
crosses into the record.

\texttt{OBSERVED}

The class names the crossing. The obligation is that the record
reflect a specific event of crossing for each entry. An entry that
claims to be observed but has no associated crossing event has
been imagined into the record.

The crossing must have a time. An observation entered with no
timestamp has not preserved the crossing's event-ness. The next
reader cannot ask when the observation happened, which other
observations were contemporaneous, or whether subsequent
observations may have superseded the entry.

The crossing must have an instrument. An observation entered
without an instrument has not been anchored to the apparatus that
produced it. A mechanic observed a coolant smell after the test
drive. The instrument is the mechanic's nose. The instrument is
not as precise as an emissions analyzer, but it is an instrument.
The entry's status depends on which instrument did the observing.

The crossing must be replayable in principle. The observation must
have been produced under conditions that could be reproduced. An
observation that happened once, under conditions the inspection
cannot describe, is not yet a durable observation. It is an
incident report.

The shortness of the name refuses the temptation to record as
observed what was merely expected. The class will not let an
entry into the record unless an event of observation has actually
occurred.

The mechanic places the stethoscope on the intake manifold and the
ticking enters the report. Before the placement, the ticking might
have been heard or imagined or argued about. After the placement,
the ticking is a noted observation with a timestamp, a location on
the engine, and a brief description: ticking present at idle,
location intake manifold near cylinder three, characteristic of
stuck lifter or worn cam follower.

The same event has crossed from suspicion into observation. The
next inspection step can treat it as on the record. The mechanic
can request a follow-up oil-pressure test to distinguish hydraulic
lifter problems from worn cam followers. The mechanic can record
the noise in a video file attached to the report. The buyer can
ask for the noise to be quoted to the dealer's service department.
Each move is licensed by the crossing into observation.

The mechanic's discipline is to make the crossing explicit. A
report that simply says noisy engine has not preserved the
crossing. A report that says ticking present at idle, intake
manifold, cylinder-three vicinity, recorded at 11:42 on inspection
date, audio clip filename xyz-12 has preserved the crossing. The
latter form gives subsequent readers a way to re-encounter the
observation.

A teacher does not grade a student's likely method. The answer
becomes observed when it appears on the page in front of the
teacher. The likely method may have been excellent. Until the
page, it is not in the record. The grade is assigned to what was
written, not to what was thought.

The teacher's discipline carries the obligation. A teacher who
grades on probable understanding rather than on what the page
shows has confused expectation with observation. The grading
conventions of academic institutions formalize this: rubrics
specify what the page must show to earn each level of credit. The
rubric is the gate. The student's mark on the page is what
crosses the gate.

A clinical trial endpoint follows the same shape. The trial
protocol specifies what observations will count as the primary
endpoint. The protocol's specifications include when the
observations will be taken, what instruments will be used, and
how the observations will be coded. An outcome that occurs between
scheduled observations may not be captured in the endpoint data.
The trial's discipline is to specify in advance which events will
cross into the record and which will not.

Observation is selective by design. The inspection will observe
many things and refuse to observe others. A scratch on the inside
of the trunk lid will not typically be observed; the inspection's
procedure does not include trunk-interior inspection. The
non-observation is not a failure; it is a scope choice.

Some recorded things remain unresolved but present.

\section{\texttt{PRESENT}}
\label{se:present}

Unresolved does not mean absent.

A sealed envelope is present without being read. A legal blank is
present without being filled. A compiler obligation is present
while still unresolved. The lazy alternatives are to declare the
unresolved thing absent or to resolve it prematurely. Both
alternatives lose information. The discipline is to keep the
unresolved thing in the record, marked as open, until the record
can close it honestly.

Records prefer closure. An open item on a form looks incomplete.
A pending diagnosis looks like a diagnosis the doctor has not
made. A flagged exception looks like a failure. The temptation is
to push each open item toward closure, even when closure is not
yet warranted. The push removes information from the record.

Open items have specific shapes. A pending lab result is a
specific kind of openness with a specific kind of closure path. A
flagged exception is another kind. An open question awaiting
evidence is another. The shapes are not equivalent.

\texttt{PRESENT}

The class refuses the lazy alternatives. The thing is not gone.
It is held open. The class names the obligation to maintain the
openness with its specific structure intact.

The open item must be locatable. A record that marks something as
open without saying where in the record the openness lives is not
yet preserving the openness. The buyer must be able to find the
open items in the record without combing through the entire
document.

The open item must be reviewable. Each open item should be tagged
with what would resolve it. A pending lab result resolves when
the lab returns the result. A flagged exception resolves when the
exception is investigated. An open question resolves when the
relevant evidence arrives.

The shortness of the name refuses the temptation to treat open as
broken. The class distinguishes between a record that has failed,
cannot be completed, and a record that is incomplete in known
places, can be completed by specific further work.

The OBD scanner reports a pending misfire code on cylinder three.
The code is pending because the engine has not yet seen enough
drive cycles for the check-engine light to come on. The buyer
should not call the engine failed; the code is not confirmed. The
buyer also cannot pretend the code is absent; the scanner has
reported it. The honest entry is that a pending code on cylinder
three is present.

The pending code's specific shape matters. A pending code is the
manufacturer's signal that the onboard diagnostic system has
detected an intermittent condition that has not yet reached the
threshold for a stored code. The threshold is specified by the
emissions regulations: typically, the condition must be observed
in two consecutive drive cycles before the code stores and the
check-engine light illuminates. The pending status is the
in-between: the condition has been observed once and is being
watched for confirmation.

The buyer's discipline is to record the pending code with its
specific shape: the code number, the cylinder, the date of
detection, the conditions under which it was detected, and the
resolution path, either additional drive cycles to confirm or
clear, or proactive investigation of the cylinder's spark, fuel,
and compression. The buyer can act on the open item as an open
item: ask the seller to address it before sale, or account for it
in the negotiated price.

A buyer who closes the pending code prematurely, deciding the code
is probably nothing, has discarded information that the
manufacturer designed the system to provide. A buyer who reports
the code as if it were a stored code has overstated the finding.
Both are violations of the discipline.

An unsigned contract clause is present on the page. It is not yet
binding. It is also not gone. The negotiation must acknowledge it
before either party can act. The clause's openness has a specific
shape: the parties have not agreed on the terms, but the
placeholder text indicates that an agreement is expected.

A contract reviewer's discipline is to identify each unsigned
clause and either obtain agreement or strike the clause. A clause
that remains unsigned at signing becomes a future dispute. A
clause that is removed without explicit deletion becomes a future
ambiguity. The professional standards for contract drafting
require explicit handling of every clause: present, removed, or
modified.

A chess game adjourned in classical chess, before adjudication
software, was a game that could not be finished in one sitting.
The position was recorded on paper. The next session resumed from
the recorded position. The recorded position is a present item:
the game is not over, and the position is preserved exactly enough
to continue. The recording discipline is precise: the position of
every piece, the side to move, the castling rights, the en
passant square, and the move count. Any imprecision in the
recording would allow disputes about the resumed position.

Open items can persist. A pending lab result that has been pending
for six months is still open, but its status as actively under
investigation is questionable. An item that has been forgotten is
no longer being actively held open; it is being neglected. The
record should mark such items differently from items under active
investigation.

The open place must still be measurable.

\section{\texttt{MEASURABLE}}
\label{se:measurable}

Holding open is not the same as refusing discipline.

A record that holds a thing open without discipline becomes a
wishful container. The held-open thing must remain accessible to
further investigation. The investigation must be able to bound the
thing, test it, and eventually resolve it or document its
non-resolution. Without that accessibility, the open item becomes a
placeholder that no one can act on.

Openness can degrade. An open item that no one investigates
eventually becomes an item that is nominally open but functionally
inert. The record has a present entry, but the entry has no path
to resolution.

\texttt{MEASURABLE}

The class says the unresolved place can still be asked disciplined
questions. The class carries the obligation that the open item is
bounded, testable, and linked to a procedure that could resolve
it.

The open item must be bounded. A candidate set that is potentially
infinite is not measurable; the investigation cannot proceed if
every possible cause might apply. The class requires that the
candidate set be finite and that the boundary be stated. A
mechanic who lists four possible causes for the brake vibration
has bounded the candidate set; a mechanic who says it could be
anything has not.

Each candidate in the bounded set must have a test. The test may
be expensive. The test may take time. The test must exist. A
candidate without a test is a candidate the inspection cannot
evaluate; including it in the candidate set does not advance the
investigation.

The tests must be ordered. Some tests are cheap; some are
expensive. Some tests rule out multiple candidates at once; some
rule out only one. The investigation proceeds most efficiently
when the cheap tests that rule out multiple candidates come
first. The class does not specify the ordering; it demands that
an ordering be chosen.

The shortness of the name refuses the wishful openness that lacks
any path to resolution.

The pending misfire code on cylinder three can be tested. The
mechanic can run a compression test on the cylinder. The mechanic
can pull the spark plug and read its condition. The mechanic can
swap the coil with cylinder four and rescan to see if the code
follows the coil. The mechanic can take the car on a cold start
and watch the fuel-trim data live. The mechanic can perform a
leak-down test to distinguish ring wear from valve seal wear.
Each test is a measurement against the open place.

The tests are ordered by cost and information yield. The spark-
plug inspection is cheap and rules out spark fouling. The
compression test is moderately expensive and rules out mechanical
compression loss. The coil swap test is cheap if the engine layout
allows access and rules out coil-related misfires. The fuel-trim
observation is cheap if the data stream is accessible and rules
out lean or rich conditions. The leak-down test is expensive and
refines the compression test's findings. The mechanic does not
run all tests simultaneously; the mechanic chooses an order that
minimizes the inspection's cost while maximizing the chance of
isolating the cause.

The open place is not a wish. It is a thing under disciplined
investigation. The buyer can be told what tests are pending, what
each test would distinguish, and what the test results would
cost. The buyer can decide whether to fund the testing, to
negotiate the testing as a condition of sale, or to walk away
from a deal whose testing would exceed the buyer's budget.

A cracked ceramic bowl can be tapped, lit, magnified, and weighed
before a conservator decides whether it can be repaired. The
crack is open. The investigation is bounded. The tap test
reveals the crack's extent by listening for the change in
resonance. The lighting test reveals the crack's pattern under
transmitted light. The magnification test reveals the crack's
edges and any micro-fracturing. The weighing test reveals whether
material has been lost. Each test is a disciplined question. The
conservator's procedure is to run the tests in a specific order,
recording the results, before deciding on a repair strategy.

The conservation profession has codified these procedures. The
Smithsonian, the American Institute for Conservation, and similar
bodies publish standards for examination and documentation. The
standards include not only the techniques but the order in which
they should be applied to minimize risk to the object.

An open scientific question follows the same shape. A hypothesis
that has not yet been tested is present; the same hypothesis
becomes measurable when an experiment can be designed that would
distinguish it from its alternatives. The experiment's
specification includes the intervention, the measurements, the
controls, the sample size, the analysis plan, and the criteria for
a positive or negative result. The hypothesis becomes practically
investigable when these are in place.

Measurability does not guarantee resolution. The pending misfire
code may resist all the tests. The compression test, the spark-
plug inspection, the coil swap, and the fuel-trim observation may
each return results that do not isolate the cause. The
investigation has been disciplined; the cause has not been found.
The obligation is met by the disciplined investigation, not by the
resolution. An item can remain measurable after the investigation
has been run and the cause has not been determined. The next
inspection cycle may find it.

Unresolved states need a process that carries them without
pretending they are resolved.

\begin{movement}{From Residue to Staging}

The record now carries leftovers it cannot ignore. Each leftover
has a face that comparison can read, a discipline that holds the
open place, and a form of investigation that keeps the open place
honest. The procession has spent the longest movement on this work
because the discipline of the leftover requires the most names.
\texttt{RESIDUE}, \texttt{BINARY}, \texttt{REPEATABLE},
\texttt{NUMERIC}, \texttt{REPRESENTABLE}, \texttt{PHYSICAL},
\texttt{COMPARABLE}, \texttt{OBSERVED}, \texttt{PRESENT}, and
\texttt{MEASURABLE} are not redundant. Each names a specific
obligation the leftover must satisfy to remain in the record
without falsifying the record.

The reader who has walked this movement has acquired a vocabulary
for talking about the imperfect record. The record's gaps,
overflows, and partial findings now have specific names. A reader
who finishes a real inspection can point to the inspection's
residues, distinguish the ones that pass a binary gate from the
ones that fail, note which are repeatable, ask whether each has
been turned into a numeric finding, recognize which are
representable as candidate sets, anchor each to a physical
process, judge whether the candidates are comparable under the
relevant question, mark which have been observed and which remain
merely suspected, hold the present ones open without prematurely
closing them, and confirm that each present one is measurable by
a specified test.

The next movement walks the disciplined leftover through a
process. The first name in the new movement is rude.

\end{movement}

\section{\texttt{GUNGAN}}
\label{se:gungan}

Every serious system needs a way to carry the awkward thing.

The function is this: the system has to carry an entry whose status
is neither closed nor invented. Not yes. Not no. Not blank. The
carrier of the entry is the machinery the class names. The entry is
held under a specific further process that will, in principle,
resolve it. Until the further process completes, the entry sits in
the awkward middle, refusing both closure and erasure.

Systems hate awkward middles. A spreadsheet prefers every cell
filled with a definite value. A status column prefers every row
marked done or not started. A workflow prefers every task either
active or complete. The awkward middle requires its own column, its
own status, its own machinery. The machinery's cost is real. The
cost is not large enough to justify dropping the function, but it
is large enough that systems are tempted to drop it.

The awkward middle is informative. An entry that has been held in
the middle for six months tells the reader something about the
entry that an entry resolved in three days would not. The duration
of the awkwardness, the conditions under which the entry came to
be awkward, the obstacles to its resolution: each is data the
record holds only because the machinery refused to push the entry
to either side.

\texttt{GUNGAN}

A rude name for a real function. A polished name like
\texttt{PendingResolutionRegistry} would describe what the class
does without telling the reader why the function is necessary. The
rude name keeps the discomfort in view. The function is
uncomfortable, and the discomfort is not a defect.

The class marks the fact that unresolved states still need
machinery, in code, in process, and in language, to keep them from
collapsing into either nonsense or premature certainty.

The awkward middle must be distinguishable from the resolved
cases. A pending lab result is not a returned lab result; a
pending diagnosis is not a confirmed diagnosis; a flagged
exception is not a passed inspection. The discipline preserves the
middle's status as a separate status, not collapsed into the
resolved cases for appearance.

The awkward middle must be addressable. The machinery must allow a
reader to find the middle entries, to inspect each one, to update
each one when it resolves, and to escalate each one when it
remains stuck. An entry that has been moved into a middle state
without an addressable handle has been lost, even if the state
column still says pending.

The awkward middle must have a duration policy. An entry that has
been in the middle indefinitely is no longer being actively held;
it has been forgotten in place. The class does not specify the
duration; it demands that some duration policy exist. The policy
may be one week, one month, or one inspection cycle. The policy
provides the discipline that prevents indefinite limbo.

The rudeness of the name refuses the polished erasure of the
function. A system that pretends to have no awkward middles has
either invented closures it did not earn or has lost entries that
should have been held.

The inspection report needs status labels for hold for second
opinion, requires lift inspection at a different shop, seller
must provide receipt before close, and call back after cold-start
data is captured. None of these labels says the inspection is
finished. None of them says the inspection is unfinished in a way
that means the buyer should walk. Each is the report's honest
record that a specific state is being held under a specific
further test.

The hold-for-second-opinion label applies to findings the mechanic
is uncertain about. The mechanic suspects the transmission may
have an internal leak, but the evidence is ambiguous: a small wet
spot under the bell housing that could also be condensation. The
mechanic flags the finding for a second mechanic to evaluate. The
flag carries forward into the buyer's decision: the second
mechanic's evaluation can change the buy/walk/renegotiate
recommendation.

The requires-lift-inspection-elsewhere label applies to findings
the inspection shop's equipment cannot resolve. The mechanic
suspects undercarriage rust may be more extensive than the
mid-rise lift allows access to. A full lift inspection at a
different shop is needed. The flag carries forward: the buyer can
budget for the additional inspection, ask the seller to provide
it, or accept the rust-extent risk and negotiate accordingly.

The seller-must-provide-receipt label applies to findings the
seller has not yet documented. The seller claims a recent timing-
belt service; the seller cannot produce the invoice during the
inspection. The flag says the inspection's confidence in the
timing-belt's age depends on the document. The buyer's decision
includes a contingency: receipt produced before close, or the
purchase price is reduced by the cost of a timing-belt service.

The call-back-after-cold-start label applies to findings that
need additional data over time. The pending misfire code may
clear or progress; the buyer needs another data point in a week.
The flag holds the inspection's findings open with a specific
follow-up scheduled.

The buyer can carry the report into the negotiation with these
labels intact. The seller can respond to each one individually.
The inspection's machinery has done the work the class demands:
the awkward middles are preserved, addressable, and under specific
further processes.

A library keeps damaged books on a repair cart instead of
returning them to circulation or throwing them away. The cart is
not the shelf. The cart is not the trash bin. The cart is the
library saying these books are present, are not yet circulating,
are not yet discarded, and are being held under a specific
further process. The machinery is ungainly. The discipline is
necessary. Without it, damaged books would either be loaned to
readers in bad condition or thrown away without review.

The library's discipline has been codified by the profession. The
American Library Association publishes standards for collection
care. The standards distinguish between damaged-but-repairable,
damaged-and-evaluated-for-deaccession, deaccessioned-pending-
disposal, and active-circulation. Each status has its own
shelving location, its own record-keeping, and its own duration
policy. A book on the repair cart for longer than three months
gets escalated to the head librarian for a deaccession decision.
The policy prevents the cart from becoming a permanent purgatory.

A hospital's admission status holding queue follows the same
shape. A patient arriving at the emergency department who is too
sick to discharge but does not yet have a confirmed admission
diagnosis is held under observation. The observation status is
not discharge; the patient stays in the hospital. The observation
status is not admission; the hospital's billing, nursing
staffing, and care protocols differ. The observation status is
its own category, with its own time limits and its own escalation
rules.

The awkward middle's machinery can be abused. A label like
requires further investigation can be applied so liberally that
the inspection's findings become a wall of pending items, none of
which actually have a resolution path. The discipline includes
the requirement that each middle-status entry has a specific
resolution path. The machinery is not a way to defer everything;
it is a way to hold specific items under specific further work.
An inspection that flags everything pending has not satisfied the
discipline; it has used the machinery as a way to avoid making
findings.

A report that lists every awkward state as fail has invented
certainty. A report that lists every awkward state as pass has
invented closure. A report that lists every awkward state as
pending without specifying a resolution path has invented
machinery. The class refuses all three moves: the awkward middle
must be held, but it must also be under a specific further
process with a specific timeline.

The carried unresolved state can now pass through stages.

\section{\texttt{SOURCE}}
\label{se:source}

A plan is not the act.

A recipe is not the cake. A score is not the music. A source file
is not the running program. The class names the form before
performance. The form may be complete in its way. It is not yet
the event the form will become.

Plans and acts can be confused. A planning document can be
celebrated as if the plan had been executed. A script can be read
as if the play had been performed. An inspection checklist can be
presented as if the inspection had been done. The substitution
flatters the planner without producing the result.

The form must be specific enough to control the act. A vague plan
does not produce a definite execution. A recipe that says some
flour produces variable bread. A checklist that says check brakes
produces variable inspections. The form's specificity is what
allows the act to be reproducible.

\texttt{SOURCE}

The class names the form before performance. The class carries
the obligation that the form exist, that the form be specific,
and that the form be distinct from the act.

The form must be inspectable independently of the act. A reader
should be able to read the plan and know what the plan calls for,
without watching the plan executed. The plan's text, schematic,
or notation must stand on its own.

The form must be revisable. A plan that cannot be amended is a
plan whose authors will not learn from the acts that follow it.
The class includes the recognition that the form will be improved
over time, in light of what the acts reveal about the form's
deficiencies.

The form must precede the act. A plan written after the act is
not a plan; it is a justification. The discipline rejects
retrospective planning.

The shortness of the name refuses the conflation of form and act.
The class will not allow the plan to be treated as the
performance.

The mechanic's inspection checklist is source. The checklist
names VIN check, title verification, fluid levels, OBD scan, tire
wear, brake wear, frame inspection, road test route, comparable
listings, and final recommendation. The checklist is a paper-and-
screen artifact. It exists before the car arrives at the shop.
The mechanic can read the checklist during quiet hours. The shop
manager can inspect the checklist to verify it covers the agreed
scope. The checklist is not the inspection. The car is not yet
on the lift. The buyer has not yet driven.

The checklist's specificity matters. A checklist that says check
brakes is too vague to control the act. A checklist that says
measure brake-pad thickness at front-inner, front-outer, rear-
inner, and rear-outer positions; record millimeters; replace if
below 3 mm has enough specificity to produce a comparable result
across cars and across mechanics. The shop's checklist evolves
over time as the shop encounters cars whose problems the prior
checklist missed.

A theater script is source. The script names entrances, lines,
lighting cues, and intermissions. The script can be read silently
in a coffee shop. The reading is not the play. The play has not
yet happened. The actors have not yet rehearsed; the lighting
designer has not yet hung the lamps; the audience has not yet
arrived.

The script's discipline has been codified by the theatrical
profession. Acting editions of plays include detailed stage
directions, character lists, and timing notes. The script's
specificity allows different productions to share a common basis
while making their own interpretive choices. A script that left
every detail to the director would not survive across
productions; a script that specified every detail would suffocate
interpretation. The codification balances the two.

A software specification before implementation describes what the
program will do, what inputs it will accept, what outputs it will
produce, and what failures it must handle. The specification is
not the program. The program has not yet been written. A reader
of the specification can verify that the specification covers the
requirements without watching the program execute. The
specification's value depends on its specificity.

Source forms can drift from acts. A checklist that is rarely
updated may diverge from what the shop actually does. A script
may be cut during rehearsal in ways the printed script does not
reflect. A specification may not match the implementation that
follows. The discipline includes the obligation to keep the
source and the act in documented relationship; a divergence that
is not documented is a divergence that future readers will be
confused by.

The source must be put through a process.

\section{\texttt{EXECUTED}}
\label{se:executed}

At some point, the plan spends itself in action.

The class says the record has crossed from source into
performance. The body has moved. The instruments have been
brought into contact with the object. The plan has been spent.
The class names the moment when the form becomes an event.

Records often stop at the form. A spreadsheet that holds the
inspection checklist is not the inspection. A signed authorization
is not the work. A staffing plan is not the staff at the work. The
discipline requires that the form's execution produce an event
whose record carries forward.

Execution has a cost. The form is cheap to produce; the execution
costs labor, materials, instruments, and time. The temptation is
to substitute form for execution and capture the cost savings.
The substitution falsifies the record.

\texttt{EXECUTED}

The class carries the obligation that the form has been spent in
an event the record can inspect.

The event must be witnessed. An execution that no one observed
has not produced an event the record can inspect. The witness may
be the executing party, an observer, an instrument log, or a
security camera.

The event must be traceable to the form. An execution that does
not reference the form it executed has not preserved the
connection. A mechanic who performs an inspection without
referring to the shop's checklist may have done good work, but
the record of the work cannot be compared to other inspections
done from the same checklist.

The event must produce an output. An execution that produces
nothing is not yet a complete execution; the plan has been spent
without leaving an inspectable result.

The shortness of the name refuses the unspent plan. The class
will not let the form alone count as having done the work.

The car is lifted, scanned, driven, photographed, compared, and
written up. Each verb is a moment of execution. The checklist
that named lift has produced a car on a lift. The checklist that
named scan has produced a scanner attached to the diagnostic
port. The checklist that named road test has produced a thirty-
minute drive on the prescribed route. The form has become an
event with a duration and a sequence.

The execution's discipline includes timestamps. The lift happened
at 10:14. The scan started at 10:22. The road test left the shop
at 10:51 and returned at 11:23. The timestamps make the
execution's sequence inspectable. A subsequent reviewer can ask
whether the execution followed the checklist's order, whether any
step was skipped, and whether the total execution time matches
the shop's expected duration.

A recipe is executed when ingredients are weighed, mixed, heated,
cooled, and plated. The recipe-on-paper persists. The performance
has happened in the kitchen, in time, with one cook's hands. The
cooked dish is the execution's output; the recipe is the source.
A diner who eats the dish cannot directly inspect the recipe, but
the dish reflects the recipe's execution. A flaw in execution,
under-cooked rice, an inverted measurement, a missed seasoning
step, shows up in the dish.

A legal contract being signed has the same shape. The contract is
the source. The signing is the execution. The witnessed
signature, the dated copy, and the recorded acknowledgment are
the execution's outputs. A contract that is drafted but unsigned
has source without execution. The distinction matters: the
unsigned contract does not bind.

Execution can deviate from form. The mechanic may skip a
checklist item because the item is not applicable to this car.
The cook may substitute an ingredient. The signatory may strike
a clause before signing. The deviations are part of the
execution's record; they should be noted as deviations rather
than hidden.

Execution must leave a carried result.

\section{\texttt{VALUE}}
\label{se:value}

After execution, something must remain usable.

What the process can carry forward after the source has passed
through the machine. The execution produces, alongside whatever
transient effects it had, a value that subsequent steps may use.

Executions can be exhausting without being productive. A meeting
can be held, a procedure can be performed, a journey can be
completed, all without producing anything that subsequent steps
can build on. The discipline requires that the execution leave a
carried output.

Values are not always tangible. The value of an inspection is
not a physical artifact, except for the written report; it is
the carried information that the buyer can use to decide. The
obligation includes the obligation that intangible outputs be
captured in some form that makes them transferable.

\texttt{VALUE}

The class carries the obligation that the execution produces a
transferable output the next process can act on.

The value must be specifiable. An execution that produces
something vague has not produced a value. The output must be
definite enough that the next reader can identify it and use it.

The value must be portable. An output that exists only in the
executor's head is not yet transferable. The discipline is that
the value enter a form that can be handed to the next party.

The value must be honest. An execution that produces a fabricated
output has not produced a value; it has produced an artifact that
pretends to be one.

The shortness of the name refuses the empty execution. The class
will not let an execution count as completed unless it produces a
value the next process can act on.

The carried result of the inspection is not the mechanic looked
at it. The carried result is the brake-pad reading, the trouble-
code list, the leak note, the repair estimate, the comparison
table, and the buy/walk/renegotiate recommendation. Those items
are the value. The buyer leaves the shop with them. The buyer may
now negotiate against them. The buyer may now decline against
them. The mechanic's hands have done the work, and the value is
what the buyer can carry into the next conversation.

The value's form matters. A written inspection report is more
transferable than a verbal summary. A signed report is more
authoritative than an unsigned one. A report with photos attached
is more compelling than a report without. The mechanic's
discipline includes producing the value in a form that maximizes
its usefulness to the buyer.

After a blood-pressure cuff runs, the value is the reading, not
the nurse's effort. The nurse's effort was real. The cuff
inflated, the cuff held, the stethoscope was placed, the
deflation was slow, the systolic and diastolic pressures were
noted. None of that effort is what the chart records. The chart
records the value the effort produced: 128 over 82. The nurse's
effort enabled the value; the value is what carries forward.

The chart's discipline distinguishes value from effort. The
chart does not record that the nurse worked hard; it records the
measurement. The physician reading the chart at the next visit
acts on the measurement, not the effort. If the chart recorded
effort instead of measurement, the next visit's physician would
not have a baseline to compare against.

A contract signing produces a value: the binding agreement. The
signatures, the seal, the witness's acknowledgment, the dated
copy: each contributes to the value's authority. The value is
what the parties carry forward into the relationship the contract
governs.

Values can be over-stated. An inspection report that states a buy
recommendation more confidently than the underlying findings
warrant has produced an inflated value. The honesty constraint
requires that the value reflect the findings accurately. The
mechanic's discipline includes calibrating the value's confidence
to the findings' strength.

Value invites the question of how much.

\section{\texttt{MAGNITUDE}}
\label{se:magnitude}

Quantity arrives late.

The public mistake is to think measurement begins with how much.
It does not. The system has already had to distinguish, admit,
count, encode, compare, represent, observe, hold open, stage,
execute, and carry value. Only then can it ask for magnitude. The
number that finally arrives sits on top of the entire procession.

Magnitudes are tempting to lead with. The buyer wants to know how
much the car costs, how many miles it has, how long the warranty
covers. The magnitudes are operational; they tell the buyer what
action to take. The discipline insists that magnitudes are not
the first move. They are the late move, produced only when the
procession has prepared the ground.

Magnitudes without context are unmoored. A number like 178 PSI
means little until the reader knows what is being measured, under
what conditions, against what reference. The obligation includes
the obligation that the magnitude come with the context that
makes it interpretable.

\texttt{MAGNITUDE}

The class names the obligation that the number be produced at the
right moment in the procession and that it arrive with its
context.

The magnitude must be specifiable to a precision the procession
can justify. An overly precise magnitude, a brake-pad thickness
reported as 4.2837 mm, claims more than the instrument supports.
An overly vague magnitude, brake-pad thickness about 4 mm,
discards information the instrument provided.

The magnitude must connect to its unit. A number without a unit
is an incomplete magnitude.

The magnitude must be reproducible. Two inspections of the same
car with the same instrument under similar conditions should
produce magnitudes within the expected variability. The
discipline of \texttt{MAGNITUDE} is the discipline of the entire
procession converging on a reproducible number.

The shortness of the name refuses the leading magnitude that
arrives without preparation. The class will not let a number into
the record unless the prior obligations have been satisfied.

The magnitude is four millimeters of front brake pad, one hundred
seventy-eight PSI of compression on cylinder one, one thousand
eight hundred dollars of estimated repairs, eighty-two thousand
four hundred forty-one miles on the odometer, and one thousand
one hundred dollars above the comparable median. Each number is
a magnitude. The numbers are not floating quantities. They sit
on instruments that have already crossed every earlier gate.

The four-millimeter brake-pad reading sits on the entire prior
procession. The car was distinguished, the inspection was
admitted, the measurement was counted, encoded, compared,
represented, observed, held open where needed, staged, executed,
and the value was carried. Only then is the magnitude four
millimeters a magnitude in the procession's sense. A magnitude
that has not passed through this much discipline is not yet a
magnitude that the procession would call \texttt{MAGNITUDE}. The
public habit of treating magnitudes as self-evident has skipped
the procession.

A kitchen scale gives three hundred twelve grams of flour. The
magnitude arrives only after the bowl has been placed on the
scale, the scale has been tared, the scale has been verified to
be on the right unit, the flour has been poured, and the digital
display has stabilized. The magnitude is the late arrival in a
sequence the cook has been managing. A cook who reads the display
before stability has been reached has caught a transient number,
not a magnitude.

A marathon finishing time follows the same shape. The number on
the clock at the finish line is the magnitude, but the magnitude
depends on the timing chip's reading at the start line, the
chip's reading at intermediate checkpoints, and the corrected
time for chip-based versus gun-based timing. The magnitude is
the procession's output, not its starting point.

Magnitudes can be misleading without being wrong. A correct
reading produced under non-standard conditions can be
technically accurate but operationally misleading. The discipline
includes the obligation to flag non-standard conditions when
they affect the magnitude's interpretation.

Magnitude without a scale is not enough.

\section{\texttt{SCALED}}
\label{se:scaled}

How much relative to what.

Magnitude is not naked. It is magnitude against a threshold, a
reference, a permitted range, or a calibrated baseline. The
threshold makes the number actionable. Without it, the number is
data without consequence.

Magnitudes do not have intrinsic meaning. A brake-pad reading of
four millimeters is concerning only because the shop's
replacement threshold is three. A compression reading of 178 PSI
is concerning only because the manufacturer's specification is
180 plus or minus 10. An asking price of 11,900 is concerning
only because the comparable median is 10,800. The magnitudes
need scales to become decisions.

Scales themselves are conventions. The shop's three-millimeter
threshold is a choice. A more conservative shop might use four
millimeters. A more lenient shop might use two. The discipline
includes making the scale explicit so that the buyer can know
which convention is being applied.

\texttt{SCALED}

The class carries the obligation that each magnitude in the
record be paired with the scale against which it is interpreted.

The scale must be public. A private scale that the inspector
uses without disclosing it is not yet a scale the record can
carry forward; the buyer cannot reproduce the decision rule.

The scale must be appropriate to the magnitude. A scale for
new-car brake pads, eight to ten millimeters typical, is not
appropriate for evaluating a ten-year-old used car, three to
five millimeters typical. The discipline includes choosing the
right scale.

The scale must be revisable when the magnitude's context
changes. The same brake-pad threshold should not apply to a
buyer who will drive twenty miles a year and to a buyer who will
drive twenty thousand. The discipline includes adjusting the
scale for the buyer's use case.

The shortness of the name refuses the floating magnitude. The
class will not let a number be acted on without its scale.

Four millimeters of brake pad is meaningful only against the
shop's safety threshold of three millimeters for replacement,
against the manufacturer's original-equipment spec of eight to
ten millimeters, and against the buyer's planned driving
distance. The same magnitude with the same unit becomes different
decisions under different scales. The shop's threshold says
replace soon. The original-equipment spec says the pads are at
fifty percent of life. The buyer's short commute says the
replacement can wait six months.

Eleven thousand nine hundred dollars is meaningful only against
the band of comparable sales at ten thousand eight hundred to
eleven thousand four hundred, against the buyer's budget of
twelve thousand, against the loan amount the bank will approve,
and against the cost of similar cars at other dealers. Each
scale produces a different framing. The comparable band says the
price is high. The buyer's budget says the price is in range.
The loan amount says the financing works. The other dealers'
prices say the deal is competitive in this region but not
elsewhere.

Eighty-two thousand miles is meaningful only against the
manufacturer's expected service life of two hundred thousand for
this model and against this buyer's expected commute of fifteen
thousand a year. The scales say the car has plenty of life left
and that the buyer will reach the next major service interval in
two years. Without those scales, the mileage reading is just a
number.

A reading of 38.5 degrees Celsius is fever only because clinical
thresholds are in play. The same number on a sauna thermometer
is barely warm. The same number on a car-engine thermostat is
dangerous overheating. The number is identical. The scale is
different. The scale is what makes the number a verdict.

The clinical scale has been codified by medical practice. Normal
body temperature is 37 degrees Celsius. Low-grade fever is 37.5
to 38.5. Moderate fever is 38.5 to 39.5. High fever is above
39.5. The thresholds drive treatment decisions. The same
magnitude could appear on a sauna thermometer with no medical
implication whatsoever; the sauna scale runs 50 to 90 with the
thresholds for use being different entirely.

A credit score of 720 is a magnitude only after the scale is
known. The FICO scale runs 300 to 850, with 720 being good but
not excellent. The VantageScore scale runs differently and
assigns different qualitative bands. Two scores of 720 from
different scales mean different things. The lender's discipline
includes naming the scale.

Scales can be biased. The shop's brake-pad threshold may favor
frequent replacement, since more replacement means more revenue
for the shop. The clinical fever thresholds may differ by
demographic in ways that have led to under-treatment of some
groups. The discipline cannot eliminate scale bias; it can
require that the scale be public so that the bias can be
inspected.

Carrying a scale creates load.

\begin{movement}{From Staging to Overhead}

The record now stages its results against scales that give them
consequence. The procession has carried the awkward middle through
\texttt{GUNGAN}, named the plan in \texttt{SOURCE}, executed the
plan in \texttt{EXECUTED}, captured the output in \texttt{VALUE},
produced the numeric magnitude in \texttt{MAGNITUDE}, and pressed
the magnitude against an interpretive scale in \texttt{SCALED}.
The procession has produced a record that contains decisions, not
just data.

The scales themselves have a cost. They must be maintained,
verified, and brought into the inspection. The reference standards
need to be current. The thresholds need to be defensible. The
codifications need to be available for review. None of this is
free, and the cost accumulates around the record without being
part of the record's substance.

The next movement is uncomfortable. The names get ruder. The
discipline is the same.

\end{movement}

\section{\texttt{LOAD}}
\label{se:load}

A scale is a burden the system must carry.

To measure under a scale, the system has to keep the scale
available. The manuals must be on hand. The calibration must be
current. The standards must be stored. The trained inspector must
be present. Each scale that gives a number consequence also
imposes a cost.

The previous movement's discipline depends on infrastructure most
readers do not notice. The shop's brake-pad threshold did not
arrive from nowhere. The threshold was set by the shop based on
accumulated experience, codified in the shop's manual, communicated
to every mechanic during training, and maintained as the shop
updates its procedures. The threshold's existence imposes a cost
on the shop. The cost is hidden inside the inspection's price; the
buyer pays for the threshold's maintenance through the inspection
fee.

Load is often invisible from outside. A buyer who walks into the
shop sees a mechanic and a car. The buyer does not see the years
of training, the certifications, the equipment calibrations, the
manuals, the warranty databases, or the continuing education
courses that allow the mechanic to perform the inspection.

\texttt{LOAD}

The class carries the obligation that the cost of carrying the
scale be acknowledged and accounted for.

The load must be identifiable. A shop that cannot specify what it
carries, manuals, calibrations, training, cannot be held accountable
for the load.

The load must be allocable. The cost of carrying the scale must be
charged to someone. The shop charges the buyer indirectly through
the inspection fee. A regulator charges the public through taxes
and fees. A research laboratory charges its funders.

The load can be excessive. A scale that demands more carrying than
its consequence warrants is an over-loaded scale. The discipline is
to keep the load proportionate to the decision the scale enables.

The shortness of the name refuses the invisible load that flatters
the process by hiding its cost. The class will not let the scale's
cost be pretended into nonexistence.

The buyer brings or trusts the mechanic to bring repair manuals,
price guides, vehicle-history report subscriptions, inspection
tools, lender pre-approval terms, state title rules, and the
shop's labor rate. The buyer also brings attention, time, and the
cost of delaying the purchase to do the inspection at all. Each is
part of the load.

The manuals must be current: the manufacturer issues service
bulletins and recall notices that the shop must integrate into its
inspection procedures. The price guides require subscriptions that
the shop or the buyer must pay for. The vehicle-history reports
cost money per report. The inspection tools, lifts, scanners,
gauges, cost capital and require periodic calibration. The
lender's pre-approval has a time limit that affects the buyer's
schedule. The state title rules vary by state and may require the
buyer to engage with multiple jurisdictions if the car has moved
across state lines. The shop's labor rate is the mechanic's hourly
wage plus shop overhead. None of these is free. The buyer's
inspection fee covers the marginal cost; the fixed costs are
amortized across many inspections.

The load is not the car. It is what the inspection has to keep
available to make any reading meaningful. A buyer who tries to do
the inspection without the load, no manuals, no scanner, no
history report, no manufacturer specs, will produce readings that
have no comparison set and no thresholds. The readings will be
numbers without scales.

A laboratory's pH readings depend on buffers, calibration logs,
clean probes, trained hands, and a thermostatted sample. The pH
meter alone is not enough. The buffers must be fresh: pH 4 and pH
10 reference solutions expire and must be replaced on a schedule.
The calibration log must record when the meter was last calibrated
and what readings the calibration produced. The probes must be
clean: ionic contamination from prior samples shifts readings.
The hands must be trained: an operator who places the probe
incorrectly or who fails to wait for stability gets the wrong
number. The sample must be thermostatted: pH varies with
temperature, so the lab runs the measurement at a controlled
temperature. The whole load travels with the reading.

A pH reading reported without the load is unreliable. A buyer of
laboratory services receives the reading and the metadata that
describes the load that produced it. The metadata is the external
surface of the load. Without it, the reading is just a number.
With it, the reading is a measurement.

Load can grow over time. Regulatory changes add required
calibrations. New equipment adds new training. New standards add
new reference materials. The load that supports a class of
measurements expands as the field's understanding matures.

Finite approximation may be enough.

\section{\texttt{FINITE\_ELEPHANT}}
\label{se:finite-elephant}

Enough can be large and still finite.

The class is funny because the elephant is big. It is serious
because the elephant is finite. The class says a finite
approximation can carry enough of the structure for the next move
to be admissible. The full thing is not available. The full thing
might be infinite, or merely too expensive, or merely not yet
reachable. The finite approximation has to do the work.

A polished name like \texttt{BoundedSamplingDomain} would describe
the function without conveying its register. The point of the rude
name is that the approximation is large, funny-looking, and
serviceable. The buyer cannot deny that the approximation is
large; the buyer cannot pretend that the approximation is the full
thing; the buyer can use the approximation to make a decision.

Real decisions cannot wait for complete information. A buyer cannot
inspect every bolt, every gasket, every wire, every weld, every
prior service event, every moment of the car's history. The
complete inspection would take years and cost more than the car.
The buyer accepts a finite inspection that covers enough of the
car to support a purchase decision. The finite inspection is the
elephant.

The finite approximation must be honest about what it covers and
what it does not. A buyer who treats the finite inspection as
complete has confused the elephant with the universe. The
discipline is that the boundary of the approximation be stated.

\texttt{FINITE\_ELEPHANT}

The class carries the obligation that the approximation be
specified, that its boundary be acknowledged, and that the
decision rest on what the approximation supports rather than on
what a complete inspection would have shown.

The approximation must be large enough to support the decision. An
inspection that examined ten percent of the car would not provide
enough coverage for a purchase decision. The discipline includes
the obligation that the approximation's size be defensible.

The approximation must be the right shape. An inspection that
examined every bolt but skipped the engine has covered a lot of
the car without covering the parts that matter for the buyer's
decision.

The approximation must be revisable. As the car ages, as the
buyer's use case changes, as new failure modes emerge, the
approximation that covered enough yesterday may not cover enough
tomorrow.

The rudeness of the name refuses the pretense of completeness. The
class reminds the reader that the inspection is large but bounded.

A buyer cannot inspect every bolt, relay, gasket, weld, wiring
harness, prior repair, owner's habit, and parking choice that has
shaped this car. A two-hour inspection cannot examine the car
exhaustively. The inspection is a finite elephant. It is large; it
costs real time and real money; it produces a real report. It is
still finite.

The inspection covers what experience has shown to be the
high-yield items: title verification, scan data, fluid levels,
tire wear, brake wear, suspension play, frame integrity,
electrical functions, road behavior. The inspection does not
cover every component. The components it does not cover are
allowed to fail later. A buyer who accepts the inspection accepts
that risk.

The shop's discipline is to communicate the approximation's
boundary. The inspection report includes a list of what was
checked and a list of what was not checked. The not-checked list
includes systems that the inspection's tools could not access, the
engine's internals without disassembly; systems that would have
required disproportionate time, every electrical connector; and
systems that the shop has decided to defer to specialists,
transmission-internal diagnostics requiring a dealer's tool. The
buyer can read both lists and decide whether the approximation's
coverage is sufficient.

The finite report can support a purchase inference even though the
exhaustive truth is unavailable. A buyer who buys the car under the
inspection's coverage knows that some failures may emerge later.
The buyer's contingency planning, warranty, emergency repair fund,
planned service schedule, is the acknowledgment that the
inspection covered the elephant but not the universe.

A museum cannot examine every pigment molecule in a painting. A
finite conservation survey can still support a treatment plan. The
survey examines the painting at representative points, uses
non-destructive techniques where possible, and accepts that some
information would require destructive sampling that the museum
will not authorize. The survey is the elephant. The survey is
enough.

The conservation profession has codified the finite-approximation
discipline. The Getty Institute, the International Council of
Museums, and similar bodies publish standards for non-destructive
examination, sampling protocols, and treatment decision-making
under incomplete information. The standards explicitly acknowledge
that complete information is unavailable and that treatment
decisions rest on finite approximations.

A financial audit follows the same shape. A public company's
annual audit cannot examine every transaction. The auditor selects
a sample based on statistical sampling principles and audit
materiality thresholds. The sample is the finite elephant. The
audit's opinion rests on what the sample supports. The audit report
explicitly notes the sampling basis and the inherent limits of
the approach. A reader who treats the audit as covering every
transaction has misunderstood the auditing discipline.

The approximation can be wrong-sized. An inspection that is too
thin misses important findings. An inspection that is too thick
wastes resources without producing proportionate insight. The
discipline does not prescribe a particular size; it demands that
the size be defended and that the boundary be acknowledged.

Once enough is measured, overhead becomes visible.

\section{\texttt{BULLSHIT}}
\label{se:bullshit}

Overhead is real.

Paperwork, ceremony, friction, handwaving, credentialing,
performance, wasted motion, social pressure, repeated explanation,
dressing-up of ordinary actions to make them feel decisive: the
world is full of cost that is not the object but attaches to the
object. The cost is real. It is also not the object. The
discipline is to keep the two separated.

Overhead can be mistaken for evidence. A seller who produces a
glossy brochure, a detailed maintenance binder, a polished verbal
pitch, and a confident handshake has generated overhead. None of
the overhead is mechanical information about the car. All of the
overhead may incline the buyer toward a favorable view. The
discipline is to register the overhead as overhead and refuse to
let it count as inspection.

Not all overhead is fake. Some overhead is necessary. The car's
title transfer requires paperwork that costs time and money; the
paperwork is overhead in the sense that it is not the car, but it
is necessary overhead. The shop's appointment scheduling requires
administrative work that is overhead; the administrative work
makes the inspection possible. The discipline is to distinguish
necessary overhead from overhead that adds no value.

\texttt{BULLSHIT}

The class names the overhead that has attached to the object
without contributing to it. The name does not condemn all
overhead. It condemns the overhead that pretends to be evidence.

A polished label like \texttt{AccumulatedNonEvidentialOverhead}
would describe the function without forcing the reader to feel its
incongruity. The rude name forces the feeling. The feeling keeps
the discipline alive.

The overhead must be identifiable. An overhead that the buyer
cannot point at is overhead the inspection has not separated from
the substance.

The overhead must be assessed for value. Some overhead is worth
the cost; some is not. The discipline is that each overhead item
be evaluated.

The overhead must not be allowed to substitute for substance. The
class refuses the substitution.

The rudeness of the name refuses the polished pretense that
overhead is content. The class will not let the glossy detailing
pass as mechanical inspection.

The seller's never been abused, the dealer's documentation fee,
the glossy detailing that hides nothing about the mechanical
condition, the warranty brochure with terms no one reads, the
urgency script the buyer has heard from three previous sellers,
and the financing theater that delays the negotiation without
changing the car: none of these fix the car. None of them is
evidence. Each is a cost the buyer pays in attention before
reaching the decision. The buyer's job is to register the overhead
without mistaking it for the inspection.

The never-been-abused phrase is a sales claim that has no
evidential support unless the seller can document the car's
history. A buyer who hears the phrase and uses it as evidence has
accepted overhead as substance. A buyer who hears the phrase and
asks for documentation has separated the overhead from the
substance.

The dealer's documentation fee is overhead that the dealer charges
to cover paperwork costs that may or may not be real. The fee is
sometimes negotiable. A buyer who pays the fee without inspection
has accepted overhead without scrutiny. A buyer who refuses the
fee or negotiates it down has separated the overhead from the
substance.

The glossy detailing makes the engine bay look clean. The
appearance does not change the mechanical condition. A buyer who
interprets the clean appearance as evidence of good maintenance
has accepted overhead as substance. A buyer who notes the
detailing but bases the inspection on the actual measurements has
separated the overhead from the substance.

A glossy detailing job is not evidence of mechanical condition,
no matter how clean the engine bay looks. The
\texttt{BULLSHIT} entry in the inspection records the overhead
without crediting it as inspection. The buyer's report includes a
section called dealer presentation notes where the detailing, the
brochure, the warranty language, and the urgency script are noted
as observed overhead. The notes do not move the buyer's decision.
They allow future buyers, and future negotiations, to recognize
the overhead pattern.

A high-pressure pitch about the car's limited availability, paired
with a request for a same-day decision, is overhead that has been
amplified for negotiation purposes. The pitch may be true; the
car may indeed have several interested buyers. The pitch is not
evidence about the car. A buyer who accepts the pitch as evidence
is substituting overhead for substance. The buyer's response is
to acknowledge the pitch as overhead, decline the same-day
pressure, and continue the inspection on the original schedule.
The overhead's existence does not warrant a deviation from the
discipline.

A meeting about the meeting can consume the budget before the
project begins. The meeting may be necessary. It is still not the
project. The cost it imposes has to be tracked separately from
the work it ostensibly supports. An organization that mistakes
meeting-time for project-progress will produce many meetings and
few projects.

The discipline of separating overhead from project work has been
codified in project management methodologies. Earned-value
management distinguishes the budgeted cost of work performed, the
project's substance, from the actual cost of work performed, the
project's substance plus overhead. The distinction allows
managers to see when overhead is consuming disproportionate
resources.

A doctor's visit that consists mostly of waiting room time, intake
paperwork, and billing form completion, with the actual clinical
interaction lasting five minutes, has the same shape. The clinical
interaction is the substance. The rest is overhead. Some of the
overhead is necessary, the intake paperwork captures critical
history. Some of the overhead is inefficient, the same forms could
be filled out online before the visit. A patient who evaluates the
visit by total time spent has used a metric that conflates
substance and overhead. A patient who evaluates the visit by the
value of the clinical interaction has separated the two.

Overhead can become substance. A piece of paperwork that started
as bureaucratic overhead may, through codification and use, become
part of the substance. A certificate that started as a formality
may, through institutional adoption, become a real credential. The
discipline includes the possibility of re-evaluating overhead as
the field's conventions change.

Overhead can reproduce itself as belief.

\section{\texttt{PROPAGANDA}}
\label{se:propaganda}

Some overhead wants to convert the record around it.

Not all overhead sits quietly. Some overhead reproduces. It travels
from seller to listing to forum to neighbor to buyer and back. By
the time the buyer reaches the inspection, the overhead has
already shaped what the buyer expects to see. The shaping does not
require dishonesty by any one participant. It only requires that
a claim repeat itself often enough to start carrying social weight
in place of evidential weight.

Plain overhead sits next to the substance, taking up attention.
Propaganda acts on the substance: it reshapes what the buyer
thinks the substance is. A glossy brochure is overhead. A widely-
repeated phrase like one owner can be propaganda when it has
acquired the social force of a positive evaluation regardless of
what the underlying record supports.

Propaganda is hard to identify in real time. The phrase is
familiar; the speakers are sincere; the social context endorses
the phrase. A buyer who has heard one owner in five previous
listings is more receptive to the phrase than a buyer hearing it
for the first time. The repetition has done the work that evidence
would otherwise have to do.

\texttt{PROPAGANDA}

The class names the process by which a claim begins carrying
social force instead of evidential force. The name is harsh because
the function is harsh. The harshness preserves the distinction
the buyer needs to keep. A polished name like
\texttt{SociallyAmplifiedClaim} would describe the function without
naming the danger. The harsh name keeps the danger in view.

The propaganda must be identifiable. A claim that is doing
propaganda work without being recognized as such is propaganda
doing its most effective work. The discipline is that the
propaganda be marked.

The propaganda must be returned to its evidential basis. A claim
that has acquired social force can sometimes be returned to
evidence by asking what the claim is based on. The seller who says
one owner can be asked for the title history. The claim either
has documentation or does not.

The propaganda may not be reducible to evidence. Some claims that
travel as propaganda are not even technically false; they are
operationally misleading. The phrase highway miles suggests an
easier life for the car than city miles. The phrase is broadly
accurate, highway driving is gentler on brakes and transmissions,
but does not necessarily apply to this specific car. The claim's
evidential basis is statistical, not individual.

The shortness of the name refuses the substitution of social force
for evidential force. The class will not let a claim move the
inspection's findings on the basis of repetition alone.

One owner, highway miles, mechanic owned, rare package, adult
driven, garage kept, never tracked. Each of these phrases can be
true in a specific way and useful for a specific buyer. Each can
also circulate as sales force long after the underlying record has
stopped supporting them. The phrase one owner becomes a brand. The
brand travels. A buyer who hears the phrase often enough may
accept it without inspecting the title, the prior registrations,
or the actual ownership history.

The propaganda function does not require the seller to be lying.
The seller may have heard the phrase from prior sellers in prior
transactions. The seller may believe the phrase as a generalization,
one-owner cars tend to be better maintained. The seller's belief is
not the buyer's evidence. The seller's sincerity does not transfer
authority to the buyer's decision. The buyer's discipline is to
return the phrase to the title history: does the title actually
show one owner, and what were the conditions of that ownership?

The phrase highway miles requires similar examination. The phrase
suggests the car was driven on highways more than on city streets.
The actual evidence would be a service-record pattern, oil changes
timed to interval rather than to mileage, because highway driving
accumulates miles faster than city driving; or an odometer trend,
high mileage in short ownership suggesting long commutes; or a
fuel economy log, highway mileage produces better fuel economy
than city. The phrase without the evidence is propaganda.

The phrase mechanic owned suggests the car was maintained by a
professional with intimate knowledge of its needs. The evidence
would be service records performed by the owner-mechanic, parts
receipts showing investments in preventive maintenance, and
possibly photographs of work in progress. The phrase without the
evidence is propaganda.

A claim becomes propaganda when it has acquired evidential weight
it never earned. The inspection's job is to return such claims to
their actual evidential weight, which is often zero until the
documentation appears. A buyer who declines to credit one owner
until the title is produced has refused the propaganda without
calling the seller dishonest. The seller may be honest; the claim
is still propaganda when separated from its evidential basis. The
refusal preserves the inspection's discipline. If every buyer
made the same refusal consistently, the phrases would lose their
social force and either acquire evidential support or fall out of
circulation.

The refusal is not always graceful. A seller asked to document a
claim may respond defensively. A buyer who anticipates the
defensive response can phrase the request as a normal part of the
inspection: I'll need to see the title history to confirm the
ownership claim, like we do with every car. The framing makes the
discipline visible without making the seller feel accused.

A product package saying doctor recommended may convert authority
into purchase pressure without showing the study. The phrase has
the structure of evidence, a class of authorities have evaluated
the product. The phrase does not show which doctors, how many
doctors, how they were selected, what alternatives they compared
the product to, and what their compensation arrangement with the
manufacturer was. The phrase travels as if it had this support. A
consumer who responds to the phrase is responding to its social
force, not its evidential basis.

The regulatory response to medical advertising has tried to
constrain this propaganda. The Food and Drug Administration
requires that prescription drug advertising include the drug's
risks alongside its benefits, in proportion to the benefits'
prominence. Over-the-counter advertising has fewer constraints,
which is why phrases like doctor recommended and clinically
proven appear on over-the-counter products in ways that would not
be legal for prescription drugs.

A political campaign slogan that has acquired social force in a
community follows the same shape. The slogan may be technically
accurate, broadly true, or simply catchy. Its function is to move
opinion through repetition, not through evidence. A voter who
responds to the slogan is responding to its social force. A voter
who returns the slogan to its evidential basis, what specific
policies does the slogan refer to, what evidence supports those
policies, has applied the discipline.

Propaganda is sometimes the only signal available. In domains
where evidence is hard to access or expensive to verify, the
propaganda may be the buyer's best working approximation. The
discipline does not demand that the buyer refuse propaganda
absolutely; it demands that the propaganda be recognized for what
it is and weighted accordingly.

Reproduced belief needs initiates.

\section{\texttt{ACOLYTE}}
\label{se:acolyte}

A reproduced system recruits carriers.

The acolyte is the person or process initiated into the structure.
The name is not polite. It should not be. It marks the point at
which overhead has become a social machine with members.

The acolyte is not necessarily dishonest. The acolyte may be
sincere. The danger is not motive. The danger is that the acolyte
moves a sentence because the sentence belongs to a system, not
because the record has earned it. The acolyte's sincerity may even
be the propaganda's most effective amplifier: a sincere carrier of
an unearned claim is more persuasive than an obvious salesperson.

Everyone moves through systems they did not personally test. A new
homeowner adopts the conventional wisdom about home maintenance. A
new investor adopts the conventional wisdom about diversification.
A new parent adopts the conventional wisdom about child sleep. The
conventional wisdom may be true. The new initiate has not
personally tested it; the initiate is repeating what the system
carries. The acolyte is not always the same person; in different
domains, every reader is an acolyte for some system.

Acolytes do not see themselves as acolytes. The acolyte experiences
the repeated claim as common sense, established knowledge, or what
everyone knows. The framing makes the claim feel like personal
observation rather than carried social material.

\texttt{ACOLYTE}

The class carries the obligation that the carrier recognize the
carriage and the buyer recognize the carrier. The rude name
reminds the reader that carrying without checking is a specific
kind of move, not just normal social behavior.

The carrier must be identifiable. A sentence that arrived from
somewhere must have a somewhere. The discipline is to ask where a
sentence came from. The sentence may have come from a relative, a
forum, a publication, a salesperson, or a previous experience.

The carrier's authority must be assessed separately from the
carrier's sincerity. A sincere carrier who has not personally
tested the claim is not yet an authority on the claim.

The buyer is sometimes the acolyte. The buyer who has heard certain
models are bulletproof and refuses to inspect them is acting as
an acolyte. The discipline applies to the buyer as well as to the
seller.

The rudeness of the name refuses the polished authority of carried
claims. The class will not let carriage substitute for evidence.

The buyer may start repeating forum slogans. These engines run
forever. Never buy first model year. Dealer service means perfect.
Avoid that color. Buy only manual transmission. The slogans may
contain experience. They may have helped previous buyers. They are
not the same as the inspected record of this car. A buyer who buys
or refuses on the strength of the slogans has skipped the
inspection in favor of the social-machine output.

The forum slogans are interesting because they are produced by
real people with real experience. A poster on a car forum may have
owned the model in question for ten years and may have observed a
specific failure mode. The poster's observation is real. The
slogan that emerges from the observation is condensed. The
condensation loses the specific conditions of the observation. The
slogan never buy first model year may have been earned by the
poster's experience with a specific first-model-year car that had
a specific failure mode. The slogan generalizes the experience
beyond its specific basis. The acolyte who repeats the slogan has
inherited the generalization without the underlying observation.

The slogan dealer service means perfect is propaganda from the
dealer industry. The slogan these engines run forever is sometimes
propaganda from manufacturer enthusiasts. The slogan avoid that
color is propaganda about resale value that may or may not apply
in the buyer's market. The slogan buy only manual transmission is
propaganda from a community that values driving engagement. Each
slogan has a community behind it. The buyer is being recruited
into the community by adopting the slogan.

The buyer's discipline is to test each slogan against this specific
car's record. These engines run forever, but does this specific
engine show evidence of careful maintenance? Never buy first model
year, but does this specific first-model-year car show evidence
of the model-year-specific problems? Avoid that color, but does
this specific color actually depress this car's resale in this
market? The tests turn the slogans into questions the inspection
can answer. The unanswered slogans remain slogans.

A junior analyst repeats a firm's house phrase about a market
because the phrase is rewarded internally, not because the model
has been checked. The phrase may be wise. The phrase has been
carried by the firm for years. The analyst who repeats the phrase
is signaling membership in the firm's culture. The signaling is
operationally rewarded: superiors appreciate the shorthand,
clients hear competence, the analyst rises within the firm's
framework. The phrase's truth is incidental to its function.

The professional discipline of the analyst should include the
question of whether the phrase is true. The discipline is hard to
maintain because the social rewards for repeating the phrase are
immediate and the costs of testing the phrase are delayed. Some
firms have institutionalized the discipline through review
processes: the phrase must be backed by a specific analysis before
it can be quoted in client communications. The institutionalization
is the firm's recognition that the acolyte function, if left
unchecked, will corrode the analytical work.

A medical resident who repeats the senior physician's diagnostic
preferences without testing them follows the same shape. The
resident may be right; the senior physician may have accumulated
wisdom worth carrying. The resident's carriage of the preferences
is acolyte behavior. The medical training system institutionalizes
a partial check: the resident is expected to read the primary
literature, to question diagnostic preferences when they conflict
with the evidence, and to develop independent clinical judgment
over time. The institutionalization is imperfect; residents under
time pressure often default to the acolyte position because
testing each preference is expensive.

Refusing to be an acolyte completely is not practical. Every buyer
relies on inherited information from some sources. The discipline
is not to refuse all carried claims; it is to recognize the
carried claims as carried, to assess their evidential basis when
possible, and to weight them appropriately when full testing is
not.

After propaganda, science must earn its name.

\begin{movement}{From Overhead to Truth Transport}

The procession has now exposed the social machinery that surrounds
a record. Overhead has been named. The visible cost of carrying
scales has been acknowledged. Finite approximation has been
distinguished from pretended completeness. Overhead that adds no
value has been called \texttt{BULLSHIT}. Claims that have acquired
social force without evidential support have been called
\texttt{PROPAGANDA}. The carriers who move propaganda without
checking it have been called \texttt{ACOLYTE}. The movement's
rudeness is its discipline. The polished alternatives would have
let the costs hide.

The next movement walks the moves that science and witness make
to keep the record honest after the overhead has been exposed.
The recovery does not erase the overhead; it works alongside it.

\end{movement}

\section{\texttt{SCIENTIFIC}}
\label{se:scientific}

The word scientific cannot mean our side won.

It cannot mean a person in a lab coat said it. It cannot mean the
story became popular. It cannot mean the consensus held a vote. It
must name a process that lets the record learn while remaining
answerable to what was observed. The word has been used carelessly
enough that its actual obligation has to be restored.

Scientific claims live in social systems. The peer-reviewed
journal, the conference, the funding agency, the press release,
the popular-science article: each is a social institution that
carries the claim. The claim can be amplified, distorted,
simplified, or transformed by each stage of the carriage. The
original finding may survive the carriage, may be partially lost,
or may be replaced by a claim that sounds like the original but
means something different. The discipline is to keep the carriage
faithful to the finding.

Scientific claims are conditional on the evidence available at the
time. New evidence may revise old claims. A claim that travels as
if it were eternal is a claim that has lost its conditionality.

\texttt{SCIENTIFIC}

The class names a process that lets the record learn while
remaining answerable to what was observed. The order in the
procession is not accidental. \texttt{PROPAGANDA} comes first
because propaganda is what science has to refuse without becoming
another kind of louder claim. A scientific finding that travels by
social force has degenerated into propaganda even if its
underlying evidence was sound.

The scientific process must be codified. A process that one
investigator follows and others do not is not yet a process; it is
a habit. The codification allows the process to be reviewed,
taught, and corrected.

The scientific process must produce findings that can be tested by
others. A finding that only the original investigator can reproduce
has not yet entered the public record of scientific knowledge. The
reproducibility constraint extends \texttt{REPEATABLE} to a wider
community.

The scientific process must include a path for revision. A finding
that cannot be revised when new evidence arrives is dogma, not
science.

The shortness of the name refuses the use of scientific as a
status marker. The class will not let a claim travel as scientific
merely because the claimant has the credentials.

The inspection earns its scientific discipline by using measured
tread depth, scan data, torque specs, compression tests, road
behavior, and documented comparables rather than sales talk. Each
entry can be re-examined. Each is tied to an instrument or a
record the buyer can revisit. The inspection's conclusions are not
defended by the inspector's confidence. They are defended by the
ability of any reader to re-run the comparison.

The inspector's training is part of the scientific discipline, but
the training alone does not make the inspection scientific. The
inspection is scientific because it uses procedures that have been
codified, instruments that have been calibrated, thresholds that
have been documented, and findings that have been recorded in a
form a subsequent reader can verify. A trained mechanic who skipped
the procedures would not produce a scientific inspection. An
untrained inspector following the procedures would produce a more
scientific inspection than the trained one who skipped them.

The codified procedures include published manuals, shop standards,
and regulatory requirements. The manuals exist because failures in
prior inspections led to revisions. The shop standards exist
because the shop's reputation depends on consistent results across
mechanics. The regulatory requirements exist because some
inspections, emissions, for example, have legal implications. The
codifications are themselves the result of accumulated learning.
The inspection inherits that learning by following the
codifications.

A seed trial is scientific when plots, controls, weather, soil,
yield, and replication are recorded so the field can answer back.
The field is allowed to disagree with the experimenter. The
disagreement is what makes the trial scientific. A trial that the
field cannot contradict is not a trial. The experimenter's
hypothesis about yield improvement may be wrong; the trial's
structure must make it possible for the wrongness to be detected.

The agricultural research community has codified seed-trial
methodology. Variety trials, replicated yield trials, multi-
location trials, and longitudinal trials each have specific designs
that allow specific kinds of conclusions. The trial's design is
part of its scientific status. A trial designed to produce a
specific result is not yet scientific; it is advocacy. A trial
designed to detect a specific effect if the effect is present and
to detect the absence of the effect if it is not is scientific.

A clinical drug trial's scientific status depends on its
preregistration, its sample size justification, its randomization
procedure, its blinding, its endpoint specification, its analysis
plan, and its reporting transparency. Each is a piece of
infrastructure the trial inherits from the field's codification. A
drug company that funds the trial does not escape the scientific
obligations by paying for the trial; the obligations are part of
what makes the trial scientific.

Scientific findings can be wrong. The class does not guarantee
correctness; it guarantees the discipline that allows wrongness to
be detected. A finding that is later overturned by better evidence
was scientific at the time it was made, provided the process was
followed, and remains a contribution to the scientific record
even as its specific conclusion is revised.

Learning raises the question of truth.

\section{\texttt{TRUTH}}
\label{se:truth}

Truth cannot float free.

Truth in this project is truth carried by a process. The system
does not get to say true without saying how the truth moved. A
sentence claimed as true without a path of carriage is not yet in
the record. The path is the truth's history of having been
transported through observations, witnesses, and instruments.

The class does not capture all that truth could ever mean. The
philosophical literature on truth is large; the class does not
pretend to settle the debates. The class captures what truth must
be inside a public record: a structured sentence with a carrier
history.

Truth is often claimed without carriage. A speaker says it is true
that the car is reliable. The sentence has been claimed. The
carriage history is missing. The reader cannot ask how the speaker
came to believe the sentence, what evidence the belief rests on,
or how the belief would be revised. The unsupported truth claim
is what the class refuses.

Truth claims compose. A sentence built from truth-claimed
components inherits the carriage histories of the components. If
any component's carriage is weak, the composition is at least as
weak.

\texttt{TRUTH}

The class carries the obligation that the sentence's carriage
history be inspectable.

The carriage history must be specifiable. A sentence whose history
is opaque has not satisfied the obligation. The history's specifics
may be brief, but they must be available.

The carriage history must include the witnesses, instruments, and
conditions that produced the sentence. A history that says someone
said it is too thin; the carriage must include who, when, under
what conditions, and with what instrument.

The carriage history must include the limitations. A sentence is
true under the conditions of its production. Conditions that differ
from those conditions require new carriage. The discipline includes
the explicit acknowledgment of the conditions.

The shortness of the name refuses the floating truth claim. The
class will not let a sentence travel as true without its carriage.

The sentence the car is worth buying is not a naked truth. It
carries with it the inspection, the lift, the scan, the drive,
the price, the asking number against the comparable band, the
buyer's use case, the commute, the climate, the planned years of
ownership, the repair risk, the open observations, the residue,
the present entries, and the comparison set, the same model at
the same year. Removing any of those carriers does not make the
sentence simpler. It makes the sentence weaker, in the specific
way that the missing carrier was supposed to bear weight.

The buyer's discipline is to keep the carriage explicit. The
inspection report's final recommendation includes the basis: under
this inspection, this price, this use case, this repair risk, the
buyer may infer worth. A reader of the report sees the carriage. A
reader knows what the recommendation depends on. A reader knows
what conditions, if changed, would change the recommendation.

The sentence's truth is operational. The buyer does not claim to
possess timeless truth about the car. The buyer claims that, under
the specified carriage, the recommendation holds. The next
inspection of the same car, six months later, may produce a
different recommendation. The earlier recommendation was not
false; it was true under its carriage. The later recommendation
will be true under its own carriage. The truth is carried, and the
carriage is recorded.

A sentence extracted from its carriage and quoted as if it were
context-free has lost its conditionality. The buyer's
recommendation worth buying at eleven five, quoted as the buyer
thought the car was worth buying, has lost its conditionality.
The condition was the specific price, the specific inspection, the
specific use case. Without the condition, the quoted claim is
propaganda about the buyer's opinion. The class refuses the
extraction.

The bridge is safe is truthful only through its inspection date,
load rating, material condition, and engineering standard. A
bridge sign that announces safety without those carriers is not
yet a public truth. It is a poster. The bridge may indeed be safe;
the poster does not establish the safety. The engineering report
establishes the safety. The poster is the user-facing summary of
the report. The poster's authority depends on the report's
existence and on the regulatory framework that requires the
report.

The civil engineering profession has codified the bridge-safety
carriage. A bridge inspection report includes the inspector's
credentials, the date, the inspection scope, the findings, with
photographs and measurements, the recommendations, and the
responsible engineer's signature. The report is filed with the
relevant transportation department. The bridge sign that lists
the load rating refers to the report; the rating is the report's
conclusion, not the sign's invention.

A court verdict that the defendant is guilty is true only under
the carriage of the trial: the admitted evidence, the jury's
deliberation, the judge's instructions, the appeals process. The
verdict is not a timeless claim about what the defendant did. The
verdict is the trial's conclusion, under the trial's carriage. A
subsequent inquiry may overturn the verdict; the original verdict
was true under its carriage, and the overturning is true under a
different carriage.

Not all carriage is equal. Some carriages are shorter, single
instrument, single observation. Some are longer, multi-stage
process with multiple witnesses. The class does not require any
particular carriage length; it requires the length to be
specified. A buyer evaluating two claims with different carriage
lengths can weight them accordingly.

Movement of truth requires witness.

\section{\texttt{WITNESSED}}
\label{se:witnessed}

A claim should not merely appear.

A truth that arrives in the record without a transport path may be
correct, but it cannot be inspected for the carriage. The carriage
is how the public verifies that the truth was not invented at the
destination. The class names the structural feature that
distinguishes a truth that traveled through a recordable transport
from a truth that materialized without trace.

Claims can be invented. A document can be drafted from scratch with
all the appearance of a witnessed claim. A signature can be forged.
A timestamp can be falsified. The discipline is to demand a witness
structure that makes invention costly: the witness must be
inspectable, the transport must be reconstructible, and the
constraints must be enforceable.

Witnesses are not always people. A signature is a witness in one
sense, but so is a calibrated instrument's log entry, a notarized
seal, a state's titling record, a digital cryptographic signature,
or a chain-of-custody log. Each is a different kind of witness
with different reliability properties.

\texttt{WITNESSED}

The class says the claim passed through a recordable transport.
It was carried from somewhere, by something, under a constraint.
The somewhere is the origin. The something is the transport
instrument. The constraint is the discipline that the transport
followed.

The witness must be independent of the claimant. A claim witnessed
only by its author has not satisfied the obligation; the author
cannot witness their own claim's transport.

The witness must be capable of refusing. A witness whose
endorsement is automatic has not really witnessed; the endorsement
carries no information. The witness's function depends on the
witness's ability to withhold endorsement.

The witness must be reachable for inspection. A witness who has
disappeared or whose records have been lost no longer carries the
obligation. The class requires that the witness be reachable.

The shortness of the name refuses the unwitnessed appearance. The
class will not let a claim into the record without an inspectable
transport.

The title transfer, the lien release, the inspection report, the
repair invoice, and the odometer statement are each signed, dated,
or otherwise traceable. The signatures may be a notary, a state
DMV stamp, a shop's seal, a digital timestamp, or a witness's
countersignature. The witness is not necessarily a person watching
the event. The witness is the recordable transport: the carriage
of a claim through a public structure that can be checked later.

The title transfer's witness is the state's DMV system. The buyer
cannot transfer the title by writing a new title; the buyer must
submit the prior title with the seller's endorsement to the DMV,
which then issues a new title in the buyer's name. The DMV's
record is the witness. The witness can be queried later. A title
that did not pass through the DMV is not yet a valid title.

The lien release's witness is the state's lien registry combined
with the lender's record. A car that was financed has a lien on
the title; the lien must be released by the lender, with the
release recorded by the state. A buyer who takes possession of a
car with an outstanding lien has not yet acquired clean ownership.
The lien-release witness chain is what makes the ownership
transferable.

The inspection report's witness is the inspector's signature, the
shop's seal, and, often, the inspector's licensed status. The
witness chain extends to the licensing authority that maintains
the inspector's certification. A report from an unlicensed
inspector has weaker witness than a report from a licensed one.

A signature without a date is not witnessed. The transport has no
time stamp. The carriage is incomplete. The inspection report
must reject the entry or hold it pending until the witness is
complete. A buyer who accepts an undated repair invoice has
accepted weaker witness than the procession requires. The buyer's
report should note the dating gap as a specific weakness, not
paper over it. The lender or insurer who later examines the report
should be able to see the gap and weight the invoice accordingly.

A notary's seal does not make a statement true by magic. It
witnesses the identity of the signer and the act of signing under
a public rule. The notary's seal is the carriage discipline. The
statement may still be false. The carriage cannot be faked without
leaving a separate, inspectable forgery. A notary who improperly
seals a document is committing a separate offense that can be
prosecuted. The notary's incentive structure, license at risk,
professional liability, criminal exposure, is what makes the seal
trustworthy.

The notarial profession has codified the carriage. The notary's
journal records each notarization with date, time, parties,
identification verification, and document description. A document
whose notarization cannot be found in the notary's journal is
suspect even if the seal appears authentic. The journal is the
witness's witness, providing a second-order carriage that makes
the first-order witness inspectable.

A chain-of-custody log in a forensic investigation follows the
same shape. A piece of evidence collected at a crime scene is
logged with the collector's identity, the time, the location, and
the recipient. Each subsequent transfer is logged. The chain ends
when the evidence is presented in court. A gap in the chain
weakens the evidence's admissibility. The chain is the witness
structure that makes the evidence transportable from the scene to
the courtroom without losing its evidentiary status. A
chain-of-custody violation can result in the evidence being
excluded, even if the evidence itself is genuine.

Witnesses can collude. The notary and the signer may conspire; the
inspector and the seller may collude; the chain-of-custody officers
may all be corrupt. The class does not eliminate collusion; it
makes collusion detectable by requiring that the witness chain be
inspectable. A reviewer who suspects collusion can examine the
witness records for inconsistencies. The institutional
infrastructure that maintains the records, DMV, state bar, police
internal affairs, is the deeper layer of witness that makes
collusion costly.

Witnessed truth must meet a world.

\section{\texttt{REAL}}
\label{se:real}

The carried truth must answer to contact.

The class names the pressure that the claim meet the world rather
than only the syntax that carried it. A truth that has carriers but
no contact with the world it claims to describe is a closed
circulation without a referent. The class is the demand that the
circulation touch something outside itself.

Truth claims can circulate within institutions without reference to
the world. A documented report can be cited by other documented
reports, which can be cited by analyses, which can be cited by
policy, all without any of the documents ever touching the world
the documents claim to describe. The circulation may be sincere.
The circulation can also be a closed loop.

Contact is informative even when it contradicts the carried truth.
A claim that the car starts reliably can be tested by trying to
start the car. The starting either succeeds or fails. The test is
informative either way. A test that always confirms the claim
regardless of the car's actual condition is not a contact; it is
part of the closed circulation.

\texttt{REAL}

The class carries the obligation that the claim be exposed to a
test the world can fail.

The contact must be specifiable. A claim that the car starts
reliably must be tested by a specific procedure: turn the key
under specified conditions, observe whether the engine starts,
repeat under varied conditions.

The contact must be capable of producing contradiction. A test
that cannot fail does not test.

The contact must actually happen. A claim that could in principle
be tested but has not been is not yet a real claim; it is a claim
awaiting contact.

The shortness of the name refuses the closed circulation. The
class will not let a claim count as real merely because it has been
cited or transcribed.

The car starts cold or it does not. The car tracks straight under
brake or it does not. The transmission slips under load or it
does not. The clutch chatter exists or it does not. The
inspection's carried truths have to meet the car. The car is
allowed to contradict the carriage. When it does, the record
updates.

The cold-start test is a specific contact. The mechanic leaves the
car overnight in the parking lot. The next morning, before the
engine has had a chance to warm, the mechanic starts the engine
and observes. Does the engine start on the first crank? How long
does it take to settle into a stable idle? Are there any noises
that disappear once the engine warms? Each observation is contact
between the inspection's prior carried truths and the car's
actual behavior. The contact can confirm or contradict.

The brake-track test is another specific contact. The mechanic
takes the car onto a straight, level stretch of road, brings the
car to highway speed, and applies the brakes firmly. Does the car
track straight, or does it pull to one side? Does the steering
wheel shake, indicating warped rotors? The contact makes visible
what the static inspection could not. A mechanic who skipped the
brake-track test would have a static inspection with no contact
for the dynamic behavior. The inspection would be witnessed but
not yet real.

A restaurant reservation is real when the table exists, the time
is held, and the diners can be seated. A reservation in the system
that does not produce a table is not yet real, no matter how
confirmed the system reports it. The reservation system can
confirm reservations beyond the restaurant's capacity; the
system's confirmation is a closed circulation that has not yet
contacted the physical seating arrangement.

A diner who arrives at the restaurant tests the reservation. The
host either seats the party or does not. The contact reveals
whether the reservation system's confirmation was real. Most of
the time, the contact confirms the reservation. Occasionally, the
contact reveals that the restaurant has overbooked, that the
table the system assigned is unavailable, or that the reservation
was lost. The exceptions are what make the contact informative.

A published economic forecast that the economy will grow at three
percent next year is a claim. The claim is real when it is
exposed to contact with the next year's actual economic data. The
contact either confirms the forecast, within some tolerance, or
contradicts it. Forecasters who never expose their forecasts to
contact, by issuing forecasts that are too vague to test or by
not following up on prior forecasts, are running a closed
circulation.

Contact can be staged. A car start that happens after the
mechanic warms the engine has not contacted the cold-start claim.
A bridge inspection that tests the bridge under normal load but
not under flood conditions has not contacted the flood-resilience
claim. The discipline includes the obligation that the contact
match the claim being tested. Contact under different conditions
tests a different claim.

Contact happens somewhere.

\section{\texttt{LOCAL}}
\label{se:local}

Every reading has a place.

A ruler sits somewhere. A clock ticks somewhere. A compiler
elaborates in a specific environment. A witness sees from a
position. The class names honest placement. The reading is not a
view from nowhere. The reading is a view from a specific location
with specific context.

Readings are often quoted as if they were placeless. The brake-pad
reading is reported as four millimeters, without saying where on
the pad the measurement was taken, in what shop, with what brand
of caliper, at what ambient temperature. The placement details may
seem irrelevant. Sometimes they are. Sometimes they are exactly
what changes the reading's meaning.

Placelessness is a kind of laundering. A reading that has been
laundered of its location can be applied to contexts where the
original location's specifics matter. The discipline refuses the
laundering.

\texttt{LOCAL}

The class carries the obligation that each reading preserve its
location, time, and context.

The location must be recorded. An inspection performed at one shop
is not interchangeable with an inspection performed at another
shop, even if both followed similar procedures. The shops may have
different equipment, different ambient conditions, different
mechanics, and different conventions. The location's specifics
are part of the inspection's identity.

The time must be recorded. A car's condition changes over time. A
reading taken in the morning may differ from a reading taken in
the afternoon, because the engine is hot, or the suspension has
settled, or the buyer is tired. The time is part of the reading's
identity.

The context must be recorded. A reading taken on a level surface
may differ from one taken on a slope. A reading taken in dry
weather may differ from one taken in rain. The context is part of
the reading's identity.

The shortness of the name refuses the contextless quote. The class
will not let a reading travel without its location.

This car lives in a snow-belt state. It has rust at particular
lift points where road salt has done its work. It will be driven
by this buyer on a thirty-mile suburban commute. An inspection
that ignores the state of registration and the buyer's planned use
is inspecting an abstract car. An inspection that names them is
inspecting this car for this buyer.

The snow-belt context matters. A car with similar mileage that
lived in a southern state would have different rust patterns,
different undercarriage condition, and different brake-line
corrosion. The same numeric findings mean different things in the
two contexts. A four-millimeter brake-pad reading on a southern
car suggests slow wear from mild conditions; the same reading on
a snow-belt car suggests faster wear from salt-accelerated
corrosion. The threshold, three millimeters for replacement, is
the same, but the projected remaining life differs.

The buyer's planned use matters. A thirty-mile suburban commute
will put roughly fifteen thousand miles on the car per year. A
long-distance commuter would put thirty thousand miles. A retiree
might put five thousand. The inspection's projections for
remaining brake life, tire life, and routine service are different
under each use case. A buyer who treats the inspection's
projections as context-free has not yet adapted them to the
specific local use.

The inspection's discipline is to record the locality and to
project from the locality, not from a placeless abstract. The
locality includes the state of registration, the climate zone,
the buyer's planned use, the typical roads, and any other context
that affects the car's projected behavior. A buyer who accepts
the locality and the projections has inherited the inspection's
discipline. A buyer who strips the locality and treats the
readings as universal has violated the discipline.

A soil test from one field corner cannot speak for the whole farm
without a sampling plan. The corner is local. The plan is what
allows the corner to speak about the field. A farmer who tests
one corner and applies the results to the whole farm has assumed
homogeneity the test did not establish. A farmer who tests
multiple corners and aggregates the results under a sampling plan
has respected the locality of each test while producing a
farm-wide conclusion.

The agricultural extension services have codified soil-sampling
procedures. The procedures specify how many samples per acre, how
to space the samples, how to combine samples into composites, and
how to interpret the results. The procedures preserve locality at
the sample level while producing aggregable information at the
farm level.

A clinical trial conducted at one site cannot speak for all
populations without a multi-site replication. The site is local.
The multi-site plan is what allows multiple localities to be
combined into a generalization. A drug that worked at one site may
work differently at another site because the populations,
practices, or environments differ. The discipline of multi-site
trials preserves each site's locality while allowing the field to
draw broader conclusions.

Locality can be over-specified. An inspection that records every
conceivable context detail produces a report no one reads. The
discipline includes the obligation to record the locality details
that actually affect the readings. The judgment about which
details matter is itself part of the inspection's expertise.

Local readings want transport.

\section{\texttt{UNIVERSAL}}
\label{se:universal}

People love to say always too early.

The class is not the erasure of locality. It is the ambition to
transport local readings without losing admissibility. The
universal does not say every place is identical. It says the local
record can be brought under a rule that travels, while preserving
the fact that it came from a place.

Public arguments need to apply across contexts. A buyer's
inspection report should be usable by the buyer's bank, insurer,
and, if the car is later resold, a future buyer. The report's
findings should translate across these contexts. The translation
requires rules that travel; the rules cannot be specific to the
shop's particular conventions, the buyer's particular preferences,
or the local state's particular regulations.

Universal claims can over-reach. A claim that says cars of this
type always fail at one hundred thousand miles is universal in
form but unlikely to be true universally. The discipline includes
the recognition that universals are tested by their failures.

\texttt{UNIVERSAL}

The class carries the obligation that the local record be brought
under rules that travel, while preserving the local record's
specificity.

The universal rule must be derived from many localities. A rule
asserted as universal on the basis of one locality has not done
the work of generalization.

The universal rule must accommodate the local. A rule that travels
by erasing local specifics has not preserved enough information.
The rule's universality must be a transport, not a flattening.

The universal rule must be revisable. As new localities are
encountered, the rule may need adjustment.

The shortness of the name refuses the premature universal. The
class will not let a one-site finding be quoted as universal.

The inspection uses general rules about brake wear, accident-
history scoring, market valuation by model and year, and expected
repair cost across cars in this class. The general rules are
universal: they apply to many cars. The application is local: it
happens to this car, in this state, with this buyer's plans. The
universal rules transport lessons across cars. The local readings
keep the transport honest.

The brake-wear rule is universal: brake pads wear at a rate
depending on driving conditions, braking habits, pad material, and
rotor condition. The rule has been derived from data on millions
of cars. The rule's application to this car requires the local
conditions: this car's driving history, this buyer's habits, this
car's pad material, and this car's rotor condition. The universal
rule provides projection. The local conditions provide accuracy.

The accident-history scoring rule is universal: cars with
documented accidents typically command lower resale prices, have
higher repair frequencies, and may have hidden damage. The rule
has been derived from market data and follow-up inspections. The
rule's application to this car requires the local accident
history: how many accidents, how severe, what repairs were done,
by which shops.

The market-valuation rule is universal: cars depreciate at
predictable rates based on model, year, mileage, condition, and
demand. The rule has been derived from many transactions. The
rule's application requires the local market: the buyer's region,
the season, the specific demand patterns.

A building code transports lessons across buildings. Each
inspection still happens at one address. The code is universal.
The inspection is local. The two need each other. A building
inspector who applies the code without considering the local
conditions, soil type, climate, seismic activity, has imposed the
universal on the local. A building inspector who ignores the code
in favor of local custom has lost the universal.

The construction profession's codification balances the universal
and the local through prescriptive and performance-based
requirements. Prescriptive requirements specify how something must
be built, a certain joist spacing, a certain insulation R-value.
Performance-based requirements specify what something must
achieve, a certain fire-resistance rating, a certain energy-
efficiency target. Performance-based requirements travel better
across localities because they allow local solutions. Prescriptive
requirements are easier to verify but may not fit local
conditions. The codification has evolved to use both, with the
balance varying by jurisdiction.

A scientific generalization follows the same shape. A finding
established at one laboratory becomes scientific knowledge when
other laboratories replicate it. The finding travels by being
independently confirmed. The replication process preserves each
laboratory's locality, its specific equipment, personnel, and
procedures, while establishing that the finding holds across these
localities. A finding that only holds at one laboratory is not yet
a scientific generalization; it may be an artifact of that
laboratory's specifics.

Universal rules can be wrong. The brake-wear rule may underestimate
wear in salt-belt conditions if the underlying data was drawn
primarily from non-salt-belt contexts. The market-valuation rule
may not capture emerging trends until the data catches up. The
universal rule's discipline includes the obligation to revise the
rule when local data accumulates that the universal does not
cover.

Universal comparison requires logic with calibration.

\begin{movement}{From Truth Transport to Decision}

The record now carries truths that have been transported through
witness, checked against contact, kept honestly local, and brought
under universal rules. The procession has produced its most
philosophically loaded movement: not by importing philosophical
machinery but by walking the operational obligations that let
truth function publicly. Scientific process. Carried truth.
Recordable witness. Contact with the world. Honest locality.
Disciplined universality.

The procession is now ready to close. The final movement does not
introduce new substantive obligations; it walks the moves that
produce the public sentence. The final section carries the weight
of the entire procession.

\end{movement}

\section{\texttt{LOGICAL}}
\label{se:logical}

Cleverness is not logic.

A clever argument that does not carry a calibration is not yet a
public decision rule. The calibration is what lets two readers,
with different prior expectations, agree on what the rule says
about a given case. Without calibration, the rule is the speaker's
preference dressed in the speaker's syntax.

Public arguments often deploy logical-looking moves without
underlying calibration. A debater can construct an argument that
has the form of a valid deduction without the premises being
grounded in measurement. The argument may persuade through its
form even though it would fail if the premises were tested. The
discipline is to demand that the premises be calibrated, not just
that the form be valid.

Calibration certificates are easy to forget. A long-running rule
can have been carefully calibrated at its origin and then quoted
for years without re-checking the calibration. The certificate
may have expired in the sense that the underlying conditions have
changed.

\texttt{LOGICAL}

The class names the calibrated structure that lets claims be
compared. In the code, the class carries a calibration certificate.
That carriage matters: logic is not free-floating cleverness. It
carries a scale.

The decision rule must be statable. A rule that cannot be written
down has not been codified.

The calibration must be specifiable. A calibration that has been
forgotten is no longer the rule's calibration.

The rule must apply consistently. A rule applied to one case and
waived for another has not satisfied the discipline.

The shortness of the name refuses uncalibrated cleverness. The
class will not let an argument count as logical merely because it
has logical form.

The decision rule for the car is calibrated. If repair risk exceeds
the offered discount against comparable price, walk away. If the
seller documents the timing-belt service and the prior maintenance,
lower the assigned risk by the documented amount. If the title is
branded salvage, change the comparable set to branded-title sales.
The rule is not the buyer's mood. It is a calibrated comparison
the buyer can apply to a different car next week and recognize as
the same rule.

The calibration is specific. Repair risk is calculated as the sum
of the inspection's open findings, weighted by their estimated
repair cost. The offered discount is the difference between the
asking price and the comparable median. The documented timing-belt
service produces a fixed risk reduction, say, 600 dollars credit
if the service was performed in the last 30,000 miles. The
title-brand check changes the comparable set, branded-title cars
sell for 30 to 50 percent less than clean-title equivalents.
Each piece of the rule has a calibration the buyer can inspect.

The buyer's discipline is to apply the rule consistently across
candidate cars. The buyer does not waive the rule for a car the
buyer happens to like; the buyer does not impose extra requirements
on a car the buyer happens to dislike. The consistency is what
makes the rule logical. A rule applied inconsistently is not a
rule; it is a rationalization.

A chess clock rule is logical because it names when time loss
follows from a measured clock state, not from the players'
persuasion. A player whose flag has fallen has lost on time
regardless of their argument. The calibration is the clock, not
the crowd. The rule applies consistently to all players in the
tournament.

The chess rule's calibration has been codified by the World Chess
Federation. The rules specify when the flag-fall counts, what
happens when both flags have fallen, and what happens if the clock
has malfunctioned. Each contingency is handled by a specific rule
with a specific calibration. The arbiter's role is to apply the
rules, not to invent them in the moment.

A credit-scoring algorithm produces a score that lenders use as a
decision rule. The algorithm's calibration is the historical data
on which it was trained, the variables it weighs, and the cutoffs
it applies. The lender who uses the score is borrowing the
calibration. A lender who disagrees with a specific score can
verify the calibration but cannot easily override it without
breaking the rule's consistency. The credit-scoring industry's
discipline is that the algorithm's calibration is publicly
documented, at least at the level of regulatory disclosure, so
that the rule's application can be reviewed.

Calibrations can be biased. The credit-scoring algorithm's training
data may encode historical discrimination, producing scores that
reinforce the discrimination. The brake-wear calibration may
underestimate wear for vehicles outside the training set. The
discipline cannot eliminate calibration bias; it can make the
calibration inspectable so that the bias can be examined.

An argument that never stops is not yet an answer.

\section{\texttt{HALTED}}
\label{se:halted}

Stopping matters.

The class does not say the process was easy. It does not say the
process was short. It says the process reached a form that can be
treated as output. A process that keeps producing more information
is not yet halted. A process that has been abandoned without
output is not halted either.

Processes can run forever. An inspection can keep finding new
things to inspect. A research project can keep finding new
questions. A negotiation can keep finding new points to negotiate.
At some point the process has to stop producing and start being
usable. The stopping is the obligation.

Stopping is sometimes mistaken for incompleteness. A buyer who
stops the inspection at a sensible point may be accused of cutting
corners. The discipline includes the recognition that stopping at
the right point is part of the discipline, not a violation of it.

\texttt{HALTED}

The class carries the obligation that the process reach a form
that can be treated as output.

The stopping condition must be specifiable. A process that stops
because the inspector got tired has not satisfied the obligation;
a process that stops because the codified stopping condition was
met has.

The output must be a form the next process can use. A stopping
that produces an unintelligible artifact has not yet halted; the
halting must produce a usable artifact.

The stopping must be re-enterable. A process that stops but cannot
be resumed is not halted; it is abandoned. The class is that the
stopping be a checkpoint, not a termination.

The shortness of the name refuses endless production. The class
will not let a process count as halted merely because it has
paused.

The inspection halts when the report is complete. The report is
complete when it carries the pass, fail, or buy-only-below
recommendation against the offered price, with the supporting
observations attached. More research could continue. The seller
could provide more documents tomorrow. A second mechanic could
re-examine the frame next week. The current run has nonetheless
reached an actionable form. The buyer can use the report.

The shop's discipline includes the codified stopping condition.
The inspection ends when the checklist is complete, the findings
are documented, and the recommendation is written. A mechanic who
keeps inspecting after the checklist is complete is not adding
value; the mechanic is delaying the buyer's decision. The shop's
procedures include a sign-off step that marks the inspection as
halted.

The buyer can request a re-inspection later. The re-inspection is
a new process with a new halt. The two halts are different events
even if the inspection's findings happen to be the same.

A bake timer halts the process enough to check the loaf. The loaf
may rest afterward. The cook may put the loaf back in for two more
minutes. The timer creates an output moment: open the oven, look,
decide. The timer does not end the baking permanently; it creates
a checkpoint at which the cook can evaluate.

The professional baker's discipline includes specific halt
conditions. A bread loaf is done when its internal temperature
reaches a specific value, when its color matches a specific
target, and when the tap test produces a specific sound. The
timer is one signal among several. The halt is the moment when
the baker can decide that the loaf is done or needs more time. A
loaf left in the oven without a halt is at risk of burning. A
loaf removed before a halt condition is at risk of being
underdone.

A software build runs through the source code, producing
intermediate representations, optimizing, and emitting machine
code. The build halts when the binary is produced or when an error
is encountered. The halt produces an output, the binary or the
error report. The next process, testing, deployment, operates on
the output. A build that produced neither a binary nor an error
has not halted; it has gotten stuck. The build system's discipline
is to detect stuck states and convert them into errors.

Halting at the wrong point can lose information. A process halted
too early misses information that further work would have
produced. A process halted too late wastes resources. The
discipline does not prescribe the halt point; it requires that the
halt point be chosen with reasons that can be inspected.

A halted answer must cross the instrument.

\section{\texttt{MEASURED}}
\label{se:measured}

An answer must be taken by an instrument.

The class says the halted output has crossed the measurement
process. A halted process whose output never passes through a
calibrated instrument has not yet been measured. The output may
be correct. It is not yet a measurement in the public sense the
record requires.

Halted outputs can be reported without having been measured. A
mechanic can pronounce the brakes fine after a visual inspection,
without having taken a caliper reading. The pronouncement is a
halted output. It is not yet a measurement.

Instruments are not interchangeable. A caliper reading from a
precision shop tool is not the same measurement as a caliper
reading from a hardware-store tool. The instrument's calibration
and the procedure for using it are part of the measurement.

\texttt{MEASURED}

The class carries the obligation that the halted output cross a
named instrument under a named procedure.

The instrument must be calibrated. A reading from an uncalibrated
instrument has not yet been measured.

The procedure must be followed. A reading produced by a
calibrated instrument used incorrectly has not yet been measured.

The measurement must be recorded. A measurement that was performed
but not recorded has not entered the public record.

The shortness of the name refuses the confident pronouncement
without instrument. The class will not let an output count as
measured without the instrument's traceable involvement.

The measured answer is the written inspection report with
readings, photos, trouble codes, repair estimates, comparison
table, and recommendation. The buyer can act because the answer
crossed named instruments: the OBD scanner, the digital caliper
for tread depth, the compression gauge, the diagnostic database
for repair estimates, the comparable-listings service for pricing.
The report is not the seller's word. It is not the buyer's guess.
It is what crossed the instruments.

Each named instrument has a calibration history. The OBD scanner
has firmware that is updated as new vehicle protocols emerge. The
digital caliper is checked monthly against a reference standard.
The compression gauge is verified annually by a metrology service.
The diagnostic database is licensed from a maintained provider.
The comparable-listings service draws on regularly-updated
transaction data. The report's measurements inherit each
instrument's calibration discipline.

A confident seller's assertion the brakes are fine is not
measured, no matter how confident. The seller may know the car
well. The seller's knowledge has not crossed an instrument. The
inspection's measurement of brake-pad thickness, recorded in
millimeters against a known threshold, is a measurement that the
buyer can compare to other cars and to the shop's threshold. The
seller's assertion is information of a different kind. The two
cannot enter the report under the same heading. The seller's
assertion may be recorded in the seller-representations section.
The measurement enters the findings section. The two sections are
weighted differently when the buyer makes the decision. A buyer
who lets the seller's assertion substitute for the measurement
has given up the inspection's discipline.

A piano tuner does not merely believe the note is close. The pitch
crosses an ear, a reference tone, and often an electronic meter.
The tuning is measured because the crossing has happened. The
piano tuner's ear is trained; the ear alone could probably set the
tuning. The reference tone and the meter exist because trained
ears disagree slightly, and the disagreements compound across the
many notes of a piano. The instruments produce a tuning that
multiple tuners would agree on, which is the measurement's public
form.

A clinical thermometer reading entered in a medical chart follows
the same shape. The thermometer is calibrated. The procedure,
sublingual, axillary, tympanic, rectal, is named. The reading is
recorded with the procedure. The next clinician reading the chart
can compare this reading to prior readings produced the same way.
A reading without the procedure cannot be compared; the patient's
temperature is not just a number but a number under a measurement
context.

Instruments can fail. A measurement crossed through a
malfunctioning instrument is not a real measurement, even if the
procedure was followed. The discipline includes the obligation to
verify the instrument's operation. Shops include instrument-check
steps in their procedures; clinical labs run controls alongside
samples.

\section{\texttt{COMPILED}}
\label{se:compiled}

Form must survive machinery.

The class says the structure has survived translation into a form
the next process can execute. A measured record that cannot be
read by the bank, the registrar, the insurer, or the buyer's
spouse has not yet been compiled. Compilation is the change of
form that preserves structure across machines.

Records get stuck in their original medium. An inspection performed
in a shop produces records in the shop's format. The bank uses a
different format. The DMV uses a third. The insurer uses a fourth.
The records have to be translatable across these formats without
losing the underlying structure.

Translation can lose information. A multi-page detailed inspection
report may be summarized into a single-line note in the bank's
system. The summary may discard information the loan officer would
have used. The discipline is that the compilation preserve enough
structure for the next process to act correctly.

\texttt{COMPILED}

The class carries the obligation that the structure survive the
translation.

The compiled form must be readable by the next process. The
discipline is that the compilation match the consumer.

The compiled form must preserve the original's identity. A
compilation that introduces errors has falsified the record. The
discipline includes verification.

The compilation must be reversible in principle. A compilation
that destroys the original record creates a risk: if the
compilation is wrong, there is no way to recover the original.
The discipline is that the original be preserved alongside the
compilation.

The shortness of the name refuses the translation that loses
structure. The class will not let a compilation count as complete
if the next process cannot act on it.

The inspection, the title history, the loan pre-approval terms,
the repair estimate, the market comparisons, and the buyer's
budget compile into a purchase packet. The packet is the form the
next process needs. The bank reads the packet to approve the loan.
The seller reads the packet to accept or counter the offer. The
DMV reads the packet to register the transfer. The packet is not
the inspection; it is the inspection's structure rendered in the
form the next machinery requires.

The packet's structure is dictated by the next processes. The
bank needs the VIN, the year, make, model, mileage, sale price,
and inspection summary. The DMV needs the title number, the
seller's information, the buyer's information, the odometer
reading, and the sale price. The insurer needs the VIN, the
buyer's information, the address where the car will be garaged,
and the inspection summary. The packet is built to serve all three
processes simultaneously, with the underlying inspection records
preserved as appendices that the processes can request if needed.

The compilation discipline includes verification. The buyer checks
that the VIN in the packet matches the VIN on the inspection. The
buyer checks that the mileage in the packet matches the
inspection's mileage reading. A discrepancy suggests a compilation
error that needs to be fixed before the packet is submitted.

A film negative becomes a printed photograph through a compilation
process that preserves the structure of the image while changing
its physical form. The negative cannot be hung on a wall; the
print cannot be re-exposed; both carry the same image. The
compilation preserves the image's content while changing the
medium. A print that does not match the negative's tonal range or
composition has been compiled incorrectly. A photographer's
discipline includes verifying the print against the negative.

The professional photography industry has codified the compilation
discipline. The print's tonal range, contrast, and color balance
must match the negative within specified tolerances. The print's
size and orientation must match the intended use. The print's
processing, chemistry, exposure time, must be documented so that
the compilation can be reproduced.

A manuscript becomes a typeset book through the same shape of
process. The manuscript's text, structure, headings, and emphasis
must be preserved through the typesetting process. The typeset
book is a different physical object from the manuscript. The
compilation preserves the text's identity while changing the
form. A typesetting error, a missing paragraph, a transposed word,
is a compilation failure that the editor's discipline is designed
to catch.

Compilations can be partial. A simplified inspection summary for a
bank loan officer may not include all the inspection's details,
but the simplified summary should preserve the high-level
findings. The partial compilation is acceptable when the next
process's needs are fully met by the partial form. A partial
compilation that omits something the next process needed is a
failure.

Once compiled, the record permits inference.

\section{\texttt{INFERRED}}
\label{se:inferred}

What may now be concluded.

The class is the quiet final name. It does not mean guessed. It
does not mean felt. It does not mean authorized by status. It does
not mean cleared by the loudest claim. It means the record now
permits the conclusion. The procession has earned a public
sentence.

The sentence is short. The procession behind it is long. The
honesty of the short sentence depends entirely on whether the long
procession held. If any of the prior names was satisfied carelessly,
the inferred sentence inherits the carelessness. If every prior
name held, the inference holds.

The pressure that drove the entire procession arrives here in
condensed form. The buyer needs to act. The buyer cannot act on
the inspection's detailed records directly; the buyer cannot stand
in the dealer's office reading every page of the report aloud. The
buyer needs a sentence the buyer can use. The sentence summarizes
the procession. The summarization is honest because each step of
the procession was honored.

\texttt{INFERRED}

The class carries the obligation that the public sentence be
supportable by the procession that preceded it.

The inference must be supported by the record. An inference that
exceeds what the record permits has not been licensed; it has been
claimed.

The inference must be expressible in the form the next action
requires. An inference whose form does not match the action is
not yet actionable. The buyer's inference must be in a form the
buyer can carry into the negotiation.

The inference must acknowledge its conditionality. An inference
that travels as if eternal has overstated its scope.

The inference must be revisable. New evidence may change the
inference.

The shortness of the name refuses the unsupported conclusion. The
class will not let an inference travel without the procession
behind it.

The buyer may infer one of three sentences. Buy at the offered
price. Buy only after a repair credit of a specific amount. Walk
away. The sentence is practical. It is also not merely pragmatic.
It is what the admissible, counted, encoded, residue-aware,
binary, repeated, numeric, representable, physical, comparable,
observed, present-but-open, measurable, awkward-state-carried,
source, executed, valued, magnitude-bearing, scaled, loaded,
finite, overhead-aware, propaganda-refused, acolyte-rejected,
scientific, witnessed, real, local, universal, logical, halted,
measured, and compiled record permits the buyer to say. The
sentence is not naked. It is the procession in compressed form.

The buyer's specific inference, for this car at this price, might
be: buy at eleven five hundred, subject to the timing-belt service
being documented before close. The sentence includes the price, a
number the procession arrived at, the condition, a contingency the
procession carries forward to close, and the implicit agreement to
walk if the condition is not met. Each piece of the sentence is
supported by a piece of the procession. The price comes from the
comparable analysis and the repair-estimate offset. The condition
comes from the seller-must-provide-receipt item flagged earlier.
The walk-if-not-met is the procession's bottom-line discipline.

The sentence is conditional. It applies to this car, at this
price, with this inspection, for this buyer, with this intended
use. A different buyer with a different use case might reach a
different sentence from the same procession. The procession is
shared; the buyer-specific application is not.

The sentence is revisable. If the timing-belt receipt is produced,
the contingency closes and the purchase proceeds. If the seller
refuses to provide the receipt, the buyer walks. If new information
surfaces, a hidden problem the inspection missed, a market shift
that changes the comparable median, the sentence's basis changes.
The procession can be re-run if the inputs change.

The sentence is short. It must be. The buyer cannot carry the
full procession into every conversation. The sentence is the
procession's compressed form. The compression is honest because
the procession holds. A buyer who quoted the same sentence without
the procession behind it would be making the same words mean
something different: an opinion rather than an inference.

Another buyer, with the same use case, looking at the same car,
with access to the same inspection report, should arrive at a
similar sentence. The similarity is what makes the inference
public. If the two buyers reach radically different sentences,
either one of them has departed from the procession or the
procession's inputs differ in ways that matter.

The procession ends here, in the buyer's sentence. The sentence
licenses the buyer's next action. The buyer offers eleven five
with the timing-belt-receipt contingency. The seller accepts,
counters, or refuses. Each response is the next move in the
relationship between buyer and seller. The procession's job was
to license the buyer's offer.

A jury verdict is an inference from admitted evidence under
instructions, not a possession of all truth about what happened.
The verdict is a public sentence the jury may speak. It is
licensed by the procession of admission, observation, testing,
witness, and deliberation that preceded it. It is not a claim that
the jury now owns the truth. It is the conclusion the record
permits.

The jury's verdict, guilty beyond reasonable doubt, is short. The
procession behind it is long. The trial's evidence, the
cross-examinations, the judge's instructions, the deliberation:
all are the procession. The verdict is the compressed form. A
subsequent appeal may revise the verdict; the appeal does not
negate the original verdict's licensing under its procession. The
verdict was honest under its procession.

A peer-reviewed scientific finding follows the same shape. The
published paper claims that a treatment produces a specific effect.
The claim is licensed by the trial's procession: the
preregistration, the randomization, the data collection, the
analysis, the peer review. The claim is short; the procession is
long. A reader of the paper inherits the claim under its
procession. A reader who quotes the claim without the procession
has stripped the inference of its support.

Inferences can be wrong even when the procession was honored. The
inspection may miss a hidden problem; the trial's evidence may
have been false; the study's data may have been flawed. The class
does not guarantee correctness. It guarantees that the inference
and the procession travel together so that, if the inference
turns out to be wrong, the procession can be examined to determine
where the failure occurred. The discipline is corrective, not
infallible.

The procession has closed. The buyer has a sentence.
